‘Trump 2028’: President poses as decorated general and threatens to run again in bizarre series of AI posts
The president has acknowledged he can’t run again but frequently talks about ‘Trump 2028’
- Bookmark
- CommentsGo to comments
President Donald Trump went on a late-night posting spree on Truth Social on Thursday, sharing a Trump 2028 poster as well as images of himself inserted, apparently with the help of AI, alongside famous figures of American history.
One post featured Trump in military regalia alongside Generals George Patton and Douglas MacArthur, who served in World War II.
Others put the president alongside his famous White House predecessors.
In one image, Presidents Trump, Abraham Lincoln and George Washington all appeared to sign a map of the United States.
Another Truth Social post had Trump standing and laughing alongside President John F. Kennedy at Camp David.
Perhaps most strikingly, the president used his post barrage to again float running for a constitutionally barred third term.
The Republican has often mused aloud or joked about a third term, including donning a Trump 2028 hat at the White House Correspondents’ Dinner, though he has also said he’s aware he can’t run.
In the Oval Office on Thursday, President Trump again blurred the lines.
“We'll have a big percentage of the chip business by the time I leave office, which could be a long time,” Trump said. “Who knows? But it could also be fairly quick. They say two-and-a-half years. We're gonna find out."
In the same conversation with reporters, the president spoke about his thoughts on Vice President JD Vance’s potential 2028 campaign, describing the present as “too early” to make an endorsement.
In private, the president has reportedly told Republican donors he prefers Vance in 2028.
The Independent has contacted the White House for comment.
The president’s allies have also offered mixed statements about a Trump 2028 run.
“It’s been a great run, but I think the president knows, and he and I’ve talked about the constraints of the Constitution,” House Speaker Mike Johnson told reporters last year.
Podcaster and former Trump adviser Steve Bannon has said there’s a “plan” that would allow Trump to seek office again.
“He’s going to get a third term. Trump is going to be president in ‘28 and people ought to just get accommodated with that,” Bannon told The Economist in a video interview.
In October, the president admitted he can’t run for a third term, calling it “sad.”
Critics have accused the president of leaning into authoritarian tendencies through a variety of actions, including cracking down on the press, the use of masked immigration agents and the display of large banners of his face put over Washington government banners.

Germans emerge as Europe’s biggest China hawks as economic pain begins to bite
Some 49 per cent of those polled see China as a rival or adversary while just 38 per cent see the nation in positive terms, new survey finds
Published amid a bout of soul-searching in Berlin over whether to back a tougher approach towards China on trade, the findings suggest that the country’s citizens might not be opposed to such a shift.
A combined 49 per cent of German people polled considered China to be primarily a rival or an adversary, way ahead of the second of the 13 countries polled, which was 43 per cent in France – a long-time supporter of a more interventionist EU trade policy.
Just 38 per cent of Germans see China in positive terms, either as a partner or ally. By contrast, 65 per cent of Spaniards and 62 per cent of Bulgarians view Beijing in such glowing terms.
“The China shock has hit Germany particularly hard: its manufacturing sector lost around 420,000 jobs between 2019 and 2025 and it continues to shed 10,000 jobs each month,” read the report by the European Council on Foreign Relations, a think tank.

Next great harbour? China’s Greater Bay Area has tools to be a yacht hub: industry veteran
Hong Kong’s maritime expertise, from finance to legal services, could help turn region’s potential into reality: yachting consultancy CEO
3-MIN READ3-MIN
Frank Chenin Shanghai
From wealth and waterways to manufacturing, the Greater Bay Area possesses the essential ingredients to become an international yacht hub, with the integrated economic and business zone having more to offer than many other regions, according to an industry veteran.
Luxury vessels could help unlock demand for the economy, and with its manifold strengths, Hong Kong could make a splash and turn the bay area’s potential into reality, said Paul Brackley, CEO of yachting consultancy Central Yacht and a founding member of UK’s Professional Yachtsmen’s Association.
The bay area – which links Hong Kong and Macau with nine mainland Chinese cities – could be “the most dynamic emerging yacht market across Asia”, Brackley said. “Berth expansion, new investment and government support are all encouraging.”
Known for riviera-style coastlines, islands, biodiversity and a bulging middle class alongside high-net-worth entrepreneurs, the bay area was among very few regions that also brought design and manufacturing to the table, he said.
The bay area’s size and market – with its gross domestic product on par with the Tokyo metropolitan area – are well recognised, but Brackley said it was only one side of the equation.
