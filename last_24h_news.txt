Joint Iran-Hezbollah strike could paralyze Israel’s energy lifelines, Alma Center warns

A combined Iranian and Hezbollah attack on Haifa Bay, including strikes against civilian shipping near the natural harbor or gas rigs in the Mediterranean, is among several key threats Israel must now prepare for, according to a new assessment published by the Alma Research and Education Center last week.
With a demonstrated willingness to target key maritime assets using ballistic missiles, drones, cruise missiles, anti-ship munitions, explosive boats, and potentially underwater threats and commando capabilities, the center warned that the threat must be taken seriously, even though Israel has proven defensive capabilities against each individual threat.
Much of Israel’s energy and water needs depend on rigs and facilities located along the Mediterranean coast. More than 70% of Israel’s electricity is produced from natural gas extracted from offshore rigs, while more than half of the country’s water supply comes from desalination facilities that purify seawater, making attacks on this infrastructure potentially devastating for the Jewish state.
Hezbollah is still capable of disrupting these key assets, the center reported, even with its diminished capabilities. Hezbollah’s shore-to-missile and its naval unit still pose a significant threat, as was demonstrated with the drone attack on the Karish reservoir in July 2022.
Though Hezbollah’s arsenal is now understood to primarily hold short- and medium-range missiles, the center assessed that it retains limited anti-shipping cruise missiles such as the Yakhont and Ghadir, alongside anti-shipping ballistic missiles such as the Khalij Fars.
Highlighting this, the center assessed based on open-source information that Hezbollah used the Paveh Iranian cruise missile in March, which fell in the south of Beqaa in Lebanon. A thwarted smuggling attempt between Syria and Iraq on July 16 also showed that Hezbollah was attempting to access components of this type of missile.
Hezbollah launched three drones toward the Karish reservoir, which were successfully intercepted. The field had already been a source of tension, as Lebanon claimed part of the natural reservoir fell in Lebanese territory. Then-Hezbollah leader Hassan Nasrallah described attempts to extract gas from the field as a “red line” for Hezbollah.
To face the combined risk the Iranian axis poses, the center noted that Israel has advanced many defensive protocols. The Saar 6 ships have carried out a test with the Barak LRAD interceptor, the long-range version of the Barak MX system, which seems to be directed at strengthening defensive layers against ballistic missile threats on aerial and maritime targets. Additionally, the center noted seaports are already protected by a land-based multi-layered air-defense system, which includes Iron Dome, David’s Sling, and Arrow 2 and 3, with plans to integrate laser cannons to defend against drone threats. Recently, the Navy also began operating unmanned underwater vessels in Israel’s economic waters in order to build a maritime intelligence picture, analyze threats, and protect strategic assets.
Many new layers of Israel's defense remain untested for joint-strike scenarios
Though advanced, the center noted that numerous newly developed and strategized layers of Israel’s defense have yet to be fully tested, and Israel must be prepared for a combined and coordinated attack on several energy and maritime targets simultaneously. If such an attack combined ballistic missiles, attack drones, cruise missiles, shore-to-sea missiles, explosive boats, and suicide drones, in coordination with electronic interference, it must be known whether Israel’s defensive layers would still be able to detect and intercept.
Additionally, the center warned of threats of underwater attacks on gas infrastructure or access routes.
Beyond posing a risk to Israel’s water and energy supply, the center noted that seaports are essential for maritime trade and such trade could be disrupted by an escalation.
In both the 12 Day War in June and in March, Iran targeted Israel’s ports, gas rigs, and oil refineries as clear wartime targets. To mitigate many of these threats, Israel proactively elected to shut down several rigs, including the Karish and Leviathan.
However, the Bazan oil refinery, which has a maximum daily production capacity of approximately 26,600 tons of oil, around 70% of which is distributed on the domestic market, was damaged in attacks on March 30 and March 19, “illustrating that the threat to Israel’s energy hinterland is no longer merely a theoretical scenario but a capability that has been used in practice.”
Though proof that Iran maintains the capabilities and desire to harm energy infrastructure, the center noted that the wars demonstrated Israel was able to maintain partial continuity of its maritime and energy economy. Proactive shutdowns, multi-layered defensive response capabilities at sea and on shore, and the maintenance of port operations, even under direct threat, were positive signs that Israeli infrastructure can cope under less than ideal conditions.
However, the maritime energy infrastructure of Israel is largely dependent on a few critical assets, meaning any successful attack would create pressure on the system. A Calcalist report in March warned, for example, that the loss of even one rig would translate to billions of shekels in damages to Israel.
One destroyed rig might be all Iran needs
While the loss of a rig may be the best-case scenario for Iran, the center warned that it would be enough to achieve a temporary shutdown of production, frightening civilian crews, hardening marine insurance conditions, or creating risk to commercial shipping, which is enough to produce a strategic effect.
“This is exactly the result seen in the two most recent wars: Israel itself chose to shut down facilities so as not to risk a direct hit, indicating that the threat was perceived as sufficiently real to justify a calculated self-inflicted disruption to production continuity,” the center noted.
Over-hardening or preventative shutdowns are part of Iran’s strategy, the center suggested.
For years, the Navy has focused on protecting energy grids and routes, designing Sa’ar 6 ships to defend the exclusive economic zone and deploying advanced aerial and maritime defense systems, including C-Dome and MF-STAR radars, to respond to missile and drone threats against rigs. However, Iran’s attacks have not only targeted these rigs but also complexes located nearby, creating the threat of an attack and the possibility of a costly shutdown with far greater ease than striking an offshore rig.
The attack on the Bazan complex, rather than an offshore rig, demonstrated that coastal processing facilities, refineries, pipelines, and power stations may be more accessible targets while being just as costly to disrupt.
Haifa and Ashdod ports are also key targets for the Islamic regime’s axis, as attacks or a sustained threat on these areas could disrupt the import of raw materials, fuel, military equipment, and civilian containers. These ports remained open during the June war, but imports were restricted to exclude hazardous materials, and additional resources were allocated to address threats in the maritime theater.
While Hezbollah and Iran’s capabilities mainly pose a risk to Haifa, the center noted that the Iran-aligned Houthi terror group in Yemen could also cause trouble for Israel’s southernmost city, Eilat.
The Houthi threat to shipping in the Red Sea and Bab el-Mandeb Strait has impacted shipping patterns, leading to a decline in port activity in Eilat by more than 90% since the Houthis first began their campaign against Israel. Such a threat led to multiple reports over the past few years that the port was facing closure due to financial collapse.
Another key threat Israel must address is in cyberspace. Iran and pro-Iranian actors have claimed operations against energy companies, airports, banks, and infrastructure in the Gulf and in Israel. In some cases, these attacks have seen leaks, while others have attempted to penetrate operational systems.
For Israel’s energy and water infrastructure, this could translate to disruption of cargo-management systems, industrial control, ship coordination, electricity systems, communications, navigation, AIS, and GPS. The center stressed that these threats meant it was essential for maritime infrastructure to be segmented, backed up, hold manual drills, be continuously monitored, and ready to operate under cyberattack.

How are Iran, Russia connected, and why does it matter in the global struggle? - analysis

Ukraine recently carried out a strike on ships in the Caspian Sea, claiming vessels were “used in military cargo shipments involving Iran.” In addition, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky claimed that Russia was provided “new assistance to the Iranian regime.”
He said, “since the beginning of July, we have recorded active Russian satellite surveillance of the Gulf states and US military facilities located there.”
The Ukrainian accusations are important because they reveal more of the connections between Moscow and Tehran. Many of these connections were already known. Russia and Iran, along with China, are key players in seeking to change the world order and weaken the US hegemony that began in the 1990s.
It’s worth recalling some of the context here. Iran was a US partner during the era of the Shah. That ended with the Islamic Revolution in 1979.
However, the revolution did not lead Tehran to rush into the arms of Russia during the Cold War. The leaders of the revolution wanted to pursue a third way between the US and the Soviets. They also had to deal with the fact that the Soviets were in Afghanistan at the time, and there was an Islamic insurgency there against the Russians.
Iran begins developing missile program
The Iran-Iraq war also began in the 1980s, and the Soviets supplied Iraq with arms.
In the 1990s things began to change. The Soviet Union collapsed, and the Russians had left Afghanistan. America’s hegemony and what George H.W Bush called the “New World Order” had begun. Russia and Iran were now relatively weak powers in that new world order.
Beginning around 1992, just after the Gulf War, Russia has played an important role in supporting Iran’s missile and nuclear capabilities over several decades. Moscow helped complete Iran’s Bushehr nuclear power plant and has continued nuclear cooperation through the Russian state nuclear company Rosatom.
This matters because the US had shown what its new military power looked like with the Revolution in Military Affairs. Precision weapons and technology had destroyed the Iraqi army. Iran and Russia had to bide their time.
Times were changing. In 1996, China founded the SCO, which included China, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Russia, and Tajikistan. The economic bloc known as BRICS also began to take shape in the late 1990s and early 2000s. For Iran, this enabled a chance to work with non-Western countries, including Russia and China. This relationship has provided Tehran with access to Russian expertise and technology.
Iran was developing its missile program. Technology appears to have come from North Korea, China, and various sources. Russian entities were accused of transferring missile-related technology to Iran. The result was a relationship in which Russia has helped Iran preserve important strategic capabilities. For Moscow, this made sense. Iran could help balance the US. It was a long road back for Moscow to confront the West.
Russia’s Vladimir Putin was willing to bide his time. This concept was also adopted by China. China’s approach, which it called “hide your strength, bide your time,” often associated with the phrase ‘tao guang yang hui’, emerged under Deng Xiaoping as China rebuilt its economy and international position after the Mao era. Deng emphasized avoiding confrontation while accumulating economic and national power.
China’s leader Jiang Zemin largely maintained this cautious approach during the 1990s, as did Hu Jintao after 2002, despite China’s rapidly expanding economic power and increasing influence. Under Xi Jinping, who took power in 2012, China has increasingly departed from this restraint.
Beijing’s military strength is expanding, and it has adopted what is sometimes called “wolf warrior diplomacy.” China’s more assertive and sometimes confrontational diplomatic style, particularly associated with the Xi Jinping era. Chinese diplomats increasingly publicly challenge criticism of Beijing, including on social media, and aggressively defend China’s policies.
Putin felt Moscow had been humiliated in the 1990s, especially during the Kosovo war. He rebuilt Russian power slowly, first defeating the Chechens and then fighting Georgia in 2008. In 2014 Russia invaded Crimea and eastern Ukraine’s Donbas. In 2015 Moscow intervened in Syria. Russia and Iran worked together in Syria. Iran’s IRGC Quds Force head Qasem Soleimani is credited with having encouraged Putin to intervene. For years, the Russians and Iranians dominated at the Astana talks regarding Syria.
However, things began to change after the invasion of Ukraine. Designed to accelerate the decline of the West and reshape world order, the invasion has not gone as planned for Moscow. Iran helped Moscow by supplying it with Shahed drones. Used in the thousands to terrorize Ukrainians, the Iranian drones have not helped Russia win the war.
Russia-Iran connection key to 2015 nuclear deal
As such, the Iran-Russia connection has not actually enabled either country to achieve major wins. However, each country sees it as in its interest to help the other confront the US and the West. There is also an interplay here. Moscow was involved in supporting the 2015 nuclear deal.
The Russia-Iran connection was key to the deal.
When Barack Obama entered office in 2009, his administration sought a “reset” with Russia while making Iran’s nuclear program a major diplomatic priority. One early decision was to abandon the Bush administration’s planned missile-defense deployments in Poland and the Czech Republic, replacing them with a different, phased missile-defense architecture.
The administration argued the change better addressed the evolving Iranian missile threat, while Moscow welcomed the decision. Obama subsequently sought Russian cooperation in talking with Tehran. Russia participated in the P5+1 negotiations that eventually produced the 2015 nuclear agreement. Moscow was able to be both a strategic competitor and pretend to be a “good cop” diplomatic partner for Washington.
This is the broader context of how we got here. Russia has backed Iran for many years. It wants a multi-polar world order. Other countries such as Pakistan and Turkey also want this multi-polar world. It’s plausible that Russia sees the US and Israeli conflict with Iran as an opportunity. Moscow assumes the world will be distracted by the war and that it will use US resources, including munitions, meaning fewer munitions for Ukraine.
Iran sees the conflict as one that spans thousands of kilometers of frontlines in the Middle East, from Jordan to Lebanon, Iraq, and Yemen. Russia likely sees it as a world struggle. China does as well. What this means is that Moscow would like to use Iran against the US in the same way Russia believes Ukraine was used against Moscow.

EU, World Bank funds saved Palestinian Authority from economic collapse, INSS finds

The Palestinian Authority’s complete economic collapse has been staved off by funds provided by the European Union and the World Bank, with only a fraction of support coming from Arab states, according to research published by the Institute of National Security Studies (INSS) last week.
The PA’s financial crisis has been well documented. Since Israel began withholding tax revenues in July 2018 to offset the PA’s pay-for-slay payments to terrorists and their families, the Fatah-led Palestinian governing body has faced growing financial constraints. The outbreak of war in 2023 further deepened the PA’s economic instability, as tens of thousands of jobs previously held by Palestinians in Israel were quickly lost and economic activity in the West Bank sharply declined.
Israeli politicians have spent years discussing the theoretical collapse of the PA, believing it imminent, though the authority has survived through 2026 with significant cuts to civil services and salaries of civil servants. This survival, INSS highlighted, is largely thanks to foreign aid from Western sources.
Without foreign funding, the PA would have operated under a structural deficit from as early as 2018, when its expenditure began exceeding its total self-generated revenues, a deficit that widened significantly after the Hamas-led October 7 invasion and massacre in 2023.
In 2023, the PA raised only NIS 3.9195 billion in domestic tax collection and NIS 1.591 billion in non-tax revenues, though total expenditure for the year was NIS 14,752 million.
The majority, NIS 10.035.4b., was made up of collections by Israel, while foreign aid provided NIS 755.2m.
Foreign aid offsets decline in PA tax revenues
Foreign aid jumped from NIS 755.2m. to NIS 2.517.8b. in 2024 to NIS 2.742.3b. in 2025. Notably, this came as collections from Israel fell to NIS 6.8568b. in 2024 and NIS 6.1557b. in 2025.
The foreign aid received, which does not include all the funds promised both to the PA and salaries to civil employees, has allowed the PA to avoid a deficit over the past two years.
Taxes and customs duties collected by Israel on the PA’s behalf in accordance with the Paris Protocol of 1994 indicate that while Jerusalem continues to collect these funds, the PA has ultimately received less of these funds. Until 2023, clearance revenue receipts amounted to approximately NIS 11-12b. annually, though Israel deducted funds to cover electricity, water, and other expenses incurred by the PA, resulting in it accessing approximately NIS 8.8b.
However, from 2023 onward, the government began withholding more of the funds to offset pay-for-slay payments and payments to PA staff in the Gaza Strip, leaving the authority with only NIS 7.9b. in 2023, NIS 4.4b. in 2024, and NIS 2b. in 2025.
Additionally, as of 2025, the PA no longer receives clearance revenues.
Before the outbreak of the war, international funding of the PA had steadily declined from NIS 2.4b. in 2018 to around NIS 1b. in 2021.
From 2023 onward, this trend has reversed significantly, with foreign aid jumping to NIS 3b. in 2024 and to approximately NIS 3.2b. in 2025.
In 2024, only NIS 611.22m. was provided by Arab states, with that figure falling to NIS 392.33m. million in 2025.
The European Union’s support surged from approximately NIS 327m. in 2023 to more than NIS 1.5b. in 2025. The World Bank’s support grew from about NIS 283m. to approximately NIS 838m. over the same period.
INSS noted that the surge in Western funds can likely be attributed to the growing perception in Europe that the PA is “an indispensable partner for any future diplomatic settlement – and that its collapse could destabilize the West Bank and undermine broader regional interests.”
As previously exposed by The Jerusalem Post, European funds are often put into Palestinian banks that are suspected of facilitating pay-for-slay payments.
The European Investment Bank (EIB) and the European Commission announced in June plans to inject the Palestinian economy with a €395m. investment. Though the funds are designed to bypass the PA, they will be channeled through financial institutions, such as the Bank of Palestine.
That bank has explicitly refused requests from Israel’s Finance Ministry to close 3,400 accounts reportedly used to distribute payments to released terrorists.
Outside of relying on international actors, INSS also noted that the PA has survived by shifting the burden of its deficit onto other economic actors. Wages for civil employees, pensions for the elderly, and payments to suppliers are delayed, paid in part, or never settled. Palestinian banks have also kept open a line of credit for the government.
In both 2024 and 2025, PA public sector employees received only around 70% of the salaries owed to them, with the unpaid balance recorded as future debt.
While this strategy has enabled the PA to continue functioning in the short term, the accumulating burden will erode its ability to provide public services.
