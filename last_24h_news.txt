How to film and photograph the eclipse without damaging your phone's camera

On 12 August 2026, Spain will witness its first total solar eclipse in over a century. Filming it without proper protection can damage your phone's camera in minutes. Here's how to do it safely.
The Moon will completely cover the solar disc on 12 August along a band across northern and eastern Spain. It will be the first total eclipse visible from the Iberian Peninsula in more than 100 years; the previous one took place in 1912. The shadow cast by the Moon will enter over Galicia and cross the country from west to east, reaching Palma de Mallorca, where the Sun will finally set.
On the mainland, the partial phase will begin at around 19:30 (peninsular time), and the moment of greatest darkness will arrive between 20:27 and 20:35, depending on the exact location. The phenomenon will end shortly before 21:30, with the Sun already very low on the horizon and, in some places, virtually set. Where it does occur, totality will last just under two minutes.
That same night also coincides with the peak of the Perseid meteor shower, so anyone who stays put at their viewing spot will be in for a double treat.
Why your mobile is at genuine risk
The reason you need to be careful is not just to protect your eyes. A smartphones optics work, in a sense, like a magnifying glass: they concentrate the light entering through the lens onto a tiny surface, the camera sensor.
Pointing that lens at the Sun for several minutes without any protection can overheat the camera module and permanently burn the photodiodes that make up the sensor, leaving marks or dead pixels that cannot be recovered. It can also trigger the phones thermal protection and cause it to shut down abruptly.
There are no public statistics on how many phones have been damaged during previous eclipses, but there are known cases of users whose photodiodes were damaged, with photos showing banding or black spots.
There are some nuances: some specialists point out that a quick, isolated shot does not usually damage sensors as small as those in a phone, which are much smaller than in a professional camera, and that the risk increases when you use zoom for a long time or attach an external telephoto lens.
The more minutes the lens spends pointing at the Sun without a filter, the greater the likelihood that the sensor will fail and black spots will appear in your footage because some photodiodes burn out and stop working for good.
How to film it without putting your mobile at risk
The rule repeated in all the guides consulted is simple: as long as any part of the Sun is visible, the camera needs a solar filter, with no exceptions. The only standard endorsed by NASA and astronomy societies is ISO 12312-2:2015, the same one eclipse glasses must comply with.
You do not need to buy an expensive accessory. A low-cost option is to cut out the lens of a certified pair of eclipse glasses and place it in front of your phones camera, covering the entire optics without leaving any gaps where light could leak through.
Those looking for something more robust can opt for high-density neutral solar filters, ND100000, designed for smartphones and available for under 40 euros.
Tips for filming or photographing the eclipse with your mobile without killing it in the process
- Lock exposure and focus on the Sun before you start recording. Each phone has different camera software, but usually it is enough to tap on the Sun and hold your finger there until a small padlock icon appears.
- Activate the cameras manual or professional mode, with a low ISO, between 100 and 200, and a fast shutter speed, below 1/1000, because in automatic mode the image often comes out overexposed even with the filter in place.
- Use a tripod and the self-timer to avoid camera shake or blurred / shaky photos and videos.
- During the partial phase, a time-lapse with intervals of one or two seconds captures the progress of the eclipse better than real-time video, because the Moons movement is very slow.
- If you are within the path of totality, switch to real-time video (without protection) only during the seconds when the disc is completely covered, to record the solar corona.
- Favour optical zoom over digital zoom, which only enlarges the pixels and reduces image quality.
- Keep an eye on your phones temperature. The August heat, combined with the Sun itself, can overheat it and cause an automatic shutdown, so it is best to keep it in the shade between shots and carry a power bank.
What you must never do if you want your camera to survive the eclipse
- Do not leave your phone recording for several minutes on end pointing directly at the Sun, not even with the filter fitted.
- Do not remove the filter at any time except during the exact seconds of totality, and only if you are within the band where the eclipse will be total.
- Do not attach binoculars, telescopes or telephoto lenses to your phone without a certified solar filter.
- Do not look at the Sun through the viewfinder or lens of a camera, telescope or binoculars, even if you are wearing eclipse glasses: the concentrated rays can burn through the filter and seriously damage your sight.
- Do not leave your phone exposed to the Sun for long periods between shots.
It is also worth remembering that watching the eclipse on your phone screen poses no risk to your eyesight (but it can to your phones eyes, its camera) because you are not looking through an optical system that amplifies the light.
The danger lies in the sensor, just as it does in our eyes, not in the screen. And anyone who does not manage to get the perfect shot should not worry too much; there will be specialist photographers covering the event with the right equipment, and for most people it will be enough simply to remember the sky darkening in the middle of the afternoon.

The British producer was best known for his work on Madonna's 'Ray Of Light' and Blur's '13', as well as several charting hits like All Saints' hit song 'Pure Shores'.
Celebrated British musician and prolific record producer William Orbit has died aged 69.
On a statement posted to social media today, William Orbit (born William Wainwright) was announced to have died at home on 23 July.
“We are deeply saddened by his passing,” the statement reads. “He will be greatly missed by us and by so many whose lives he touched through his music, friendship and kindness.”
No cause of death was given.
Orbit worked with a number of musicians including Madonna, Blur, U2, Britney Spears, All Saints, Beth Orton, Robbie Williams and more - with his most acclaimed work being Madonna's 'Ray Of Light' and Blur's '13'.
Born in London on 15 December 1956, Orbit helped reshape the pop scene in the 90s and the early 2000s. His first major hit was working with Bassomatic, with the band scoring a Top 10 hit with 'Fascinating Rhythm'.
The success of that song led him to work with Madonna, whose 1998 album 'Ray Of Light' is considered by many as one of her greatest LPs. It won three Grammys in 1999 and Orbit went on to work with Madonna on the albums ‘Music’ and ‘MDNA’.
Orbit would also transform Blur’s Britpop sound on their 1999 album ’13’ by bringing his electronic sound to the mix. '13' is considered by many fans as Blur's greatest album.
Throughout his career, Orbit also released 12 successful solo albums, most recently 'The Painter' in 2022. His last credited work was with the band All Saints on their 2018 album ‘Testament’ - Orbit had previously worked with the band for their UK Number One hit ‘Pure Shores’.
RIP William Orbit 1956 - 2026

How the Iran war expanded from military targets to critical infrastructure

- Iran, allied groups have carried out at least 172 attacks on non-military infrastructure, according to conflict monitor ACLED
Modern wars are increasingly fought not only against armies but against the infrastructure that keeps societies functioning.
For military planners, attacking critical infrastructure can weaken an opponent's ability to sustain a war. For civilians, it can disrupt electricity, water, transportation, communications and economic activity.
The conflict involving Iran, the US and Israel has expanded beyond conventional military targets to steel mills, petrochemical complexes, transport networks, power plants and digital infrastructure.
The shift reflects a broader trend in modern warfare, where infrastructure that supports a country’s economy and security is increasingly viewed as strategically important.
 
US-Israel attacks shift toward economic infrastructure
The conflict, which began on Feb 28, initially focused mainly on military-related targets.
US and Israeli operations focused on nuclear facilities, missile sites, Revolutionary Guard installations, radar systems and command centers, while Iranian response was towards Israeli military positions and US bases across the region.
By late March, the scope of US-Israeli strikes had expanded to include infrastructure linked to Iran's industrial and energy sectors, including Mobarakeh Steel Company in Isfahan, Khuzestan Steel Company in Ahvaz and petrochemical facilities in Tabriz, Mahshahr, Bandar Imam, Asaluyeh and Shiraz. Strikes also hit railways, highways and bridges.
The broader target list was accompanied by a shift in US rhetoric.
In April, President Donald Trump warned that unless Iran reached an agreement with Washington, US forces would strike "each and every one" of its electricity-generating plants and "bring Iran back to the Stone Age."
Iran expands its list of targets
Iran has said its response was linked to the expansion of US and Israeli operations inside the country, and said that companies providing technological, financial or logistical support should no longer be viewed as purely civilian entities.
Local media published lists of American companies and infrastructure that Tehran said supported US military operations or Israel.
They named technology companies including Microsoft, Google, Oracle, Nvidia and Palantir, defense contractors such as Lockheed Martin and Boeing and multinational companies including Amazon Web Services, ExxonMobil and JPMorgan Chase.
They identified Gulf steel producers as potential targets, while senior military officials warned that refineries, petrochemical complexes, energy infrastructure and desalination plants could also be attacked.
Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said any attack on Iran's infrastructure would trigger "a powerful and decisive response."
According to conflict monitor ACLED, Iran and allied groups carried out at least 172 attacks on non-military infrastructure across Gulf Cooperation Council countries between Feb. 28 and July 30, accounting for 18% of all Iranian land attacks.
Nearly half targeted energy infrastructure, while airports, ports, industrial facilities, financial centers and data centers accounted for much of the remainder.
The UAE and Kuwait recorded the highest number of attacks, followed by Bahrain and Saudi Arabia, while Qatar and Oman experienced comparatively fewer incidents.
According to the ACLED, Gulf states that support US military operations through bases and logistics, such as Kuwait and Bahrain, are likely to remain more exposed in the event of continued attacks.
At the same time, Gulf countries that decide to engage in direct action against Iran are likely to face greater risk during escalation.
Energy, water, data become strategic assets
The widening target lists show how modern conflicts increasingly seek to disrupt the systems that keep economies functioning, both in the conflict region and beyond.
Energy is the clearest example.
Much of the world's spare oil production capacity lies in the Gulf, while Qatar is among the largest exporters of liquefied natural gas. Attacks on production, export facilities and shipping routes have sent shockwaves through global markets.
Earlier this month, semi-official Fars News Agency listed Gulf energy facilities it said were within range of Iranian missiles in the event of another US attack.
They included Saudi Arabia's Ghawar oil field and Abqaiq processing plant, the UAE's Ruwais refinery, Qatar's North Field gas reserve and Ras Laffan LNG export terminal, Kuwait's Burgan oil field and Bahrain's Sitra refinery.
The agency said that attacks on the sites would reverberate far beyond the region. It also cited Iran's strike on Qatar's North Field gas processing infrastructure after its own South Pars gas field came under attack.
Water infrastructure presents another critical vulnerability. Gulf countries depend heavily on desalination, with Dubai, Abu Dhabi, Doha and Kuwait City relying on it for most of their freshwater.
Iran has also reported attacks on its desalination plants, most recently saying a US strike in July left around 10,000 people without water.
Digital infrastructure has emerged as another new battlefield.
During the conflict, reports said US-Israeli strikes hit data centers in Tehran, including one at Sharif University of Technology that Iranian media said supported the national artificial intelligence platform.
Iranian drone strikes also reportedly damaged Amazon Web Services facilities in the UAE and Bahrain in what some analysts described as among the first known physical attacks on commercial cloud infrastructure.
Experts say data centers are both vulnerable to attacks and increasingly central to national economies.
The infrastructure now underpins banking, logistics, telecommunications, government services and AI development, meaning even localized disruptions can ripple across industries.
