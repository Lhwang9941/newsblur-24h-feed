HAVANA, Oct. 2 (Xinhua) -- The Cuban government on Friday expanded foreign companies' access to its wholesale market and allowed their local commercial offices to hire workers directly, outlining new rules aimed at boosting foreign investment and trade.
The rules, published Friday in the Official Gazette of the Republic of Cuba, are part of government reforms intended to increase supplies of materials and other inputs for domestic production.
Under the new provisions, branches of foreign companies established in Cuba can import and export goods and distribute them wholesale within the country.
Deputy Minister of Foreign Trade and Investment Deborah Rivas said the changes would give foreign suppliers more direct access to the Cuban market and reduce the number of intermediaries.
"One of the main effects this measure could have is the elimination of intermediaries in the distribution chain," Rivas told a news conference, adding that fewer intermediaries should lead to lower prices.
Previously, foreign companies supplied goods while Cuban importers handled customs clearance, transportation and distribution, she said. The new rules allow foreign suppliers to handle wholesale distribution themselves.
The hiring changes build on Decree-Law 128, published in September, which amended Foreign Investment Law 118 to allow businesses with foreign capital to choose whether to hire workers directly or through employment agencies.
Rivas said the latest provisions extend that option to foreign commercial representative offices, which operate under separate regulations.
Cuban legal entities can also apply directly for authorization to import and export, without going through intermediaries, using the Foreign Trade Single Window, an online platform accessible nationwide.
A separate Foreign Investment Single Window remains available and has received inquiries from prospective investors in several countries, Rivas said.
Cubans living abroad can also participate in foreign investment projects and contribute capital from inside or outside Cuba on equal terms with other economic actors, she added.
The measures build on initiatives announced by the Ministry of Foreign Trade and Investment since November 2025 to stimulate the sector and support Cuba's economic recovery.
"Cuba is an open door to the world when it comes to foreign investment," Rivas said. ■

watch carsten höller turn art-world encounters into surreal games for a giant polaroid
the world's largest instant camera travels across europe as artists and friends submit themselves to carsten höller’s games.
games as a way of seeing
‘I would prefer not to be in this film,‘ Carsten Höller says. Too late. Over the course of a year and a half, the artist is followed from Venice to London, Arles and the Fondation Beyeler near Basel, joined along the way by Hans Ulrich Obrist, Jürgen Teller, Maja Hoffmann, India Mahdavi, Bas Smets and an unusually cumbersome traveling companion: the Polaroid 20×24, the world’s largest instant camera.
The resulting 20-minute documentary, released by the Polaroid Foundation as The Carsten Höller Episode of The 20×24 Project. The film offers a loose and funny glimpse into the artist’s world through the games collected in his Book of Games. People pull hair, walk, pose and submit themselves to small experiments whose rules are often simple enough to sound almost inconsequential. What Höller is interested in is what happens once somebody agrees to play.
the world’s largest instant camera joins the trip
The camera travels with him through a circuit familiar to those in the art world. Games take place at Palazzo Diedo in Venice, London’s Institute of Contemporary Arts, Rencontres d’Arles and Fondation Beyeler in Riehen. Obrist, Teller, Hoffmann, artist and musician Casey Spooner, Mahdavi and Smets drift in and out. Rather than conventional interviews, the games become a way of approaching them. People have something to do, a rule to follow or another person to respond to, and the camera watches what changes.
At the center of all this is the enormous Polaroid 20×24. Its photographs measure roughly 20 by 24 inches, turning the familiar immediacy of an instant photograph into something closer to an event. The project leaves behind a growing collection of portraits alongside the moving image footage, and fixes individual moments from Höller’s experiments at a scale that feels almost absurd for something normally associated with a photograph developing in your hand.
inside höller’s world of birds, food and experiments
The journey eventually becomes more personal. In Stockholm, Carsten Höller brings the crew home, where around seventy birds live in his aviary, before heading to the roof of Moderna Museet and to Brutalisten, the concept restaurant he founded around food prepared from a single principal ingredient. Elsewhere, the group heads into the Camargue, France, on what the film describes as a ‘sad bird’-watching expedition.
Games have occupied Höller for decades, often as a way of disturbing familiar patterns of behavior and perception. Book of Games, published in 2024, gathers those experiments into instructions that anyone can follow. Some are social, some psychological and some intentionally ridiculous. Höller speaks in the film about removing the middle ground between opposing states, especially joy and fear, to leave players somewhere closer to either end.
what happens when people agree to play
There isn’t much separation between portrait, interview, experiment and joke. A photograph becomes evidence that something happened, while the game creates the conditions for it to happen in the first place. And despite Höller’s stated reluctance to appear, the accumulating footage gradually becomes a portrait of him too: his friends, his birds, his recurring questions and the elaborate situations he keeps inventing to see what people might do next.

How do I know my child is ready to leave the house on their own?
By the time children are around 12 years old, many will begin to ask if they can go out to the local park, playground or shops to spend time with their friends without adult supervision.
This may happen more often during the school holiday period. They might say:
All of my friends are going. Can I hang out with them?
When a child first asks this question, it can be difficult for parents to judge whether their child is ready. Parents might want experts to give them a specific age recommendation, but children develop at their own pace and readiness varies.
This means there is no single right age when children are ready to spend time with friends, out of the house, unsupervised.
As the parent of three children, I've faced this situation myself.
So how do you know when it's okay to let your child go out on their own?
The importance of independence
Children need opportunities for independent activities to build wellbeing.
The freedom to go out with friends without adult supervision gives children the chance to explore, make choices and navigate unexpected challenges. It allows them to develop their autonomy, confidence and resilience.
It can also help kids get more active. Adventurous outdoor activities with friends, such as bike riding, allow children to learn how to cope with potentially risky situations and uncertainty, which may reduce their chances of developing anxiety.
Research on children whose parents allowed them to travel and play in their neighbourhoods also found it may help them cultivate friendships and create a sense of community.
What are the main concerns?
While many parents and carers acknowledge the benefits of letting their child have more independence, they may still feel nervous about the potential risks that come with it.
A 2019 New Zealand survey asked more than 2,000 parents about their attitudes towards risky play and children being out on their own, and the barriers that prevented them from allowing their children to be more independent.
The two biggest concerns were road safety (increased risks of traffic accidents) and "stranger danger" (children potentially having encounters with harmful adults).
So, with these concerns in mind, what are some things you can do to see if your child is ready?
Have a conversation
If your child asks to be out alone, you might start by asking them:
What makes you feel ready to be out on your own?
Your child's answer may give you a clearer idea of whether they're ready or not.
I've stayed at home alone before. I'm responsible. I think you should trust me enough to be able to do this on my own.
Evaluate their readiness
There are a number of things to consider when deciding if your child is capable.
Loading...
First, is your child aware of their surroundings? Do they notice potential hazards? For instance, would they notice if a storm was approaching and they needed to seek shelter?
Second, does your child have good judgement? Would they think clearly if they were faced with an unexpected problem, such as getting a flat bike tyre?
Third, is your child reliable? Do they follow instructions? Can they be trusted to return home at an agreed-upon time?
Agree on some rules
If you think your child is ready, it's important to set ground rules for when they're out. This might include your child agreeing to stay within a particular area, being home by a certain time, and communicating if they are going to be late. You might also ask them to stay with their friends the entire time they're out.
Start closer to home
For many families, it helps to begin with an activity closer to home, such as walking to the park and back. As trust and confidence build for both of you, your child can then be allowed out longer and further away.
It's also good to have a plan with your child about what to do if something goes wrong, they want your help or they need to contact you.
While it can feel daunting initially, independent time with friends without parent supervision helps your child to grow, develop and thrive.

In brief
- NSW ICAC’s Operation Rosny examined alleged links between Liberal Party figures and developer Jean Nassif over donations and influence.
- The high-profile inquiry exposed damaging allegations ahead of the state election and renewed scrutiny of party fundraising.
Phone calls packed with colourful language, meetings at Nobu, Fijian hideouts, all-day drinks at London pubs, promises of millions of dollars.
It was enough to fill out 38 sitting days as the NSW Independent Commission Against Corruption (ICAC) sliced and diced a Liberal Party corruption scandal.
At the heart of the probe, the ICAC examined alleged collusion between fugitive property developer Jean Nassif and party powerbrokers including two brothers of former NSW premier Dominic Perrottet.
Jean-Claude Perrottet, Charles Perrottet and his wife Anita Perrottet endured days of grilling in the witness box.
In an inquiry dubbed Operation Rosny, the watchdog investigated alleged kickbacks involving Nassif and his company, Toplace, between 2020 and 2023.

Political donations from developers have been banned in NSW since 2009, yet the ICAC's widening net led to mentions of other powerful Liberals, including federal Opposition Leader Angus Taylor.
Former ICAC assistant commissioner and retired Supreme Court judge Anthony Whealy rates Rosny one of the most impactful investigations in the body's almost 40-year history.
"You can already rank it as a very consequential inquiry," he told the Australian Associated Press (AAP) news agency.
"That is because it's taking quite a broad swipe into the machinations of party politics, the relationships between developers and politicians and where those intersections can go seriously wrong."
The inquiry began with a bang in July when counsel assisting Peggy Dwyer launched her opening statement with the adage, "Money in politics, as in other spheres of life, has the potential to corrupt."
It ushered in weeks of forensic accounting and questions over embarrassing text exchanges about surveillance of mayors, sham contracts and fake arrangements spanning Beirut, London and Cessnock.
Twenty witnesses were called, including the bombastic Nassif, who dialled in virtually from his bedroom in Beirut, wrapping up on Tuesday.
Their memories — from a demure Jean-Claude Perrottet and emotional senior Liberal MP Damien Tudehope to brash lobbyist Jeremy Greenwood and a befuddled Anita Perrottet — were put to the test on the stand.
In their responses, revelations were unearthed, and admissions recorded that have damaged the Liberal Party as it heads to a state election in March.
"In terms of the Academy Awards, some would be given out for splendid attempts to shy away from the truth without very much success," Whealy says.
"Overall, the spectacle we saw was rather amusing, but then it wasn't amusing at all; it was really quite serious."
He says a number of witnesses have tarnished their reputations by not being open and honest with the commission.
Central to the probe was how the Reformers, a conservative Christian sub-faction within the Liberal Party, allegedly circumvented donation caps by setting up schemes to hide the money trail.
Taylor, who is not under investigation by the corruption watchdog, has denied making pledges to the ultra-conservative faction.
"I want to shake down Angus for 10K," the now sacked head of Catholic Schools NSW, Dallas McInerney, wrote in a message aired at the inquiry on Tuesday.
The opposition leader said "no payment was made" when questioned about a $15,000 "injection" and monthly $3000 donations to the Reformers.
Former NSW premier Nick Greiner doesn't want to wade into how Operation Rosny will affect the party whose federal executive he served until March this year.
But he credits the creation of ICAC in 1989, during his tenure as premier, as one of his most important contributions to keeping politics accountable.
"ICAC is now close to 40 years old, so in a broad sense it's been successful — and the fact that it's been copied everywhere is a fair indication of that," he says.
Its public airing of dirty laundry, which played a role in Greiner's own political demise, later brought down popular Liberal premier Gladys Berejiklian and Labor heavyweight Eddie Obeid.
Yet it has not been immune from criticism.
Greiner says the commission's sometimes spectacular binge-watching sessions were not all necessary.
"There's room for more common sense and discretion," he insists.
But he reserves sterner ire for how long it often takes the ICAC to establish a probe and make corruption findings, and then whether it recommends prosecutions.
The watchdog aims to have a report tabled in parliament within 180 days of receiving final submissions but in practice it takes much longer.
"Clearly the time frames are absolutely absurd," Greiner says.
"It cannot take years and years when you've got all the powers that ICAC has ... My general impression is it's way too slow."
Rosny was sparked when state Liberal MP Ray Williams used parliamentary privilege in 2022 to claim powerbrokers in his party were engaged in branch-stacking and improper local council dealings.
Whealy says the watchdog's invaluable oversight role should reassure the public that no matter how corrupt political parties might appear, there is always a comeuppance.
"There are some rotten apples around and (the ICAC) reinforces that notion but it doesn't destroy trust altogether in the political system," he says.
"It's a comfort to the community ... that there's a vigilant oversight body doing its job properly."

Why Carney’s economic overhaul is clashing with Canada’s unions
Unions say proposed changes to Canada’s labour laws could weaken workers’ right to strike.
Toronto, Canada: As Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney races to make the country less dependent on the United States, attract billions in new investment and get major projects built faster, his economic overhaul is running into resistance from a group that says it wants many of the same things: organised labour.
The latest clash, over the right to strike, comes after months of mounting pressure on Ottawa from Washington, DC.
US President Donald Trump, since taking up a second term in office last year, has imposed steep tariffs on Canadian goods and weakened trade agreements – perilous moves as Canada has historically sent close to 80 percent of its exports to its southern neighbour. He has repeatedly piled on with the tariff threats to make Canada the 51st US state.
Canada has responded with retaliatory tariffs, while a broader movement to “Buy Canadian” has taken hold. The hockey-inspired “elbows up” — meaning a readiness to defend against an opponent — has become a national rallying cry.
With trade talks stalled since August, Carney has moved with urgency to shore up the economy and invite new investments. Last month, he announced Bill C-39, known as the Building Canada Strong Act, promising to bring “speed, certainty and predictability” to investors.
Along with measures to speed up approvals for major projects, the bill would also give Ottawa clearer powers to intervene in legal strikes and lockouts in federally regulated workplaces — a provision that has put Carney on a collision course with unions.
Canada’s largest unions have come together against the changes. The largest, the Canadian Union of Public Employees (CUPE), had its national executive go as far as voting to defy the bill’s proposed limits on the right to strike if it passes Parliament without amendments.
CUPE National President Mark Hancock credited Carney’s handling of Trump and said the union’s 800,000 members, who work everywhere from hospitals to schools to municipal services, want to be part of “Team Canada”.
“But at what cost?” he said in an interview with Al Jazeera.
A fight over the right to strike
The controversy centres on Section 107 of the Canada Labour Code, which already gives the Labour Minister broad powers to step into disputes if needed to maintain “industrial peace”, end work stoppages and force the two sides into a binding arbitration.
Beginning in 2024, Ottawa used the provision eight times to intervene in disputes involving airlines, Canada’s two largest railways, three major ports and Canada Post and again during the Air Canada dispute in 2025 when flight attendants went on strike.
Unions have challenged the use of that law in court and had hoped the proposed C-39 would restrict, if not repeal, that clause while businesses wanted it strengthened.
The bill does neither. Instead, it introduces two conditions that the government would have to fulfil first before turning to Section 107.
Before stepping in, the government would have to appoint a special mediator, wait for a public report and consider whether a work stoppage is hurting the “national interest”.
Once a strike or lockout is underway, the minister could direct the Canada Industrial Relations Board to get operations running again and impose a binding process, such as arbitration.
The government says those steps put clearer guardrails around a power that already exists, and Carney has said the bill “absolutely reinforces the right to strike”, according to the Canadian Broadcasting Corporation.
Unions have rejected this defence, pointing out that the meaning of “national interest” would ultimately be decided by the minister. They also argue that the prospect of government intervention is bound to change what happens at the bargaining table.
“The political lesson that employers will draw from it is simple,” Larry Savage, a professor of labour studies at Brock University, told Al Jazeera. “Hold out long enough, emphasise the economic damage, and then Ottawa will remove the union’s leverage for you.”
Disruption, Savage said, is precisely what gives a strike its power. “Every effective strike is disruptive,” he said.
Unions say they have already seen employers expect Ottawa to intervene.
Teamsters Canada alleges the Canadian National Railway (CN) and the Canadian Pacific Kansas City (CPKC) railroad companies were counting on Ottawa to step in when they locked out thousands of workers in 2024. Section 107 was invoked within hours.
“Companies have gotten used to the idea that if there is a labour dispute, they can just wait for the government to intervene,” Christopher Monette, Teamsters Canada’s director of public affairs, told Al Jazeera.
CUPE said it saw a similar dynamic at Air Canada in August 2025. Hancock said bargaining stalled after more than 10,000 Air Canada flight attendants voted overwhelmingly to strike because the airline was “expecting the government of Canada to step in and end the strike” — an account Air Canada disputed.
Less than 12 hours after workers walked out, Ottawa invoked Section 107. Air Canada CEO Michael Rousseau later told BNN Bloomberg the airline had expected the provision to be enforced and did not expect a strike.
“That was why they didn’t have a strategy on how to deal with the strike,” Hancock said.
Building Canada faster
The fight over the right to strike is also part of a much bigger argument taking shape around Carney’s economic agenda: Who gets a say in how Canada should change, and how quickly, in the name of making itself less vulnerable to the US?
Critics accuse Carney of using the economic threat from the US to push through changes that go well beyond responding to Trump’s trade war.
New Democratic Party leader Avi Lewis has accused him of using the “fear and disorientation around the trade war to push through a series of unpopular measures that he did not run on, has no mandate for and would never get away with under normal circumstances”.
That tension was on display in Toronto just a week before C-39 was introduced. Inside Canada’s first national investment summit, hundreds of global investors met with government officials and executives as Carney pitched a country ready to build.
Outside, hundreds of protesters marched through downtown towards the summit’s opening gala under the banner “The Many vs The Money”. The rally brought together labour, Indigenous, environmental and migrant-rights groups, with signs and speeches taking aim at everything from fossil fuel and military projects to AI and the use of public money to attract private investment.
For unions, C-39 has sharpened that debate, pitting the government’s promise of greater certainty for investors against workers’ ability to exert pressure through strikes.
But Jim Stanford, an economist and director of the Vancouver-based Centre for Future Work, questions how much of an economic threat strikes actually pose.
While work stoppages can be costly for individual companies, Stanford said, “It is very, very rare that you would see a noticeable and sustained impact on GDP, employment or incomes.”
Production and transportation are often delayed rather than permanently lost, he added, with businesses catching up once work resumes.
Stanford also questioned the idea that strikes are driving away investment, noting that more than 95 percent of collective bargaining ends without a work stoppage and saying there is “no empirical evidence whatsoever” that strikes have reduced investment.
“This is more of the government giving some icing on the cake for business. It’s not that this has to happen or else our investment won’t work,” he said.
There is also an economic cost to weakening the right to strike, Stanford argued. Workers need bargaining power to win higher wages, which can support consumer spending, productivity and worker retention.
“It may seem like labour peace is a good thing, but if it means that a worker’s share of the pie shrinks, then this actually hurts the economy.”
For labour leaders, that’s where Carney’s economic push goes too far.
“Canada’s unions are part of Team Canada. We have our elbows up,” Canadian Labour Congress President Bea Bruske said in a statement. “But we can’t have our elbows up against Trump with our hands tied at the bargaining table.”
Savage said the government’s “Team Canada” rhetoric risks treating workers’ bargaining power as a national liability.
“I don’t think Canada becomes stronger by telling workers in ports or railways or airlines that their rights have to be surrendered whenever employers invoke competitiveness or the national interest,” he said.

Ukraine has lost another Mikoyan MiG-29 fighter and its pilot. The Thursday shoot-down extends the steady attrition of the Ukrainian air force MiG fleet, potentially drawing down the fleet to its pre-war strength.
That means Ukraine may have lost as many of the twin-engine, supersonic MiG-29s as it has received from its allies or restored from surplus stocks since February 2022.
It's an ominous milestone. The Soviet-vintage MiG-29 is no longer the Ukrainian air force's most important fighter. That honorific belongs to the air force's ex-European Lockheed Martin F-16s.
But the MiG-29 is still the air force's second most important fighter—and will remain so through 2027 as Ukraine awaits its first ex-Swedish Saab Gripens, due early next year, and French-made Dassault Rafales, due from 2028. Ukraine sorties MiG-29s for ground attack missions targeting Russian troop concentrations, strikes on Russian air defense systems, and air defense sorties over Ukrainian cities and ports.
The air force announced the loss of the MiG-29 and its pilot, Capt. Marat Mambetov, shortly after it occurred on Thursday. "Flying a MiG-29 fighter jet, [Mambetov] carried out missile and bomb strikes against Russian occupiers along one of the front-line directions," the air force stated. It's the 37th confirmed combat loss of a Ukrainian MiG-29 since Russia widened its war on Ukraine in February 2022, according to a tally by the Oryx open-source intelligence collective. Oryx has also registered damage to an additional three Ukrainian MiG-29s.
Thirty-seven write-offs, plus the likely grounding of several previously flyable MiG-29s owing to intensive wear and tear since 2022, may have reduced the Ukrainian air force's MiG-29 fleet to around three dozen active jets.
Fighter subtraction
The math is simple. The air force probably had between 30 and 35 MiG-29s in service when Russia attacked in February 2022. As losses cut into the fleet, Poland donated 14 surplus MiGs, Slovakia donated 10, and Azerbaijan reportedly donated three. Meanwhile, Ukrainian engineers tapped a global pipeline of spare parts to reactivate an estimated 20 grounded MiG-29s moldering in hangars at various Ukrainian airfields.
That's as many as 82 MiGs in and, as of Thursday, 37 out owing to Russian action—plus additional jets grounded due to airframe fatigue. The resulting active fleet should number around three dozen planes, around as many as Ukraine had on the eve of the wider war.
Trending Now
There aren't many more old MiGs that Ukraine could obtain to bolster the fleet. Poland still has as many as nine very tired airframes, but Kyiv and Warsaw have consistently failed to negotiate a transfer. There may yet be a few ex-Soviet MiG-29s, grounded for years or even decades, that Ukrainian engineers can restore to flightworthy status. Other than that, options are scarce.
Ukraine’s Swedish radar planes have flown inside Russian missile range for six months. None has been shot down.
The three dozen MiGs still in the inventory may have to last a little while alongside the few survivors of Ukraine's other Soviet-vintage warplanes, including Sukhoi Su-27s, Sukhoi Su-24s, and Sukhoi Su-25s.
Older Western types—the 80 or so F-16s European allies have pledged, about half delivered, as well as potentially a dozen or so ex-French Dassault Mirage 2000s—are steadily replacing the ex-Soviet jets. (So far, Ukraine has lost five F-16s and one Mirage.) Next year, the transformation of the Ukrainian air force from a largely Soviet-equipped force to a largely Western-equipped force will accelerate when the first of 32 Gripens arrive. Sixteen Rafales are to follow from 2028.
Barring catastrophic combat losses among Western types and assuming Ukraine can maintain and arm these new warplanes, by the end of 2027, the air force may not need its remaining MiGs and Sukhois, which by then could be very tired.

Curious people tend to have more of the experiences linked to cognitive reserve, study finds
Unknown
Unknown Publisher
2026-10-02
22:00:02 UTC
07:00:02 KST

A small study in the Netherlands found that people with a more curious personality tended to score higher on indicators of cognitive reserve, such as education and mentally stimulating leisure activities. However, neither curiosity nor these indicators of cognitive reserve were associated with performance on cognitive tests. The paper was published in Personality and Individual Differences.
As people age, many experience gradual cognitive decline. However, not everyone experiences this decline at the same pace or at the same age. While some individuals show marked cognitive decline relatively early, others maintain normal cognitive abilities into very advanced age. To help explain these differences, scientists developed the concept of cognitive reserve.
Cognitive reserve is the brain’s ability to maintain cognitive functioning despite aging, brain injury, or disease-related changes. It is thought to develop gradually across life through experiences that repeatedly engage the brain, such as education, intellectually demanding work, reading, learning new skills, and other mentally stimulating activities. In later life, greater cognitive reserve may help delay or reduce the cognitive effects of brain atrophy and other age-related changes, even though it does not necessarily prevent those physical changes from occurring.
One factor that may be of particular importance for building cognitive reserve is curiosity. Curious people are more likely to seek new information, explore unfamiliar experiences, and continue learning throughout adulthood. Activities encouraged by curiosity, such as reading books, learning languages, or exploring new places and ideas, may provide repeated cognitive stimulation over many years. Research has already linked the personality trait of openness to experience (a trait that also predisposes people to curious behaviors) to slower cognitive decline, suggesting that it may support resilience against it.
Study author Carolien Torenvliet and colleagues at Radboud University’s Donders Institute for Brain, Cognition and Behaviour explored the association between curiosity and various indicators of cognitive reserve. They also examined whether a curious personality (i.e., openness to experience and interest-based curiosity) was associated with cognitive abilities, and whether cognitive reserve might explain any such link.
Study participants were 106 adults recruited mainly from around Nijmegen in the Netherlands. They were between 40 and 85 years of age, with an average age of about 62, and 57% were women. People with current or past psychiatric or neurological disorders were excluded. Participants’ average estimated IQ was 118, well above the population average of 100.
Participants completed three questionnaires assessing curious personality: two measuring interest-based curiosity and one measuring openness to experience. They also completed the Dutch version of the Cognitive Reserve Index questionnaire, which estimates cognitive reserve from a person’s education, the complexity of their work, and their leisure activities. Finally, they completed three brief neuropsychological tests measuring memory, executive function, and processing speed.
Results showed that more curious individuals tended to score higher on indicators of cognitive reserve, specifically education and leisure activities, but not the complexity of their work. This association held after accounting for participants’ age. However, neither curiosity nor the cognitive reserve indicators were associated with performance on the cognitive tests.
“Although this study is limited by its cross-sectional design and relatively healthy sample, the results suggest that a curious personality may drive individual differences in cognitively stimulating experiences, but not cognitive abilities themselves,” the study authors concluded.
The study contributes to the scientific understanding of cognitive reserve. However, it should be noted that the design of the study does not allow any causal inferences to be drawn from the results. Curiosity may lead people to pursue more education and stimulating activities, but those experiences could also foster curiosity.
In addition, cognitive reserve was estimated from participants’ own reports of their past education, work, and activities rather than measured directly. Finally, participants were healthy and had higher-than-average estimated intelligence, and the sample was relatively small for a study of this type, which may have made it harder to detect links with cognitive performance.
The paper, “Curious people, resilient minds? The role of a curious personality in cognitive reserve and cognitive abilities,” was authored by Carolien Torenvliet, Dirk Bertens, Joukje M. Oosterman, and Iris Wiegand.

America’s only major etrog grower is in survival mode amid global competition

Unknown Publisher

06:47:18 KST

On a small ranch in California’s Central Valley, below the foothills of the Sierra Nevada, an orchard grows an ancient and sacred fruit: the etrog, or citron.
At just 7 acres, the etrog grove at Lindcove Ranch in Exeter is a speck in Tulare County, where about 40% of California’s citrus crops are grown. But by another measure, the grove is giant: Lindcove Ranch is the only major commercial grower of kosher etrogs in the United States.
“This is my life — I really feel a calling to do this,” said Greg Kirkpatrick, 65, whose family has owned and operated Lindcove Ranch for more than 60 years.
The fruit looks like a strange lemon, with a knobby exterior, thick rind, and oblong shape. It exudes a sweet, fresh, lemony fragrance. It’s not a fruit you find in grocery stores. But for Jews, the etrog is crucial for fulfilling the commandments of Sukkot. During the holiday ritual, which includes a blessing, an etrog is held in the left hand while a bundle of palm, myrtle, and willow, called a lulav, is held in the right. Brought together, they are shaken in the four directions as well as upward and downward.
The Kirkpatrick family, who are not Jewish, began growing etrogs 46 years ago, when an Orthodox distributor from the East Coast reached out looking for a domestic grower. He wanted to avoid the costly import process of bringing in etrogs from any of the three major growing regions around the world: Israel, Italy, and Morocco.
Greg’s father, John, who was already a successful citrus farmer, decided to give the specialty crop a chance, and soon citrons became an important part of the ranch’s annual income. Its citron orchard is part of a larger specialty-citrus operation, which includes yuzu, finger limes, makrut limes and sour oranges on about 30 acres. The citron, however, has been the ranch’s top-earning crop.
The citron business is a difficult one that Kirkpatrick likens to the diamond industry, with single pieces of fruit selling for hundreds and sometimes thousands of dollars.
“It’s not so much how many we can grow. It’s the quality of the fruit,” he said.
An etrog must meet stringent requirements to be considered kosher for ritual use. It must be largely unblemished, retain its natural stem, called a pitom, and come from a documented lineage of trees that have never been grafted or crossed with another variety.
The requirements make growing them different from growing ordinary citrus, according to David Karp, a pomologist, citron expert and citrus researcher at UC Riverside.
“Many people have tried to grow citron in California,” Karp said, noting the financial allure of an etrog’s price tag. “But just because you can grow a lemon well doesn’t mean you can grow an etrog that’s right for Jewish ritual use. You can grow them, but you’ll end up selling them for $5 each, or feeding them to the cows.”
Karp said little in horticulture can compare to the scrutiny that etrogs undergo for physical appearance, all for the sake of religious ritual.
'Nothing remotely like this,' Karp says
“There’s nothing remotely like this,” Karp said. “In Japan and perhaps other countries in Asia … there’s a cultural predilection for perfect, extremely expensive fruit that would be given as a gift. But there’s nothing quite like the citron for Jewish ritual use.”
A typical crop may begin with 10,000 to 15,000 pieces of fruit, but perhaps only half will be suitable to bring to market, according to Kirkpatrick. His harvesters are trained to pick citrons carefully after inspecting them on the tree.
“Being able to judge on the tree if the fruit’s good enough to bring into the packing house or not is important, so we don’t have to spend hours wading through and washing and dealing with fruit that’s not going to make it to the market,” Kirkpatrick said.
Once picked, each fruit is placed in its own spot in a foam tray. The citrons do not touch one another through the picking or packing process because even minor bruising or scratches can reduce their value.
There are multiple grades of etrog, ranging from cheaper, wholesale stock sold in mail-order sets to exceptionally large and blemish-free fruit sought by elite buyers.
For decades, Kirkpatrick mostly sold his etrogs through a specialized marketing network to Orthodox Jewish customers on the East Coast, who seek citrons that are not only kosher but exceptional in size, shape and color.
“My partner sells to a very select group of Orthodox Jews in Lakewood, New Jersey, by appointment only,” Kirkpatrick said. “And if you don’t have appointment-grade fruit, they won’t come to buy your fruit.”
In 2023, the ranch received about 22 inches of rain and failed to get much of a crop. That same year, John died, leaving the grieving Kirkpatrick family to navigate the business without him. Then, in 2025, the ranch produced what Kirkpatrick thought was a successful crop, but his marketing partner was unable to sell enough of the fruit to recoup the $250,000 it cost to produce. Those years were the two worst for etrogs in the ranch’s history.
Distributors simply want more top-grade etrogs than the Kirkpatrick operation is able to supply.
Rabbi Menachem Sirota, a longtime etrog distributor based in Lakewood, New Jersey, who has purchased Lindcove Ranch citrons in the past, said he has opted to buy from larger growers who can supply more of the highest grade of the fruit.
“Personally, as a wholesaler, I need a very large quantity of nice ones,” Sirota said. “For a while I was getting them from California, but in recent years I was not so satisfied with the quality.”
As a result of the huge financial loss from this shift, one of the family’s homes on the ranch ended up in foreclosure earlier this year, Kirkpatrick said. With labor being one of the ranch’s biggest expenses, Kirkpatrick has had to cut back from eight full-time employees to five part-time workers.
One of the Lindcove Ranch etrogs that was delivered to Chochmat HaLev. (Aaron Levy-Wolins/J. Staff/Report For America)
The family is even thinking of selling the ranch.
But for now, Kirkpatrick is trying to save his business by becoming more of his own wholesaler.
“We’re reinventing ourselves,” he said.
The ranch is focused on selling bulk orders directly to congregations. Just before Sukkot began on Sept. 25, for example, Kirkpatrick delivered a shipment to Congregation Beth Emek in Pleasanton and Chochmat HaLev in Berkeley, California.
Etrogs grown without pesticides, chemicals
Kirkpatrick is also courting a new customer base with a pitch built on a genuine difference between his etrogs and those grown abroad. His etrogs are grown without pesticides or other chemical treatments, so after Sukkot they can be eaten or made into jam, candied peel or liqueur.
That sets them apart from etrogs grown overseas, according to Karp.
“Because a citron is of little worth unless it’s cosmetically perfect, they spray the heck out of these trees and fruit,” he said, adding that he wouldn’t advise eating etrogs grown outside the United States. “It wouldn’t be my first choice for a marmalade, let’s put it that way.”
Whatever Kirkpatrick’s hopes, the fate of Lindcove Ranch, and of California-grown etrogs, will depend on several factors, including the weather and how the next growing season plays out.
“I’m a farmer, I’m always optimistic,” he said. “You have to be in the farming business.”

Three years after the Hamas attack on October 7, Israeli television is returning to the events of that day with an unusually wide range of documentaries, special broadcasts, interviews, and memorial projects.
What is most striking about much of the new programming is how different it feels from the documentaries made in the first months after the attack. Those early films, understandably, were compiled at breakneck speed using cellphone footage, security-camera recordings, interviews, news videos, and even clips filmed by the terrorists themselves, to establish what happened and preserve evidence.
Now, three years later, filmmakers have had time to follow survivors, bereaved families, soldiers, released hostages, and displaced communities as they try to rebuild their lives, heal, and honor those they lost. They have developed certain narratives carefully; many of the new documentaries are not simply about surviving October 7, but they examine what it has meant to keep living afterward.
KAN’s anniversary programming includes several major new productions, and one of the most distinctive returns to the first hours of October 7 from an unexpected vantage point: Soroka Medical Center in Beersheba.
On October 5, the program Zman Emet will air The Shift (HaMishmeret), a documentary by Chen Liberman and Roni Singer about what happened inside the Beersheba hospital as hundreds of casualties began arriving on the day. Soroka found itself at the center of what turned out to be an unprecedented medical emergency, treating hundreds of wounded patients in a single day while the attack was still taking place.
Lineup of TV programs detailing life after Oct. 7
The program includes previously unseen security-camera footage from inside the hospital documenting the corridors, entrance plaza, and emergency-room gates in the hours after the massacre began.
KAN News will also broadcast After They Returned from October 5-7, a three-part series by anchor Tali Moreno.
Rather than focusing on the campaign to free the hostages, Moreno visits families after their relatives have come home. They speak about physical and psychological pain, the need to get to know returning family members again, and the growing realization that release from captivity did not mean a return to the life that existed before October 7.
On October 6, Hanoch Daum’s Taboo brings together four former hostages – Aviva Siegel, Doron Katz-Asher, Eitan Mor, and Guy Gilboa-Dalal – for a kind of retreat on a moshav. The four speak openly about how they survived, recalling the constant fear and how they maintained hope, and they open up about the long and complicated process of rebuilding life after their release. In keeping with Daum’s irreverent style, there is some black humor along the way.
A new documentary, His Footprints Remained, airing on October 5, tells the story of Ibrahim Kharouba, a veteran Bedouin IDF tracker who was killed fighting at the Nahal Oz base. Kharouba, who came from a family of trackers and had devoted his life to military service, joined the effort to defend soldiers trapped at the base during the attack.
KAN will also revisit October 7 through its digital memorial project, “7.10.360,” an interactive reconstruction of events in communities, towns, and roads throughout the Gaza border area. Drawing on thousands of hours of footage, testimony, WhatsApp messages, documents, recordings, interviews, and news reports, the project allows users to follow events beginning at 6:29 a.m. and currently includes around 250 individual stories.
HOT 8 is also featuring innovative programming that spotlights new angles, such as Saved by the Ball (Segel Haser), a film by Aviad Livny, about the Kfar Aza Foxes soccer team. The movie was screened at Docaviv 2026 and will air on October 5 at 9:15 p.m. on HOT 8 and will also be available on HOT VOD and NEXT TV.
Just days after October 7, the surviving players, who had been evacuated with other residents of Kfar Aza to Shefayim, began playing soccer again. They did so without the shoes they had left behind in homes they had fled, and without many friends and relatives who had been murdered or kidnapped.
They soon realized that soccer provided something almost nothing else could: 90 minutes during which they could feel, at least a little, like the people they had been before the attack. Roughly every two weeks, the Foxes began playing anyone willing to face them, including teams from other kibbutzim, hi-tech companies, Maccabi Tel Aviv veterans, Supernova music festival survivors, and Ashdod Port employees.
But when organizers tried to revive the tournament among Gaza-border kibbutzim in the summer of 2024, the Kfar Aza players refused. Their position was simple: They would not return “until everyone comes back.”
Displacement, bereavement, uncertainty: how 'Whose Are You?' explores life after Oct. 7
Another new HOT 8 film, Whose Are You? (Shel Mi At?), directed by Dotan Naveh, approaches Kfar Aza from a very different perspective. Ten women who married men from the kibbutz had formed a WhatsApp group called “Whose Are You?” as a way of helping each other find their place in the tight-knit community. On October 7, that ordinary question suddenly took on an entirely different, and often tragic, meaning.
The documentary focuses on three of the women who all experienced October 7 in different ways, despite living so close to one another. It follows them for over two years through displacement, bereavement, new motherhood, uncertainty, and the attempt to decide whether – and where – they can make a home again. As they find themselves growing interested in the sights and sounds of India, they grapple with a painful question: If they allow themselves to move away from their grief and focus on other things, are they somehow abandoning their children? The film will air October 7 at 9:15 p.m. on HOT8.

HOT 8 will also broadcast the documentary, The Campaign: Disinformation in a Time of War, airing October 4 at 9:15 p.m., which examines propaganda and digital influence during the Israel-Hamas war. The soldiers express emotions ranging from denial and thoughts of revenge to humor, and they even play pranks on each other. Through recordings from the radio network, combined with intimate footage from combat, the film offers a window into the emotional world of soldiers in the period after October 7. The film traces the quiet voices of a group of reservists who answered the call to serve and found themselves staggering under the burden. It draws you into their world and makes you understand their stress and coping strategies.
These films will deepen people’s understanding of the tragedy itself, what led up to it, and, perhaps most importantly, will be helpful in grappling with the question of what to do next.

Rais-Ali Delvari Museum reopens after major redesign in southern Iran
TEHRAN - Rais-Ali Delvari Museum has reopened in the southern city of Delvar following extensive physical and content redesign, with officials describing the museum as a venue for documenting the resistance of local communities against British forces during World War I.
Speaking at the reopening ceremony on Thursday evening, Minister of Cultural Heritage, Tourism and Handicrafts Seyyed Reza Salehi-Amiri said museums serve as part of a nation’s collective memory and provide a platform for revisiting its historical and cultural identity.
Salehi-Amiri stressed the importance of presenting historical narratives accurately and professionally to younger generations as well as domestic and international visitors.
He said the Rais-Ali Delvari Museum had undergone comprehensive renovation and redesign, including the refurbishment of its rooms, revision and translation of exhibition content, landscaping and preparation of its central courtyard.
A newly designed relief wall in the courtyard depicts the history of resistance by Iranians, particularly the people of Tangestan, against British colonial forces. According to the minister, efforts have been made to present the historical narrative in a way suited to contemporary audiences.
Four books focusing on Rais-Ali Delvari, the history of resistance in Delvar and the struggles of the people of Tangestan against British forces were also unveiled during the ceremony.
Salehi-Amiri said museums need knowledgeable and capable narrators who can communicate historical subjects clearly and engagingly.
“Rais-Ali Delvari” has become part of the popular cultural memory of people in southern Iran and Tangestan, he said, adding that the museum could serve as a center for revisiting this historical identity and passing it on to future generations.
The minister also highlighted Bushehr’s historical significance as a center of resistance in modern Iranian history, saying the region’s heritage could be incorporated into new cultural tourism products.
He suggested turning the history of southern Iran’s resistance to foreign powers into a subject for research and tourism, using museums, storytelling, cultural tourism and specialized programs to present it to different audiences.
Salehi-Amiri also outlined the ministry’s plan to expand coastal and maritime tourism, saying Iran’s southern coastline, from Chabahar and Makran (or Makkoran) to Hormozgan, the Persian Gulf islands, Bushehr and Khuzestan, could be developed as an interconnected tourism corridor.
He said investment in accommodation facilities, piers and tourism services, along with the expansion of maritime tours, could help local communities and the economy of Bushehr benefit from the region’s coastal potential.
Rais-Ali Delvari (1882–1915) was a military leader and a prominent figure in Iran’s resistance to British forces following the occupation of Bushehr in 1915, during World War I. Iran had declared neutrality in the war.
Born in the port city of Bushehr, Delvari organized resistance in Tangestan after British forces occupied Bushehr and Delvar. He was killed on Sept. 2, 1915, aged 33.
His former house in Delvar, near Bushehr, was previously converted into an ethnological museum. The collection includes some of his personal belongings and historical documents, as well as firearms and horse equipment.

Space Development Agency to Launch 21 More Data Transport Satellites Next Week
Unknown
Unknown Publisher

06:01:00 KST

The Space Development Agency is set to launch 21 Tranche 1 data transport satellites on Oct. 5—its fourth Tranche 1 mission since last fall and the first to carry Northrop Grumman-built satellites.
The launch is scheduled for 1:15 a.m. local time from Vandenberg Space Force Base, Calif., on a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket. Once in orbit, the 21 spacecraft will join 63 Tranche 1 satellites built by York Space Systems and Lockheed Martin.
The mission represents a few firsts for Northrop. It’s not only the contractor’s inaugural Tranche 1 launch—the company is on the hook for another batch of 21 Tranche 1 Transport satellites and 14 missile tracking spacecraft—but it’s also the firm’s first time delivering and operating such a large quantity of satellites. SDA Director Gurpartap Sandhoo told reporters during an Oct. 1 prelaunch call that based on some of the challenges other providers have had with scaling, he expects there will be some challenges.
“It’s one thing to go through launch and early ops of one, two, maybe even three or four satellites. Twenty-one is a completely different ball of wax,” said Sandhoo, who is also the Space Force’s portfolio acquisition executive for missile warning, tracking, and defense. “We’ll see some issues, but I’m confident the team at Northrop … the issues that pop up, any patches, any updates we have to do, they’re ready to go.”
Since SDA started in 2019 to build out a proliferated constellation of data transport and missile warning satellites in low Earth orbit, the agency has faced setbacks that have delayed launches and fielding timelines. While it had hoped to have all 154 of its Tranche 1 satellites in low-Earth orbit by this summer, supply chain issues and technical challenges on the ground and in orbit disrupted those plans. Sandhoo has attributed many of those slowdowns to the challenges that come with building and launching satellites in large numbers.
“The issue has not been the capability of the system,” Sandhoo told reporters. “The issue has been building them at scale, getting them tested at scale, launching them at scale, doing the launch and early ops at scale. It’s the volume of these things that has been a challenge.”
Following the launch of York and Lockheed’s first spacecraft last year, initial on-orbit checkout and testing took much longer than the four to six months SDA expected. The agency ended up taking a nine-month pause to sort out issues on orbit and make changes on the ground to ensure its next satellites didn’t run into the same hiccups.
In July, SDA resumed Tranche 1 launches, flying York’s last 21 transport satellites. Checkout of those spacecraft went much smoother, Sandhoo said. Since then, the agency has moved control of York’s satellites into its operations center and started doing some limited experimentation. SDA has had some issues transferring Lockheed’s spacecraft to the operations center. To date, it’s only been able to move four, and Sandhoo said the company and SDA are working through the engineering issues to understand the cause and make sure it’s addressed before Lockheed launches more satellites.
“As we get the rest of the architecture, the rest of the planes up, it’s going to get more complicated,” he said. “We need to make sure the understanding is there, the behavior, and the dependability. Being able to get them in the ops and working properly is critical.”
A spokesperson for Lockheed told Air & Space Forces Magazine the company is working closely with SDA to transition the satellites to the agency’s control centers.
“These experiences are an important part of developing new, integrated space systems and we’re focused on ensuring reliability and mission success during this transition, while still moving quickly as a team,” Lockheed said. “We will apply lessons learned from each plane into future efforts.”
While the agency isn’t out of the woods yet in terms of schedule and scaling woes, Sandhoo told reporters he expects that as companies and their suppliers get used to building and flying larger numbers of satellites, their performance will improve. But for now, he said, he’s not satisfied with the time it’s taken to get to this point.
Asked what SDA could have done differently to avoid these setbacks, Sandhoo said it’s possible that for the early tranches of satellites, the agency could have required more insight into the companies’ processes rather than opt for the more hands-off approach it took in its contracting strategy.
“We are trying to get to a place where we can have a capability-driven architecture. We tell them what the system needs to do, and our vendors and our industrial base builds that out to that spec with minimal oversight,” he said. “Maybe we should have, for the first tranche, been probably more intrusive in that process than we were.”
Following the Oct. 5 launch, SDA is eyeing its first Tranche 1 tracking layer mission either in December or early next year. Beyond that flight, it’s hoping to finish the last four launches and have all Tranche 1 satellites in orbit by late spring 2027.

War and diplomatic crisis in the Horn of Africa: Why Ethiopia is at odds with its neighbors
Renewed fighting between Ethiopia’s federal government and regional armed groups has escalated into a diplomatic rupture with former wartime ally Eritrea. What was an internal conflict has become a wider regional confrontation over ports, water, and alleged foreign interference. The violence threatens a fragile 2022 peace deal that ended Ethiopia’s two-year Tigray war, raising fears of a return to full-scale conflict.
What triggered the diplomatic rupture?
Ethiopia announced on Thursday the closure of its embassy in the Eritrean capital, Asmara, and ordered ten Eritrean diplomats stationed in Addis Ababa to leave Ethiopia within 48 hours. Its Foreign Ministry accused them of threatening national security and blamed Eritrea for “direct and indirect acts of hostility and belligerence against Ethiopia.”
Eritrea reciprocated the measure and severed “all diplomatic ties with Ethiopia with all the negative corollaries that this entails.” It rejected the allegations and accused Addis Ababa of planning to take Eritrean ports and of training and arming Eritrean opposition groups to pursue regime change. Addis Ababa said it remained committed to peaceful coexistence and resolving disputes through dialogue.
The breakdown in relations came days after Addis Ababa accused Eritrea of financing armed groups seeking to overthrow Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed. Asmara denies the accusations.
What are they fighting over?
The conflict grew out of a struggle over federal power and regional autonomy. The Tigray People’s Liberation Front (TPLF), the political party governing Ethiopia’s northern Tigray region, dominated national politics for nearly three decades before Abiy took office in 2018. Tigrayan leaders accused his government of seeking to centralize power at the region’s expense. Their confrontation erupted into war in November 2020.
Eritrean troops and Amhara forces fought alongside the federal army. Hundreds of thousands died before an African Union-mediated agreement signed in Pretoria, South Africa, in November 2022 halted the large-scale fighting. But disputes over territory claimed by both Amhara and Tigray, the return of displaced people, and the disarmament of Tigrayan forces remained unresolved. Addis Ababa accuses the TPLF of failing to disarm; Tigrayan leaders accuse the government of failing to honor the settlement.
The TPLF further strained the settlement in May by restoring Tigray’s prewar regional council, displacing the interim administration established under the agreement. Amhara militia fighters known as Fano, meanwhile, had turned against Addis Ababa in 2023 after the government moved to dismantle regional security forces.
Separate disputes have widened the confrontation. Landlocked Ethiopia wants sovereign access to the Red Sea, particularly through Eritrea’s Assab port, which it calls vital to its economy and security. Asmara fears an attempt to take its coastline by force. Egypt and Sudan also dispute Ethiopia’s operation of the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam on the Blue Nile, fearing threats to their water supplies and demanding a binding agreement governing its filling and operation.
Why has fighting resumed in Ethiopia?
On September 20, seven armed groups, including the TPLF and its former battlefield enemies, announced an alliance pledging to oust Abiy’s government and establish a transitional administration. The coalition, known as the Ethiopian Peoples’ Forces Alliance for Survival, accused the ruling Prosperity Party of imposing “dictatorial” rule and pushing the East African country toward disintegration.
Clashes erupted two days later. TPLF forces took control of airports in Mekelle, Axum and Shire, prompting state-owned Ethiopian Airlines to suspend flights to all three cities. Flights were also suspended to the Amhara town of Lalibela, home to UNESCO-listed rock-hewn churches, as fighting spread.
On September 23, Addis Ababa accused the TPLF of launching an “all-out offensive” against government positions in Amhara and Afar. Tigray’s regional authorities said their troops entered a “defensive battle” after federal forces attacked them with aircraft, drones, and artillery. The federal army said it had repelled attacks in Amhara’s North Wollo Zone and killed 272 Tigrayan fighters and wounded 260 others in a counteroffensive.
The army and its allied Tigray Peace Forces subsequently reported capturing Alamata and Korem and retaking Erebti in Afar. Explosions were also heard in Addis Ababa overnight Wednesday to Thursday of this week, although opposition claims of drone attacks remain unverified.
What is the impact on civilians?
Doctors Without Borders reported more than 420 war-wounded patients at Korem and Alamata hospitals between September 22 and 25, and over 300 at Suhul hospital in Shire during the latest fighting. Insecurity forced the organization to suspend activities at Alamata hospital.
On September 25, UN human rights chief Volker Turk condemned forced recruitment by the TPLF, including of children, and reprisals against those resisting recruitment and their families. He also raised reports of arbitrary conscription by government forces.
Why did Ethiopia and Eritrea fall out?
Eritrea was once part of Ethiopia but gained independence in 1993 after a decades-long liberation war, leaving Ethiopia landlocked. The neighbors fought a border war in 1998–2000, followed by years of hostility until Abiy and Eritrean President Isaias Afwerki reconciled in 2018. The rapprochement helped earn Abiy the 2019 Nobel Peace Prize.
Their partnership fractured after the Tigray war. Eritrea was not a party to the Pretoria agreement despite its military involvement. According to the International Crisis Group, Asmara resented being sidelined and wanted Addis Ababa to pursue a complete battlefield victory over the TPLF rather than a negotiated settlement.
Who else is accused of backing the armed groups?
Ethiopia’s accusations extend beyond Eritrea. Army chief Birhanu Jula also accused Egypt and Sudan of supporting the rebels. Both countries denied the allegations. On Thursday, Ethiopia ordered an Egyptian diplomat to leave within 48 hours. Egypt retaliated by giving an Ethiopian Embassy counselor the same deadline, calling Addis Ababa’s decision unjustified.
Sudan’s army has also accused Ethiopia of supporting the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces, its opponent in a civil war that began in April 2023.
How are regional and international bodies responding?
The AU has urged Ethiopia, Eritrea, and Egypt to exercise “maximum restraint and refrain from actions or statements that could further escalate tensions and undermine regional peace and stability.” It asked the three countries to resolve their differences through diplomatic channels.
The UN and EU also urged de-escalation. Russia called for a ceasefire on September 25. Foreign Ministry spokeswoman Maria Zakharova urged compliance with peace commitments and noninterference, stressing Ethiopia’s unity and territorial integrity.

Netflix’s East of Eden Brilliantly Reinterprets ‘Evil’ Cathy—Without Betraying Steinbeck
Unknown
Unknown Publisher
2026-10-02
20:51:42 UTC
05:51:42 KST

This article discusses all seven episodes of Netflix's East of Eden.
When John Steinbeck published East of Eden, a top contender for Great American Novel, in 1952, the response to its primary female character, Cathy Ames, was effectively unanimous. Whether they admired or deplored Steinbeck’s portrayal of her, midcentury critics judged Cathy to be pure evil, a veritable Satan in stockings. Male reviewers, which is to say just about all reviewers, disagreed only on the magnitude of her repugnance; while this publication deemed her a “vicious slut,” the New York Times crowned her the “most vicious female in literature.” And why not? She drives a man to suicide and kills her parents while still a teen. She sleeps with her new husband’s brother, gives birth to twins, and promptly abandons them, gravely injuring her shocked spouse as she escapes. Then she ensconces herself at a brothel, kisses up to the madam, fatally poisons the lonely woman once she has willed Cathy her fortune. Her career from there on out is a blur of sadomasochism, blackmail, exploitation, and violence.
It’s hard to imagine a character with this rap sheet as anything short of a psychopath. But here’s a thought experiment, one I often found myself running as I read the great novel and watched Zoe Kazan’s insightful Netflix adaptation: What if Cathy, born into the prison of 19th century womanhood, had behaved less viciously in any of the above situations? She might’ve prevented an adult man’s suicide—but only by becoming his child bride. She didn’t have to murder her parents—but if she hadn’t, her father would have kept beating her at the slightest provocation. She could have fled from her husband-to-be before the marriage, the infidelity, the pregnancy, the gunshot—but she would have been a wounded and destitute and potentially wanted woman, with nowhere to go and doom all but certain. And so on. In other words: Cathy might not have acted much differently if, instead of personifying evil, she were chasing freedom at any cost.
Kazan’s seven-episode series proceeds as if this were the case. It’s a feminist project, in that it takes for granted that a character who happens to be a woman should be given the same humanity as one who happens to be a man. And despite its allegorical nature, with two generations of brothers reenacting the tragedy of Cain and Abel, East of Eden is a book that grants even minor characters unique personalities and preoccupations, good qualities and bad. By now, we’re used to seeing female villains like Cruella de Vil and Nurse Ratched glibly issued trauma-plot backstories and wardrobes that slay and rebranded as meme-worthy badass antiheroes. What makes Kazan’s Cathy refreshing—and Florence Pugh’s mesmerizingly self-possessed performance so moving—is that the character is so firmly grounded in Steinbeck’s Cathy. “I think the text supports my reading,” the creator recently told TIME, and that wasn’t false bravado. She saw no need to invent explanations for Cathy’s ghastly behavior because they were already abundant in the original text. Kazan also evidently understood that to do Cathy justice is not to vindicate or forgive her; it’s to make sure we genuinely see her.
This is the symbolic as well as the literal effect of the series’ opening scene, which replaces Steinbeck’s patient California scene-setting with a flash-forward to a crucial moment that comes significantly later in the chronology. The night sky is so black, it takes a second to perceive Pugh’s brutally beaten and whimpering Cathy crawling on her belly towards the farmhouse where her arrival will change lives for generations to come. “I believe there are monsters born in this world to human parents,” she intones in a voiceover. “A monster is a variation from the accepted normal, to a greater or less degree… Still, you must not forget, everyone is normal to himself. And to a monster, the norm must seem monstrous.” These ideas, and most of the language itself, come straight from Steinbeck’s introduction of Cathy. Kazan’s departure is to put them in her mouth, rather than that of a narrator who resembles the author. In their original context, but even more so in the new one, they suggest a lack of malice on the monster’s part. Can you blame someone for being the only thing she could possibly have been?
The scene also gets viewers used to seeing through Cathy’s eyes, before the premiere backtracks through the lives of rival brothers Adam (Christopher Abbott) and Charles Trask (Mike Faist) up until her appearance at their Connecticut farm. East of Eden’s second episode, “Cathy”—the first of two named after an incarnation of the character—interweaves her convalescence, nursed by the gentle and guileless Adam, with her own origin story. In our earliest glimpse of Cathy, she’s a precocious five-year-old paging through Alice in Wonderland and looking quite a bit like classic illustrations of Alice when an older boy’s outstretched hands offer her candy if she’ll follow him into the woods. (Alice author Lewis Carroll’s biography adds its own dark resonance to the scene.) It’s her choice to run after the sweets; running after sweets is fundamental to her nature. But isn’t it tragic that Cathy’s Eve-and-the-apple moment comes so early in life, and that the implied (then, much later in the series, confirmed) consequence of her decision is a sexual assault that cements her misanthropic worldview?
Pugh told Vanity Fair it was this scene, modified somewhat from an incident in the book where a slightly older Cathy is supposed to have led boys into sin, that drew her to the role: “I thought it was the most beautiful love letter to protecting her as a child. When I read that, I was like, ‘I can get behind her.’ Because I can’t imagine for a moment after that abuse that she’ll ever be the same person again.” Indeed, as she develops more agency, her choices become more selfish and violent. What the schoolteacher (who in our time would be considered an abuser) and her parents have in common is that they try to possess her, to constrict her freedom—a nightmare for any person, but especially for someone whose bodily sovereignty was violated in childhood. When she crawls, half-dead, onto the Trasks’ farm, she has just narrowly escaped murder at the hands of a pimp who made her his mistress, then grew jealous because she wouldn’t reciprocate his love. All of this comes straight from Steinbeck, though Kazan and Pugh put us closer to Cathy’s experience. We feel her fear, her distress, her simple longing to be left alone.
If she ever had love to give—and the series does leave that possibility open—it’s been drained by the time she marries Adam and follows him to a farm in California, pregnant with twins who may be Charles’ biological sons, having duly warned Adam that this was all a very bad idea. So she keeps doing what she’s always done when someone traps her: she escapes, wounding him physically and destroying him emotionally. She does the same to Faye (Martha Plimpton), the madam whose adoption of Cathy as surrogate daughter and official heir comes with the caveat that she can no longer earn her own money seeing clients. Unlike some of her past victims, neither Adam nor Faye deserves the pain she inflicts on them. But they’re casualties of their own denial almost as much as they are of Cathy’s monstrousness. And, in her own horrible way, she’s still just trying to get free. “The only freedom in this world is economic freedom,” she says.
For a character traditionally read as pure evil, she comes off as surprisingly fair-minded and free of wanton malice. Steinbeck made a point of depicting her as such in a passage on how (after assuming the name Kate) she manages the brothel she inherits from Faye: “If they followed the rules she laid down, followed them exactly, Kate would take care of them and protect them. There was no love involved and no respect. She never rewarded them and she punished an offender only twice before she removed her. The girls did have the security of knowing that they would not be punished without cause.” Her sadism, in the book and in the show, is reserved for the hypocritical men who wield power, preach virtue, and demand respect in their public lives, then come to the brothel to indulge in all manner of degradation. And it reflects the outlook she developed at age five—the knowledge of good and evil she gleaned from her bite of the fruit.
As the book progresses, Kazan has rightly noted, “in a weird way, Steinbeck loses control of Cathy as a character and punishes her for that. He starts being really mean about her, like her physical appearance.” She ages unattractively. Arthritis gnarls her hands. But Steinbeck deserves credit for examining every character from multiple angles—even Cathy, to whom he gave more grace, or at least plausible deniability, than critics tended to perceive. “When I said Cathy was a monster it seemed to me that it was so,” he writes at one point. He continues:
"Now I have bent close with a glass over the small print of her and reread the footnotes, and I wonder if it was true. The trouble is that since we cannot know what she wanted, we will never know whether or not she got it. If rather than running toward something, she ran away from something, we can’t know whether she escaped. Who knows but that she tried to tell someone or everyone what she was like and could not, for lack of a common language. Her life may have been her language, formal, developed, indecipherable. It is easy to say she was bad, but there is little meaning unless we know why."
It’s a remarkable admission; an author is supposed to know everything there is to know about their characters, even things it would never occur to any reader to wonder. That Steinbeck instead throws up his hands and tells us that there are aspects of Cathy that will always remain unknowable to him leaves room for precisely the sort of interpretation Kazan and Pugh are doing. Maybe it’s reductive to say they can get closer to her because they are women and know firsthand how much going through life in a female body can feel like having your most cynical assumptions about human nature constantly reaffirmed… but the fact remains that they do get quite close to her. (Though the 1955 film adaptation by Kazan’s grandfather, Elia Kazan, is a masterpiece in its own right, its emphasis on Adam’s sons relegates Cathy to the margins.)
Kazan takes her greatest liberties with the character as her suicide—an act that stems from her delayed acknowledgment that, in fact, her life has no purpose—approaches. She has druggily hallucinated conversations with Charles, the one person she might have once been capable of loving. Cathy is drawn in parallel with the story’s other selfish Cs, Charles and her son, Cal (Joseph Zada). Both come off as better people, though we get the sense that nurture accounts for the difference more than nature. Charles has the means to sequester himself at the farm. Cal benefits from a loving, morally sound upbringing by the brilliant Lee (Hoon Lee) and, ultimately, his father’s blessing. No one has ever given Cathy the permission that Adam, on his deathbed, gives Cal to try to live a good life.
So it’s fitting that Cathy’s final hallucinated encounter is a tearful embrace with the five-year-old in the Alice in Wonderland frock who may have been the last version of the character capable, with more freedom and if not for that boy with the candy, of overcoming her cold nature. Pugh is astonishing in this scene, letting loose a lifetime’s worth of masked and suppressed emotion. The Cathy Steinbeck created was never Satan. She’s more like Eve, and Kazan reinforces that resemblance. Yet this East of Eden leaves us with an alternate image, of Cathy as Alice, chasing the white rabbit of her innate appetites down a hole from which she can never emerge.

Timber installation Reinterprets Lunano’s Stone Towers
Located in Lunano, in Italy’s Marche region, What the Stones Whisper is a six-meter-tall timber tower that reinterprets the nearby Torre Lunano and the stone structures scattered across the surrounding landscape. Designed and built by Collectif MAGMA in two weeks for the Fe.M – Festival di Microarchitettura, the installation uses limited timber, self-build methods, and a repeated structural system to create a lightweight landmark along a local walking path.
Eight triangulated timber frames form the tower, using 50 × 60 mm pine battens assembled through repeated connections and bracing. Ten layers of timber shingles are suspended from jute ropes to create a discontinuous envelope that moves with the wind. Their changing position produces shifting patterns of light and shadow while allowing air and sound to pass through the structure.
Movable Timber Envelope Frames the Landscape and Sky
Two narrow doors frame views toward the surrounding hills and lead into the tower’s open interior. Successive structural frames gradually direct the gaze upward toward the sky, while the moving shingles create a changing acoustic and visual environment.
The tower rests on a shallow timber foundation designed to remain largely concealed beneath the ground. Built by a small team of amateur builders, the construction process involved fabricating the timber frames and shingle modules in an open-air workshop before assembling them on site. The project by design practice Collectif MAGMA uses repetition, lightweight construction, and a movable envelope to reinterpret the permanence of the region’s stone architecture through a temporary timber structure.

Hundreds of Arabs from the Palestinian Authority apply for asylum in Israel each year on the basis of the fact that they are members of the LGBTQ community. However, even asylum does not guarantee protection from persecution by their own families. Other queer Arabs insist on identifying as Palestinian even though they are Israeli citizens, while others face prejudice from the international community because of their support for Israel.
On a summer night in Tel Aviv, a group of queer Arabs has gathered for a party at a nightclub. Young and flamboyantly dressed, they laugh loudly, speak in deliberately campy voices, and gesture theatrically, seemingly eager to attract attention. One of them, a strikingly beautiful woman, is asked by another patron, “Where are you from?” She pointedly replies, “Palestine,” seemingly testing her interlocutor’s reaction.
Even among queer Arabs born and raised in Israel who are fluent in Hebrew, Arabic, and English, life is still far from secure, and those still living in the Palestinian Authority (or who come to Israel to seek asylum) face an even more uncertain future. According to data from HIAS, a Jewish organization that advocates for refugees, as of July 2026 there were around 300 Palestinian LGBTQ asylum seekers in Israel, and that figure includes only those known to organizations working in the field.
“They hung me up in an abandoned building and started beating me”
In November 2023, 23-year-old Yamen, who is gay, fled to Israel from Nablus, a major Palestinian city in the northern West Bank. He was forced to abandon his studies in physics at a local university, and after arriving in Israel he found a job cleaning offices. Yamen told HIAS representatives that he was learning Hebrew and looking for better work.
When Yamen’s family learned about his sexual orientation, his uncle — an intelligence officer with the Palestinian Authority — threatened to have him jailed. His relatives also accused him of collaborating with Israel, shouted at him, and beat him.
From the time Yamen was a teenager, his family had noticed that he was somewhat more effeminate than other young men, but they never discussed it. According to HIAS representatives, this is a common pattern in Palestinian families. Violence against LGBTQ people by their families begins only after a relative’s sexual orientation or gender identity becomes known to others.
Yamen says that from a young age, people around him treated him aggressively – mocking, harassing, and attacking him. Once, when he was 14, a police officer stopped him and a friend. According to Yamen, the reason was their “refined” appearance. The officer wanted to put them in his patrol car, but after a few phone calls he ultimately let the teenagers go. Yamen concluded that the officer had only wanted to frighten them.
However, an attempt to abduct Yamen succeeded and forced him to flee:
“One day I was just walking down the street. About 20 men suddenly attacked me – they simply forced me into a car and drove me away. They hung me up in some abandoned building and started beating me. They were relatively young. Then others joined them – men around my father’s age. I didn’t understand who they were or what they wanted from me. I had never experienced anything like this and had no idea how to react. It was as if they were saying, ‘We found a guy who looks gay, let’s take him and make fun of him for entertainment.’ I couldn’t believe this was happening to me…
They took everything I had, even my phone. Then things became even more serious when they found out my last name. They said, ‘Now we have a problem, and we don’t know what to do.’ They called my family. When my relatives came to find out what had happened and why I had been attacked like that, they were told, ‘Your son is this and that.’ I wasn’t allowed to explain anything, wasn’t given a chance to say a word. I had been a good child my whole life, and this was the first problem that required my family’s intervention.
But my relatives didn’t take my side. They were angry with me, and I didn’t understand what I had done. Instead of being angry with the people who had abducted their son, they simply abandoned me. It was the worst feeling I had ever experienced in my life…
From that moment on, my family turned my life into hell. The story spread until it reached the police, and an investigation was opened — but it was closed very quickly, because nobody cares about an abducted gay man. No one was prepared to protect me. And then I realized: if I want to stay alive, I have to run away from there.”
“I was afraid to go outside”
Twenty-five-year-old lesbian Manar fled to Israel from a village in the West Bank after her father discovered romantic messages with another woman on her phone.
Manar later told HIAS representatives her story:
“When my father found out that I was attracted to women, he started beating me regularly. Then my family decided to marry me off to some guy. He was a loser, a bad person, and used drugs. They simply put me in his house. There was no real wedding, no ceremony, nothing. I was basically kept there, unable to leave the house. “One day, on my way home from work, I noticed someone photographing me on the street. I later recognized him as a close friend of my cousin. As it happened, there was a police officer nearby. I approached him and said that the guy over there was photographing me for my cousin, who had threatened to kill me. Now they would know where I was. The police did nothing. They didn’t even bother approaching him to ask why he was photographing me. They said it was none of their business, that it was purely a family matter.
“So I stopped working and stayed home because I was afraid to go outside, to go to work, or to be seen by anyone. I didn’t know what to do with myself or where to go. I became depressed and tried to kill myself... My partner found me and called an ambulance.”
After another attack, Manar decided to flee to Israel.

Twenty-seven-year-old Amjad, who was living in a third country by the time of his interview, described how his “once-loving” family discovered his correspondence with a man and tied him to a post outside the house for more than two weeks. Twenty-eight-year-old Amir, from a major city in the West Bank, said armed members of the Palestinian security forces attacked the home where he lived with his partner. Amir was detained for several days in connection with a case involving “indecent acts.”
“I was constantly pretending so I could be accepted”
“As a child, I often felt like an outsider. I remember sitting alone at lunch and wondering how people could make friends so easily. Mean Girls helped me,” says 28-year-old K. He dyed his hair platinum blond while keeping his perfectly trimmed beard black.
“That movie taught me to pretend to be someone else so society would accept me. It taught me to project confidence and put on a show for those around me. K. grew up in northern Israel, in Nazareth, a city sacred to Christians. His father is an Arab Christian, his mother comes from a secular Lebanese family, and he has Jewish and Muslim relatives. K. says his parents celebrate Christian holidays, while everyone in the family tries to respect one another — they never discuss politics.
From childhood, K. understood that he did not fit any conventional mold, and his attraction to both men and women made things even more complicated. He never came out to his family:
“I used to think identity had to be fixed, that you had to choose one label and stick to it. If I’m gay, for example, then I’m only gay. But as I grew older, I realized I couldn’t fit myself into any one category. Over the years, I discovered new things about my sexuality. More importantly though, I realized I don’t owe anyone a simple definition of who I am. My identity is fluid and multilayered, and I don’t feel the need to explain who I am to anyone."
At the same time, his family was wary of the way he dressed, spoke, and moved. They drew their own conclusions.
“When I was younger, my parents would comment on it. It wasn’t easy for them either, because I had always been the ‘black sheep’ of the family. Whenever someone was getting married, they would think twice about whether to invite me, because they knew I might show up in some huge oversized suit and everyone would say, ‘What the hell are you wearing? That’s your grandfather’s suit!’"
After he earned a bachelor’s degree in fashion design and began working with major brands, K.'s parents realized that their son wasn’t just “playing games.”
“Yes, damn it, I’m Palestinian”
As a child, K. identified as Lebanese, but after struggling to find work following graduation from university — and amid the war that followed the Hamas terrorist attacks of October 7, 2023 — he came to fully embrace his Palestinian identity:
“While I was studying, I lived in a bubble – peace, love, and all that. But after graduation, I saw my Jewish classmates move up very quickly in the fashion world, while doors were closing for me. I asked myself, ‘Is this really happening because I’m Arab?’
Then I started noticing that there were almost no Arab names at Tel Aviv’s nightclubs and festivals – no DJs, designers, or artists. That’s when I realized it wasn’t just about me. There weren’t enough platforms where Palestinian creatives could be seen and supported.
So I said, ‘Yes, damn it, I’m Palestinian, and I’m going to fight for this community.’ That’s how I created a platform for Palestinian creatives. It’s not just about parties; it’s a space creating opportunities for people who didn’t have them, just as I once didn’t.”
In July 2026, K. moved to Berlin to begin pursuing a master’s degree while continuing to develop his project. When people ask where he is from, he insists on answering “Palestine” — “and my best friend, who is Jewish, says he’s from Israel,” K. adds with a smile.
The status of Arabs in Israel
According to 2025 data from the Israeli government, around 2.1 million Arabs live in the country, accounting for about 21% of the population. Arab citizens formally enjoy equal rights: they can vote, stand for election to the Knesset, form political parties, bring cases before the courts, and so on. At the same time, a 2024 study by the Israel Democracy Institute found that 79% of Arab respondents believe they face discrimination compared with Jews.
In addition to Arab citizens, there are Arabs living under Israeli control – residents of East Jerusalem and the West Bank. The West Bank itself is divided into three areas under the Oslo Accords signed in 1995. Area A is fully controlled by the Palestinian Authority. Area B is under joint control: the PA administers civil affairs, while Israel is responsible for security. Area C is under Israeli control. More than 3 million Arabs live in the West Bank, according to the Palestinian Authority’s Central Bureau of Statistics.
The most vulnerable group in the State of Israel
While K. was able to build a life for himself and move to Berlin to study, hundreds of other queer people from Israel’s Arab community face persecution because of their identity.
“This is the most vulnerable group in the State of Israel,” says lawyer Nimrod Gorenstein, chair of The Aguda, Israel’s LGBTQ rights association. “Unfortunately, some are still killed in horrific crimes that never even make the news. Others repeatedly seek shelter and a roof over their heads so they can live in safety and peace and integrate into society.”
Some are still killed in horrific crimes that never even make the news
Although Israel generally grants asylum to queer Palestinians living in the West Bank, this does not always guarantee their safety or protect them from their persecutors. In 2022, for example, 25-year-old gay refugee Ahmad Abu-Maruhi was brutally murdered near Hebron: he was decapitated and his body mutilated. Human rights activists said a video of his murder was sent to other LGBTQ Palestinians to intimidate them.
There is no official law against homosexuality in the Palestinian Authority, explains Hamoudi Omar, the director of Israel's Beit al-Mim nonprofit organization, which helps LGBTQ Arabs in Israel and the West Bank. Nevertheless, religious and social norms make life unbearable for people in the community.
Last year, around 400 people approached Beit al-Mim seeking asylum, and upwards of 600 sought psychological support — up from just 200 two years ago. Omar described one of the most memorable cases the organization has encountered this year: after learning of a young man’s sexual orientation, his father and brother cut off his genitals. The young man sent the organization’s staff photographs, but later stopped responding.
From Berlin, K. notes the even harsher reality faced by LGBTQ people living in the West Bank.
“Their reality is nothing like mine. When we meet and I hear their stories, I realize I wouldn’t have survived in their place. There, you can be killed simply for wearing the wrong thing. But even there, small communities hold secret gatherings where people can dress however they want.”
“Israeli Arabs look down on people from the West Bank”
As a rule, queer people forced to leave the West Bank do not receive official refugee status in Israel. Instead, they are issued temporary residence permits – valid for three, six, or 12 months – which must be renewed. According to Omar, before October 7, around 90% of applicants received permits; in the aftermath of the Hamas terrorist attacks, however, the figure has fallen to about 5%.
Without official status, queer Palestinians struggle to find permanent housing, to secure work, or to access healthcare. Low levels of education and limited Hebrew skills make integration even harder. Israeli Arabs look down on people from the West Bank, while Jews see them as a security threat, Omar explains.

Many of the refugees dream of moving to Canada, Switzerland, or Europe, but their limited education and lack of language skills also stand in their way. Beit al-Mim helps them find safe housing in Israel and provides legal and psychological support — sometimes simply making sure they have food to eat.
Omar himself is an openly gay Muslim and an Israeli citizen. When he came out, his religious, hijab-wearing mother told him, “God made you this way. We love you as you are.” But, he says, far from all LGBTQ Arabs in Israel find support from their families. “Many face homophobia and threats at work and in their communities. On top of that, we often experience discrimination because we are Arabs,” he says, recalling an incident in which a gay couple in Budapest asked him whether he was carrying a knife after hearing him speak Arabic.
Triple prejudice
L., an Arab transgender woman, has identified as Israeli since childhood. She became particularly aware of her Israeli identity during the Second Lebanon War in the summer of 2006. “They attacked all of us. I was born in this Jewish state. This is my home,” she explains.
L.'s mother sought to integrate into Israeli society. Her father, however, insisted on identifying as Palestinian, seeing it as a form of resistance against the “Zionist regime” — “they were constantly arguing over which language should dominate in our home,” L. recalls.
After high school, L. voluntarily enlisted in the Israeli military, where she first began seriously thinking about the Palestinian side of the conflict. “That’s when I began confronting the issue of the occupation and what we as a state do beyond our borders,” she says.
Reflecting on her gender identity, L. says she is tired of living up to other people’s expectations. “Some people hate me because I’m too queer. Others because I’m Israeli. Some don’t like me because I’m Arab. Others because I said something they didn’t like. Well, to hell with it! It’s time to return to being myself,” she concludes.

Salcedo says she had been considering egg freezing for around six years. Assisted reproductive technology (ART) involves retrieving and cryopreserving mature, unfertilised eggs — known as mature oocytes — for potential future use.
As women age and the number and quality of eggs naturally decline, freezing eggs can preserve the potential to use younger eggs later. But experts say it doesn't guarantee a future pregnancy or birth, and success depends on factors including the woman's age when the eggs are frozen and the number of eggs stored.

For Salcedo, who says she has focused on her career in recent years and is currently single, egg freezing has provided an "insurance policy".
I've got a lot of friends that are having children, getting married and ... it's something that I've always wanted to do. It's just not been where my life has taken me.
For women considering egg freezing, the decision can involve balancing age-related fertility decline with the treatment's cost, physical demands and uncertain chances of ultimately using the frozen eggs — raising questions about how much reassurance the technology can realistically provide.
Why more women are considering fertility preservation
Australia's first baby conceived through in vitro fertilisation (IVF) was born in 1980.
Today, ART is an established part of fertility care in Australia, with about one in 16 babies being born as a result of assisted reproductive technology, according to information published by the government-funded platform YourIVFSuccess.
Tamara Hunter is a Perth-based reproductive endocrinology and fertility specialist and believes more women are choosing to freeze their eggs for elective, or non-medical reasons.
She sees women like Salcedo daily and says she also turns away some young women.
"For whatever reason, they've asked their GP to perform a test of their ovarian reserve," Hunter tells SBS News.
"Then, they'll come in with that level, going 'I need to freeze my eggs'. [To] some of them, I'll say, 'Actually, you're 25, your egg count is within the normal range, there's no hurry. Why don't you come back and see me when you're 30?'"
But a woman's egg count is only part of the fertility picture. Ovarian reserve tests can indicate the number of eggs remaining, but they cannot measure egg quality or reliably predict a woman's chances of conceiving naturally.
A study led by researchers at the University of California, Los Angeles, published last year, analysed elective egg-freezing rates in the United States between 2014 and 2021. It found the number of planned elective egg-freezing cycles increased from 4,153 in 2014 to 16,436 in 2021. Among women who froze their eggs between 2014 and 2016, 5.7 per cent returned to use them within the study's five-to-seven-year follow-up period.
But as elective egg freezing has become more common, researchers are still working to understand how frozen eggs perform over longer storage periods.
Hunter says it's unclear whether the amount of time an egg is frozen affects fertility.
We just don't have the large studies to be able to show that ... if you leave it [eggs] frozen for more than five years, it's going to be significantly degraded.
She says that's because data has historically drawn from the women who freeze their eggs for medical reasons, including women whose fertility was at risk from treatments such as chemotherapy, making it difficult to apply some of those findings directly to women who freeze their eggs electively.
Modern egg-freezing techniques have also changed the evidence base. Vitrification — a rapid freezing technique — has improved the survival of eggs and embryos during freezing and thawing, although researchers say long-term outcome data for planned egg freezing are still developing.
But professor Georgina Chambers, director of the National Perinatal Epidemiology and Statistics Unit at the University of New South Wales, says modern freezing techniques have produced strong results for embryos.
"We get very good success rates from frozen embryos. How long can they be stored? We're not sure yet, but we know that with modern freezing techniques that are called vitrification ... the quality of those frozen embryos remains extremely high," she tells SBS News.
Chambers is also a lead author of a report which tracked ART treatment practices, pregnancy and birth outcomes across Australia and New Zealand.
The 2024 Australian and New Zealand Assisted Reproduction Database report recorded more than 113,000 IVF treatment cycles in 2024, resulting in more than 20,900 babies. It also found three in four IVF babies were born using frozen embryos, reflecting a clear shift towards frozen embryo transfers.
Chambers says freezing embryos can allow a woman's body to return to a natural cycle before the embryo is transferred, which can improve treatment outcomes.
"We allow the woman's body to return to a natural cycle, where her endometrium is ready to receive that embryo, and we get higher success rates from that process."
How much does egg freezing cost?
In Australia, elective egg freezing is usually self-funded, although costs vary between clinics and some medically indicated fertility-preservation treatment may attract Medicare rebates.
A single egg freezing cycle can cost several thousand dollars, with the total depending on what is included in the clinic's fee, as well as medication, anaesthetist, hospital and storage costs. YourIVFSuccess estimates elective egg freezing typically costs between $8,000 and $12,000 per cycle upfront, with additional annual storage fees.
The cost of using the eggs later is separate. The eggs must be thawed and fertilised to create embryos that are transferred to the uterus, creating further treatment and out-of-pocket expenses.
In September, Salcedo underwent her second egg freezing cycle via a private fertility specialist. Working in the banking sector, she was entitled to five days of family planning leave but admits the process was physically and emotionally taxing.
In my first cycle, I was just hungry, constantly hungry. I gained 7kg in that time from start to extraction, and I was like, 'Oh my god, my body's completely changed'.
Egg freezing requires women to administer daily hormone injections for about two weeks to stimulate the ovaries. The eggs are then collected during a procedure performed under sedation or anaesthesia.
"I was pretty much so bloated that I looked like I was six months pregnant," Salcedo says.
The 'egg count' test
Luke Grzeskowiak is a practitioner fellow at Flinders University's College of Medicine and Public Health and recently published a study analysing almost 1.4 million health records for Australian women.
The study found that anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) tests in Australian general practice increased more than 13-fold between 2011 and 2021. Women aged 30 to 34 recorded the sharpest increase, with testing rates rising almost 20-fold.
AMH is often marketed as a fertility test or "egg count" because it provides an indication of ovarian reserve — the number of eggs remaining in the ovaries.
"AMH testing can tell you about the quantity of eggs remaining, and the largest predictor of that is really just age-related. So, as we get older, there's a natural decline in egg count," Grzeskowiak tells SBS News.
The issue is it can't tell you anything about the quality, and it's really the quality that is the most important predictor of a potential good pregnancy outcome.
Grzeskowiak believes that rise in testing can be attributed in part to increased consumer marketing and ads about AMH.
"There's some interesting research that's published that's evaluated what's marketed around these tests, and what they find is a high level of misinformation around what AMH tests can and can't tell you and their usefulness or potential lack thereof," he says.
He acknowledges AMH testing can have a clinical role, including for people undergoing ART or cancer treatment and can also form part of the assessment of conditions such as polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome — formerly known as polycystic ovary syndrome — a hormonal condition that can affect the ovaries and reproductive health.
What happens to frozen eggs that aren't used?
As a clinician, Hunter says the rise in elective egg freezing also poses complex ethical questions around the storage and disposal of unused eggs.
We've got this unique irony where we're growing these banks of eggs, and at some point, there won't be enough room.
"So, we've got this opportunity then to talk to young women about, 'Well, you're not going to use them, would you consider donating them?'" she says.
"In that space, there are a lot of ethical and societal challenges: how do we compensate women who've frozen their eggs and paid storage every year for 10 years?"
The rules governing how long eggs, sperm (also called gametes) and embryos can be stored vary between Australian states and territories.
Some jurisdictions regulate storage and removal, including for donated gametes or embryos and in circumstances where a donor dies. Other jurisdictions, including the Northern Territory, do not have specific ART legislation.
"In Western Australia you can store them for 10 years and then beyond that we have to ask, 'Do you want to dispose of them ... do you want to donate them?'" Hunter says.
We're not having those discussions with young women who are egg freezing, and that's a problem.
In June last year, health ministers called for a rapid review into ART regulation and accreditation following concerns about "safety, oversight and transparency" in the sector. The review followed two high-profile cases in which embryos were mistakenly transferred.
In September 2025, the review released 10 recommended areas for reform, including changes to how ART services are accredited and regulated.
The Department of Health says the new national standards aim to better align with state-based legislation and regulation, including requirements for the storage and disposal of reproductive material.
A transition to the new scheme is expected by December 2028, with the current regulatory framework to remain in place until then.
In a statement to SBS News, the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) said it provides "ethical guidance for the use of ART in clinical practice and research" through its guidelines, which also set "out the ethical responsibilities of IVF clinics regarding stored gametes and embryos".
However, the NHMRC said the regulation of ART clinical practice is a matter for state and territory governments.
Meanwhile, for women like Salcedo, egg freezing continues to provide a sense of long-term security.
"I think when I get to 40, I'll probably decide if I want to choose to be a single mum and do that, but I'm leaving that decision until later.
"I think a lot of people start to lose hope, especially if you start to get closer to 40, but there are ways to achieve that."

The remains of military personnel killed in the battles of September 1939 were discovered at Holoskivske Cemetery. Tags, medallions, and personal belongings were also found.
At the old Holoskivske Cemetery in Lviv, exhumation research has been completed, during which the remains of 113 soldiers of the Polish Army and 17 Wehrmacht servicemen were discovered. The Ukrainian Institute of National Remembrance reported this, UNN writes.
Details
The work lasted from August 25 to September 28, 2026. It was carried out to exhume, examine and subsequently rebury the remains of Polish servicemen who died in September 1939.
In addition to 130 complete sets of remains, the researchers discovered separate disarticulated remains whose affiliation has not yet been established. According to preliminary estimates, they may belong to two more people.
Identification tags, medallions and personal belongings found
During the work, archaeologists also discovered soldiers’ identification tags, medallions, buttons and other uniform elements, personal belongings and shell casings.
The research was conducted by Ukrainian Memory LLC with the participation of specialists from the Polish Institute of National Remembrance and Wrocław Medical University. Search specialists and an anthropologist from the Lviv Regional Council’s municipal enterprise “Dolya” were also involved in the work.
In September 1939, battles took place in the area of what was then the village of Velyke Holosko between the Polish Army and the Wehrmacht. The fallen Polish servicemen were buried in a mass grave at the local cemetery. Signs of these burials were discovered during search operations back in 2019.
