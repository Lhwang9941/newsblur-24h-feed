Age-check firms want eSafety to dig into how social media giants enforce ban
Wed 29 Jul 2026 at 5:00am
In short:
Age-checking technology companies want Australia to use stronger investigative powers to verify if social media giants are implementing the under-16s ban effectively.
But legal and tech industry groups have warned the proposed information-gathering powers must be narrowed to avoid inadvertently capturing children or parents.
What's next?
A Senate inquiry will consider submissions and, in August, report on Labor's bill to expand eSafety's powers and raise penalties for systemic failures to enforce the social media age ban.
Companies who sell age-checking software want Australia to grant the eSafety Commissioner investigative powers to "test" if social media companies are using their technology properly.
The Age Verification Providers Association (AVPA) has backed Labor's bill to strengthen Australia's social media age laws and called on the federal government to go further with a mandatory audit of every platform captured by the under-16s ban.
The group warned social media giants could opt to wear fines, no matter how large, if it helped to sink the age laws.
Age assurance companies, which often sign confidentiality agreements with social media platforms, also asked for legal protections for providing information "in good faith" to an eSafety investigation.
Support was mixed in submissions to a Senate inquiry into Labor's proposal to double the maximum penalty to $99 million for tech companies that fail to take "reasonable steps" to enforce the age limit.
The bill would also allow eSafety to compel documents from social media platforms and third parties when investigating suspected systemic failures to uphold the ban.
Legal experts recommended Labor narrow the scope of these investigative powers to explicitly protect children and parents, while the tech sector opposed the larger fines.
Social media companies accused of wanting ban to fail
In a damning submission, the AVPA, which represents third-party age technology companies, warned big fines could still be seen as a "small price to pay" if it allowed Australia's ban to "wrongly" be portrayed as a failure.
Early research into the efficacy of the ban in preventing Australians aged under 16 from accessing the likes of TikTok, Snapchat, Facebook and Instagram has found the majority of young people were still able to log in to at least one platform.
Loading...AVPA said expanding the eSafety Commissioner's power so it could compel documents from tech companies was "necessary to test whether social media platforms have effectively configured the age assurance systems they claim to have implemented".
The group recommended Australia go further by having a "mandatory independent audit and certification requirement" for every social media platform captured by the ban.
"The expanded power will also reach our members … which are typically bound contractually by confidentiality obligations," the submission said.
"Most such agreements already permit disclosure that is required by law, so compliance with a valid notice should not expose suppliers to significant contractual risk."
Call to protect kids and parents in beefed-up law
The Law Council of Australia has made a submission flagging concerns the scope of the proposed section on information-gathering extended "beyond its intended operation".
"As drafted, the provision appears capable of applying to any person, including end-users who are children and their parents or guardians, where they possess relevant information or documents," the submission said.
The council recommended the bill be amended to ensure it "better reflects" the government's policy intentions and provides "certainty" that children were not "inadvertently" captured by the expanded information-gathering power.
"If the power is not intended to apply to children and their parents or guardians, its scope should be revised to ensure that it is appropriately targeted," it said.
Communications Minister Anika Wells said eSafety would not compel documents from children and parents because "everyday Australians" did not have evidence of "systemic non-compliance with the law by billion-dollar tech companies".
"Instead, these strengthened powers are intended to target third-party companies, such as age assurance technology providers and app-stores, where the eSafety Commissioner suspects they may hold evidence of systemic non-compliance," she said.
"We know social media companies are doing the bare minimum to comply with our social media minimum age law; that's why we are strengthening eSafety's powers and doubling fines for non-compliance."
Tech companies oppose tougher fines
Digital Industry Group Inc (DIGI), an industry group that advocates on behalf of the sector in Australia, has criticised the proposed bill, arguing the government had not tested if gaps in enforcement could be addressed in other ways.
It also opposed the doubling of the maximum penalty because, with no fines yet issued, it was not clear if there was a "link" between the dollar figure and the effectiveness of the age ban.
DIGI said the eSafety Commissioner already had an "extensive suite of investigative and enforcement powers" related to the social media age restrictions and argued the government should be clear about any "deficiency" the new bill was trying to remedy.
The group raised concerns about the new information and document-gathering powers being "very broad" noting they were not limited to app stores and age assurance providers.
"Australians should not have to rely on a regulator exercising restraint as the sole safeguard against the misuse of broad-ranging powers against individual citizens,"the submission said.
DIGI said any increased powers should be accompanied by greater transparency about how they were used.
"Powers of this breadth should be matched by clear public reporting on the types of services and individuals to whom notices are issued, and the basis on which the Commissioner decides to open an inquiry into a service or person," the submission said.
eSafety can't reach 'onus of proof' without documents
The eSafety Commissioner has backed the expanded powers as currently any investigation into tech giants suspected of failing to take "reasonable steps" to block under-16s from their social media platforms relies on "representations" from those same companies.
"Without access to primary documents relevant to compliance with the [social media minimum age] obligation, the Commissioner would likely be unable to discharge the onus of proof required in any civil penalty proceeding brought to the courts against a provider of a [platform]," eSafety said.
The commission said information and documents from third parties, such as age assurance providers, would be "instrumental" in determining whether the tech giants had taken sufficient steps to comply with Australia's laws.
In its submission, eSafety also argued the "full suite" of investigative powers available to it elsewhere in the Online Safety Act, such as the ability to "compel witnesses to give evidence on oath or affirmation", should apply uniformly.

'People are his kryptonite': Angus Taylor took a minute to figure parliament out
Wed 29 Jul 2026 at 5:00am
In the classic 1989 comedy When Harry Met Sally, one of the most iconic lines comes when Sally — played by Meg Ryan — is desperate for details about her ex's new girlfriend.
"What does she look like?" Sally asks her best friend Marie.
"Oh," drawls Marie, played by Carrie Fisher, dry as a martini.
"Thin. Pretty. Big t*ts. Your basic nightmare."
The horror of finding out that one's competitor is quite so richly endowed with natural advantages is a common and unifying human experience.
And when Angus Taylor in 2012 walked away from an absurdly successful and remunerative consulting career to contest the seat of Hume for the Liberal Party, the headlines didn't exactly say, "Rich. Handsome. Rhodes scholarship. Your basic nightmare." But they weren't all that far off.
On May 28 of that year, when Liberal leader Tony Abbott hastened to Goulburn to launch Taylor's campaign for the seat of Hume, The Australian newspaper chose especially empurpled prose to convey news of the event.
"Smart, rich, handsome: Angus Taylor made to stand," ran the headline on an article making much of Taylor's Rhodes scholarship and his personal wealth.
"As if that wasn't enough, he represented Australia in the 2009 triathlon world championships," the article continued, before noting that Taylor — a father of four — also had "the looks and the charm of a young Kennedy".
The Liberal Party certainly welcomed him as a great recruit. But some in the deeper end of the party's talent pool viewed this new entrant with interest grading into caution.
Triple-threat Taylor
The party room at the time already had two Rhodes scholars: Tony Abbott and Malcolm Turnbull.
And the propensity for Oxford blue-on-blue violence was well established between them.
Suspicion of the triple-threat Taylor existed across both camps; Abbott at various points fancied that Taylor was plotting with Turnbull, while Turnbull thought Taylor was aligned with Abbott.
In Taylor's own recollection, he was hilariously oblivious to this rippling matrix of suspicion.
In an interview for Kitchen Cabinet, Taylor recalls feeling a sense of overwhelm and reverence for the parliament when first he took his seat there.
"For me to sit in that chamber was just almost otherworldly," he says.
"It was … I'd never believed that I would sit in such an important place, in such a relevant place."
But within two years, the Abbott landslide had given way to horrendous instability within his government, as Malcolm Turnbull prepared to challenge Australia's 28th prime minister and become its 29th.
Angus Taylor had never experienced a leadership coup before. In the Liberal Party, these are crepuscular affairs, ungoverned by the cleaner factional lines that dictate loyalty in the Australian Labor Party.
"I realise now I got overtures," he says.
"It was not always obvious. So, people would sound you out to see if you had a grievance or you're unhappy with something… And I thought, 'that's very nice of them to ask!'
"One of the things that did surprise me when I came into politics was just how friendly people were. They were very friendly. Some of that friendliness was genuine, some of it was motivated, and I knew that, but of course, at the time, you're still trying to work out which one is which, and there was an element of that going on."
It took him a while, he says, to figure it all out.
'I didn't come up through the Young Liberals'
"I didn't come up through the Young Liberals or anything like that. I wasn't a staffer. So it was coming in pretty cold in a lot of ways."
Two weeks after becoming prime minister, Turnbull published the names of the colleagues who would join his first Cabinet, and Taylor's wasn't on it.
The deposed Abbott rang Taylor to commiserate, observing with characteristic joviality that at least he now was convinced Taylor was not among the plotters.
"Which I wasn't," recalls Taylor, drily. "So yeah, that was pretty much the sum of it."
This seems to form something of a pattern for Taylor. People develop assumptions about him, and he doesn't seem to mind overly. Or — in some cases — even notice.
To the Labor Party, Taylor is an over-promoted rich boy and a bungler, "born with a silver foot in his mouth", a Keatingesque assessment coined by Treasurer Jim Chalmers.
On social media, he's still lampooned for a Facebook blooper in the 2019 election campaign, in which "Angus Taylor MP" commented admiringly on his own post announcing a new commuter car park: "Fantastic. Great move. Well done Angus." It seemed fairly clear to any observer familiar with the functioning of electorate offices that the most likely explanation was an inexperienced staffer forgetting to log out of the boss's account and into their own before administering praise.
But Taylor — who has since made an effort to embrace the gag, going so far as to produce mugs and stubbie holders featuring the infamous meme — clammed up about the precise circumstances for so long that it began to seem possible that the error was, in fact, his.
"No. No, it wasn't," he clarifies. "But that fact that it wasn't is neither here nor there. It was my account. I'm accountable for it. And I'm gonna wear it."
A preference for projects not people
To some colleagues within the Liberal party room, he's a conundrum; equipped with a nimble brain for systems, policy, technology, but less capable of reading tone.
Malcolm Turnbull (who of course cannot be counted as an unbiased observer on his fellow Oxford alumnus) delivered on ABC TV — on the day of Angus Taylor's elevation to the leadership — the most savage barb of all, saying Taylor was described by people he knew as "the best-qualified idiot they've ever met".
This is — while a tremendously entertaining line — unfair.
The reason Angus Taylor made such a success of management consulting was his ability to step into a new industry or company, assess its structures and systems, and figure out a way to make it work more profitably.
His thesis at Oxford was on beer pricing: "The Competitive Effects of Vertical Integration and Exclusive Purchasing: An Application and Extension of Theory to the UK Brewing Industry."
One of his early triumphs — as a junior burger at consulting giant McKinsey — was his assignment to assist the divided New Zealand dairy industry to establish a better system for pricing milk. He assisted 10,000 sometimes cantankerous dairy farmers to form Fonterra, now responsible for around a third of the world's dairy exports.
Apart from a clear interest in the price points for various popular liquids, Taylor is most enthusiastic when talking about projects and systems.
He went into politics, he says, because he loved being a part of building things and wanted to do so on a national level. His interest in systems and innovation is evident.
"People are his kryptonite," says one colleague, not unkindly. Even this feels a bit unfair: Taylor has an evident and genuine enthusiasm for people who create new businesses, who build things, who defy expectations.
When asked who he admires most, he nominates not Robert Menzies or John Howard but his maternal grandfather, William Hudson, the commissioner of the Snowy Hydro-electric Scheme, who brought a potentially explosive mix of postwar immigrants together in the late 1940s to build one of Australia's most remarkable pieces of civil infrastructure.
But in 2026, Hudson's grandson finds himself in charge of a crumbling project; an organisation that consists of people who don't seem to be building anything, and certainly not together. The Liberal Party — an organisation arguably more in need of a McKinsey-style corporate enema than pretty much any other — is nearing a state of ungovernability, and there's not terribly much its federal leader can do.
The party's structure is designed to give state divisions significant independence, preventing the kind of centralised command-and-control operation Labor displayed in its tautly-managed national conference this week.
The factional warfare in some hot spots (Victoria, NSW, South Australia) is starting to resemble the tobacco wars. In Taylor's home state of NSW, his own right-wing grouping is enmeshed in an ICAC inquiry — Operation Rosny — which has made no allegations against Taylor, but is singeing party operatives close to him. None of this is building or producing anything.
Angus Taylor may be a systems enthusiast, but his latest management consultancy challenge is an organisation that is by turn setting itself on fire or helplessly picking itself to bits.
It's when articulating social or semantic concepts ("What is a monoculture?") that he seems to struggle. Possibly because he thinks such rhetorical exercises a waste of time? And yet, he is asked to comment on culture war grenades launched by Pauline Hanson (or by his own party president Tony Abbott) more regularly than he gets to talk about his preferred territory, like the indexation of income tax brackets — the significant policy he announced in his budget reply speech in May.
LoadingThe One Nation problem
Angus Taylor is not the first political leader to marry an extremely smart woman.
It is, however, unusual to find a prime ministerial contender whose wife writes a regular political column. Louise Clegg is a barrister who met Angus Taylor at law school; they became a couple while Taylor was still in plaster from a near-catastophic fall off a balcony at Sydney University in which the 22-year-old — somewhat intoxicated and significantly wracked with grief over the recent death of his mother — broke his back.
Clegg, who is possessed of a quick brain and a deep interest in conservative politics, pens a regular column in The Spectator. It would be an error to conflate her views always with those of her spouse, but the glimpses we get of him periodically in her writing are instructive.
A fortnight ago, for instance, Clegg wrote an item canvassing the varied responses to One Nation within the Coalition, ranging from party president Abbott's openness to some sort of deal with the resurgent protest party to Andrew Hastie's Shakespearean preference to die — if necessary — a bloody death on this political battlefield.
Where does Taylor stand on this spectrum? If you ask him, it's clear that the rhetoric of this moment exhausts him. He wants to lock down migration rates. But he speaks with genuine reverence of his grandfather, one of whose great strengths was the ability to bring people who did not share a language — who in some cases had been on different sides of a brutal conflict just months earlier — and unite them in pursuit of a grand project, a new home, a safe place.
And in the Kitchen Cabinet interview, he voices his strongest defence yet of "incredible" Australian Muslims.
Clegg, in her analysis, is generous about Abbott's response (it derived from "genuine respect" for Hanson, she said), and also about Hastie's ("Given One Nation declared war on him first, that seems a fair response too").
But her description of her husband's response to these twin outliers on his own side is instructive; in her telling, Taylor heard the flights of rhetoric, but chose another way.
"The always patient Angus did something much less exciting — and very Angus," she wrote.
"He reached for the calculator. While thoroughly dismantling Labor's economic management, he observed that One Nation's policies simply did not add up."

White House aide who made $100K betting on Trump speeches is out of his government job
President Donald Trump’s longtime teleprompter operator no longer works in government after he was placed on unpaid leave over alleged insider trading
- Bookmark
- CommentsGo to comments
Donald Trump’s longtime teleprompter operator no longer works for the government after federal investigators determined he made tens of thousands of dollars by placing bets on the president’s speeches on the prediction market Kalshi.
Gabriel Perez, who has traveled and worked alongside Trump for roughly a decade, “no longer works in the federal government,” a White House official told The Independent. The official would not tell The Independent whether he was fired or if he resigned.
Perez was among the highest-paid aides in Trump’s second administration, earning a salary of $175,000 as “deputy assistant to the president and technical advisor,” according to a White House report submitted to Congress.
He was placed on unpaid administrative leave earlier this month following reports that Kalshi referred an internal probe to the Commodity Futures Trading Commission for an investigation into insider trading. Perez allegedly used his knowledge of the president’s speeches to place bets on whether Trump would say specific words or phrases to rake in more than $100,000 in Kalshi winnings.
White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt told reporters earlier this month that Trump personally made the decision to put Perez on unpaid leave. She called the allegations “deeply unfortunate and frankly a disgrace.”
Perez, who has worked for Trump since his 2016 presidential campaign, is among a handful of top aides who sees the president’s remarks before they are delivered. “I have a guy, Gabe,” Trump said during a campaign event in Reno, Nevada in 2024. “He’s excellent. I’ve had some real bad ones, but I have Gabe.”
According to ABC News, which first reported the investigation, Perez placed bets on Trump’s State of the Union address in February and more than a dozen other events over a three-month period, including a speech at the World Economic Forum in Davos, Switzerland, and a Medal of Honor ceremony.
Kalshi’s surveillance team “promptly flagged and referred these trades” to federal investigators and the company is “cooperating and assisting regulators,” Kalshi's head of enforcement, Bobby DeNault, said in a statement earlier this month.
“I think this played out exactly how it should,” Leavitt told reporters. “Kalshi notified the [the commission] of suspicious activity. They investigated that, identified the individual, and now he will no longer be working at the White House.”
Federal prosecutors in Manhattan reportedly declined to open a criminal investigation, though regulators have allegedly reached a settlement deal with Perez that would require him to return his profits and refrain from making similar bets.
Critics fear that government employees’ access to sensitive, nonpublic information can be used to move global markets and potentially shape geopolitics, made all the easier with prediction market websites.
Lawmakers and several state legislatures are considering measures to restrict government officials’ use of websites such as Polymarket and Kalshi, and the White House has warned its staff against similar transactions after a surge of suspicious trading related to the U.S. war with Iran.
Federal ethics rules already bar employees from gambling on government property, but the White House Management Office sent a memo to all White House staff in March to warn against using nonpublic information for private gain.
That warning followed a flurry of suspiciously well-timed activity on betting websites, including a U.S. Army soldier accused of using classified information about ousted Venezuelan leader Nicolas Maduro’s capture to make more than $400,000 on Polymarket.
Gannon Ken Van Dyke faces three counts of violating the Commodity Exchange Act, each of which carries a maximum sentence of 10 years in prison. He also faces one count of wire fraud and one count of an unlawful monetary transaction.
Van Dyke allegedly used his access to classified information in a series of bets waging more than $33,000 on the prediction market before Trump announced Maduro’s capture in January, according to federal prosecutors.
In April, Kalshi announced three congressional candidates wagered on the outcome of their own elections. The company fined and suspended Ezekiel Enriquez, who ran in a Republican primary for a House seat in Texas; Matt Klein, a Democratic state senator running for a House seat in Minnesota; and Mark Moran, who is running as an independent in Virginia's Senate race.
The president’s social media platform Truth Social, however, has announced plans to launch its own prediction market.
Trump has said the U.S. would be “left out in the cold” if the government prevents companies such as Kalshi and Polymarket from operating.
“The whole world, unfortunately, has become somewhat of a casino, and you look at what’s going on all over the world in Europe and every place they’re doing these betting things,” he told reporters in April. “I was never much in favor of it. I don't like it conceptually, but it is what it is.”
