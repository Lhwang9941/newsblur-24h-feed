Some fish swimming in the Great Lakes could be upward of 400 years old, new study finds
Previous research had put the ‘living dinosaur’ fish’s life expectancy at 150 years
- Bookmark
- CommentsGo to comments
Michigan lake sturgeon were already known as “living dinosaurs” - but they may be a lot older than you might think, according to a new study from Michigan and Wisconsin officials and universities.
Some of the spiny, whiskered females may live for as long as 427 years, the groups found, following an analysis of populations in the Great Lakes.
That’s nearly as long as the lifespan of Greenland sharks. The longest-lived vertebrates on Earth, Greenland sharks can live for up to 512 years while swimming through the dark, frigid depths of the Atlantic Ocean.
And the researchers’ estimate is also hundreds of years longer than researchers previously believed.
Past research capped the age of lake sturgeons - which have been on the fossil record for 150 million years - at 150.
To reach these conclusions, the researchers didn’t actually catch a fish that was that old. Instead, they did some math using 44 years of data on five different populations of lake sturgeon.
The team captured and measured the fish, determined their sex, and marked and released them. Then they would do it again in future years, measuring the growth of fish they had already caught.
Unlike humans, fish continue to get bigger as they age.
Lake sturgeon are already one of the largest freshwater fish in North America, reaching up to 200-300 pounds, according to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.
And some sturgeon can grow bigger than others in environments with more prey, such as small fish and mollusks that they suck up from lake bottoms using their large, extendable mouth.
Using these measurements, the researchers could estimate how low the fish lived and what their growth rate was.
“Simply stated, we used this data to estimate lake sturgeon longevity by asking ‘If a lake sturgeon grows X centimeters per year, then how many years does it take for a lake sturgeon to get to a particular size?’” the department explained in a release.
They related sturgeon growth rate to the size of the fish, finding that males that were 63 inches long could live between 90-279 years and larger 71-inch females could live between 99-427 years.
“We've concluded that sturgeon live far longer lives than initially thought based on looking at growth rings and fin ray cross sections," Edward Baker, manager of the Michigan Department of Natural Resources' Marquette Research Station, told News Channel 3.
Baker noted that less than one percent of the historic population of over two million lake sturgeon remain in the Great Lakes.
Understanding how long these fish live is important for the conservation of their species and habitats, the department says.
Lake sturgeon mature later than other fish and females may not reproduce until they are 15-25 years old. That means strict protections for the species are crucial to ensure their population can rebound.
The sturgeon were nearly wiped out by fishermen in the late 1800s, the station noted. There are still harvests allowed - but last year’s was only six, the department said.
"It sort of reinforces the need to be very restrictive in what we allow in terms of harvesting," Baker said. "It's not a fish that we just allow anybody to go out and catch as many as they want."
But the species is also considered threatened by human pollution, infrastructure and climate change, according to the Center for Biological Diversity.
The non-profit said it was suing the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service earlier this year after Endangered Species Act protections were denied for lake sturgeon in 2024.
President Donald Trump recently said he’d move to roll back protections under the 1973 law.

Mexico and Brazil agreed to deepen cooperation in trade, energy and multilateral affairs during a high-level meeting in Brasilia, as both countries seek closer coordination amid growing regional and global tensions.
Mexican Foreign Minister Roberto Velasco met his Brazilian counterpart, Mauro Vieira, on Thursday during the sixth Mexico-Brazil Binational Commission, before holding talks with Brazilian President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva, who renewed his invitation for Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum to visit Brazil.
The two governments said they would continue to expand cooperation in strategic sectors, including trade, energy, science, technology and innovation, while strengthening coordination in international forums.
They also reiterated their support for former Chilean President Michelle Bachelet's candidacy to become the next UN chief,
Energy cooperation remained a central topic of the discussions, with both advancing plans for closer collaboration between Mexico's state-owned oil company Pemex and Brazil's Petrobras in areas including hydrocarbon exploration and biofuels.
The meeting came amid growing tensions between Brazil and the US following disputes about tariffs and diplomatic relations. Mexico has sought to diversify its international partnerships without undermining its close economic relationship with Washington.
Days before the meeting, Brazil denied visas to two US State Department envoys who had planned to meet with electoral authorities, accusing them of attempting to undermine confidence in the country's electoral system.
The diplomatic dispute followed an escalating trade conflict after the Trump administration imposed a 25% tariff on Brazilian goods earlier this summer, following a 50% tariff introduced last year.
The meeting took place against the backdrop of those growing tensions, as Mexico and Brazil seek to expand bilateral cooperation and coordination on regional and multilateral issues.
Mexico and Brazil have strengthened ties in recent months through regular high-level contacts between Sheinbaum and Lula. In June, the two leaders reaffirmed their commitment to multilateralism, international law and the principle of non-interference, while instructing their foreign ministries to prepare for the sixth Binational Commission meeting.
As Latin America's two largest economies, Brazil and Mexico have expanded cooperation in recent years through joint initiatives on trade, energy, investment and regional integration.

New Seventh Air Force Chief Faces Tough China, North Korea Air Mission

The incoming head of the Seventh Air Force will face an intense mission while relying on fourth-generation fighters to stave off looming threats from North Korea and China.
Recently promoted Lt. Gen. David G. Shoemaker took command of the pivotal post during a ceremony held Aug. 7 at Osan Air Base, South Korea, where the Seventh’s headquarters is located.
As head of the Seventh Air Force, Shoemaker will oversee the 51st Fighter Wing at Osan and the 8th Fighter Wing at Kunsan, which fly F-16s alongside South Korean allies’ F-35s. The Air Force is also incorporating lessons from a multiyear series of “Super Squadron” experiments in which F-16s were consolidated from Kunsan to Osan to see whether larger squadrons would more efficiently launch and maintain the jets.
Shoemaker previously served as director of operations for Air Combat Command. He is an experienced F-16 pilot with more than 3,900 flight hours, and has commanded units including the 421st Fighter Squadron, also known as the “Black Widows,” and the 8th Fighter Wing, nicknamed the “Wolf Pack.”
He initially served as an air surveillance officer following his 1994 commission from the Air Force Academy.
The Seventh Air Force currently relies on rotational deployments for U.S. fifth-generation aircraft such as the F-35 Lightning II and F-22 Raptor. F-35s most recently deployed to the peninsula in August 2025 for the Ulchi Freedom Shield exercise, in which 10 Joint Strike Fighters took part. F-22s from the 19th and 199th Expeditionary Fighter Squadrons also practiced dogfights with Republic of Korea F-35s in 2024.
Korea and China
That paucity of fifth-generation aircraft and reliance on rotations are problems for advanced U.S. airpower in more than just Korea.
Kadena Air Base, Japan, is a year behind schedule to receive its contingent of F-15EXs, an updated version of the F-15 that is slated to arrive in 2027, Secretary of the Air Force Troy E. Meink said in May.
Misawa Air Base, Japan, received its first four permanent F-35s in March and continues to rely upon the F-16 for its mission set.
A more permanent presence of advanced aircraft is needed to foil the military ambitions of both North Korea and, more broadly, China, said retired Air Force Col. John “JV” Venable, senior resident fellow at the Mitchell Institute for Aerospace Studies.
“Korea is one of two nations where US fighters can be permanently stationed within the first island chain,” said Venable, a former F-16 pilot and commander. “Fighters based in either Korea or Japan could sortie to thwart a surprise move by either China or North Korea as soon as it happens.”
He noted that the next-closest locations outside that island chain are Hawaii and Alaska, which require at least a day to mobilize and deploy to the region before the fight.
Another Mitchell expert, retired Navy Cmdr. J. Michael Dahm, senior fellow for Aerospace and China Studies, noted that the mere presence of additional fighter aircraft has an effect.
“Just having more forces in theater makes them available to the PACAF commander on short notice, even if they’re not fighting from the peninsula,” Dahm said, in a nod to the wider Agile Combat Employment concept the Air Force is incorporating in the Pacific region.
Agile Combat Employment is a strategy for quickly dispersing aircraft and personnel across multiple locations to avoid enemy strikes.
“If they deployed out of Korea and were flying out of the Philippines or somewhere else in Southeast Asia, they could get there in a relatively short period of time, and that could have a deterrent effect,” Dahm continued. “China would need to consider the U.S. presence in theater beyond Okinawa, Yokota, or Guam.”
Super Squadron
The Super Squadron experiment worked in two phases. The first phase in 2024 saw a batch of nine F-16s and 150 Airmen move from Kunsan Air Base, home of the 8th Fighter Wing and its 80th and 35th Fighter Squadrons, to Osan Air Base, home of the 51st Fighter Wing, about 50 miles from the North Korean border.
Then, in the second phase, starting in October 2025, another 22 F-16s moved from Kunsan to Osan, bringing the total to 31 Kunsan fighters alongside the Osan F-16 squadrons.
The experiment, dubbed “a force optimization” test to generate more combat power and operate more efficiently, examined whether a larger unit would be more efficient at employing and maintaining the fighters, Capt. Bryce Hughe said in a July 2025 release.
The Super Squadron experiment had been slated to run until October, but in April, Pacific Air Forces commander Gen. Kevin Schneider concluded it early. The Air Force said lessons from the test “are informing readiness enhancements across PACAF and USAF F-16 enterprise.”
The F-16s returned to their pre-existing three-squadron structure at both Osan and Kunsan.
The Super Squadron experiment was separate from two F-16s that moved permanently from Misawa Air Base, Japan to Osan in June 2025. Long-term plans call for all of the F-16s at Misawa to move to Korea as F-35s arrive in Japan.
The Misawa F-16s, which replaced legacy F-15C/Ds and A-10s under Osan’s 51st Fighter Wing, feature upgraded features such as a new active electronically scanned array radar.
