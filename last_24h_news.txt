From Tehran’s walls to the world: Iran’s visual propaganda as an art of war (2/2)
Tehran at War (Part 2/2). In Iran, the war with the United States is also fought through enormous, state-sponsored posters. Murals transform walls into billboards advertising the official narrative, designed to carry the government's voice far beyond its borders. FRANCE 24 examines this visual strategy, shaped during the Islamic Revolution and supercharged by artificial intelligence.
A towering image of Donald Trump with his mouth sewn shut looms over Valiasr Avenue, one of Tehran’s busiest thoroughfares. The US president’s mouth is covered by a blue veil in the shape of the Strait of Hormuz.
Beside his image, a slogan in Persian – which graphic designers have made sure to also translate into English reads: "At the breaking point."
The billboard-sized poster was installed on May 2, when the war was in full swing and negotiations over the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz had stalled. This maritime passage, which Iran seeks to control – and through which nearly 25% of the world's oil is transported, is displayed here as a weapon capable of silencing Washington.

Since the start of the war against the United States and Israel, these colourful murals have been displayed across the capital.
Very few images make it out of the country. Journalists in Iran are subject to intense scrutiny, and the internet is still largely cut off for the public, especially during the first few months of the war.
However, Iran’s leaders permit and even encourage news agencies to photograph and officially circulate posters like the one where Trump’s mouth is sewn shut. The image was widely shared by international media on social media.
Other posters include a mural of Iranian drones shattering the Star of David and protesters chanting "Death to America".
Another banner depicts the lineage of religious leaders standing together on a field of flowers: the founder of the Islamic Republic, Ruhollah Khomeini, stands beside Ali Khamenei – killed in the war's initial strikes on February 28 – who hands the Iranian flag to his successor and son, Mojtaba Khamenei.
The walls and buildings of Tehran have become canvases for state propaganda. The messaging is not just targeted at Iranian citizens – it’s also intended to speak to Iran’s foes.
A tradition of visual messaging
While many of these monumental wartime billboards are now designed to be shared by non-Iranians outside of its borders, they are part of a long tradition of visual propaganda within the country.
For Iranians, they are a fixture of the urban landscape. Since the 1979 Islamic Revolution, walls have served as a "space for political expression", explains Sepideh Parsapajouh, an anthropologist and researcher at the French National Centre for Scientific Research (CNRS).
Large-scale murals constantly reinforce the narrative of the values upheld by the Islamic Republic – be it to eulogise martyrs of the Iran-Iraq War (1980–1988) and display revolutionary slogans, or express anti-American sentiment and hostility towards Israel. “The city thus becomes a vast narrative space," the researcher explained.
However, this culture of visual messaging predates the current regime by a long time. According to Parsapajouh, this method of storytelling through images is rooted in an older tradition called “pardeh-khâni” – a practice in which a storyteller would share religious or epic tales in front of a massive painted canvas depicting various scenes from the story.
Industrial-scale propaganda
"At the start of the Iran-Iraq War, many of the paintings were created by committed artists, art students, and sometimes talented amateurs," said Parsapajouh, adding that there were also community-driven local initiatives.
“Sometimes, the families of martyrs made requests. Artists painted portraits of young men who had died on the walls of their homes, as gifts for their mothers. There was a deep personal and emotional dimension to it."

But this artistic and emotional dimension gradually faded away. "It became institutionalised very quickly. The murals turned into actual state-commissioned projects," she noted.
Today, the process is entirely centralised. The massive billboards that have replaced hand-painted murals are commissioned directly by government bodies. Some designs are produced by the "House of Islamic Revolution Designers", a semi-governmental arts and media organisation. The installation of the designs is managed by several institutions, including the Islamic Development Organisation and the Tehran Municipality’s Beautification Bureau.
There’s been a change in medium as well. Delicately painted murals have given way to giant printed banners that can be installed or replaced in a matter of hours – allowing the Iranian regime to adapt its visual messaging in real time.
'The Lord of the Rings' treatment
AI has also played a major role in accelerating the speed with which these often-theatrical images are rendered, and it has also affected the actual content of the posters.

"Compositions today are far more sophisticated. They borrow the visual language of advertising, cinema, and blockbuster movie posters," observed Parsapajouh.
Some even draw on Western pop culture. One notable example, displayed on the facade of a building in Fatemi Square in east-central Tehran, caught the attention of several Western media outlets because it referenced fantasy saga “The Lord of the Rings”.
A programme on popular Franco-German channel Arte called “Le Dessous des Images” ("Behind the Image") highlights this mural, which depicts two colossal statues standing on either side of a narrow waterway symbolising the Strait of Hormuz. Their arms are outstretched in a signal to block aircraft carriers and warships from passing through.
The statue on the left is Alireza Tangsiri, the former commander-in-chief of the Revolutionary Guard Navy who was killed on March 26, 2026, in a bombing by Israel. On the right is Rais Ali Delvari, an Iranian national hero renowned for organising local resistance against British forces in the Tangestan region in 1915.
The image bears an undeniable resemblance to the Argonath – the "Gates of Kings" from “The Lord of the Rings” franchise where two enormous statues flanking a narrow waterway symbolically marked the entrance to the Kingdom of Gondor and warn off any enemies trying to enter, in the world of Tolkien.
Such depictions "effectively immortalsze political figures by casting them as heroes", said Parsapajouh.
Politicians, soldiers, and scientists immortalized
Tehran’s walls are plastered with portraits of figures such as former Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei or members of the Revolutionary Guards killed in Israeli-American air strikes. They appear alongside other iconic figures of the Islamic Republic, such as General Qassem Soleimani – the former commander of the Quds Force – who was killed in a US strike in Iraq in 2020.
"The hero may die, but he becomes immortal through this narrative. At Karbala, Imam Hussein died a martyr, yet his sacrifice became a memory that is constantly being renewed through his story. Contemporary murals follow this exact logic. The soldier, politician, or scientist who has been killed is not merely depicted as someone who has died; they become a hero, a martyr, and a character in a national and state story that is still being written," explained Parsapajouh.
"Tehran’s walls are not just a disconnected succession of images; together, they form a genuine visual text. Moving through the city, one passes from one story to another, from one martyr to the next, from one historical episode to another. The city constantly tells the story of its national history – a story told by those in power."
Omnipresence doesn’t guarantee support
Propaganda in Tehran is also tailored to where it will be displayed. For example, murals celebrating anti-imperialism are displayed near the former US embassy. The images at Palestine Square frequently address their messaging directly to Israel; some are even translated into Hebrew.
Valiasr Square hosts the most monumental posters and Revolution Square remains one of the regime's primary sites for visual communication with its citizens.
All these images are posted throughout the capital and seen daily by thousands of its residents.

However, the omnipresence of these murals does not guarantee public support. "Most Tehran residents have lived with these images for decades. They are part of the urban landscape. Most passersby barely pay attention to them anymore. Others may have an emotional response, and some interpret them critically," observed Parsapajouh, stressing that public imagery doesn’t necessarily reflect public opinion.
Regardless, "the city remains a space for dialogue", she noted, and some Tehranis do not hesitate to reclaim the walls wherever there’s a gap to fill.
"Large, monumental walls are heavily controlled by institutions, but there are more discreet forms of expression like graffiti, inscriptions, subversive reworkings, and even acts of erasure. These are far less visible – often fleeting – yet they are photographed too and shared on social media."
These forms of expression may be understated and often erased quickly, but they allow Tehranis to reclaim the narrative of their home and life in the capital.

US President Donald Trump has been highly complementary of de la Espriella, calling him a "smart, strong, and tough leader."
The US State Department said on Friday that it planned to announce a $1 billion security package for new Colombian President Abelardo de la Espriella's right-wing administration.
In a press release, the office of the spokesperson for the department said the package aimed to help Washington and Bogotá achieve "our shared goals."
De la Espriella was sworn in as president on Friday in a ceremony attended by a US delegation that included acting attorney general Todd Blanche.
"The delegation demonstrates U.S. commitment to reinvigorating the U.S.-Colombia relationship and deepening of our shared security and economic partnership," the State Department said.
US President Donald Trump has been highly complimentary of de la Espriella, calling him a "smart, strong, and tough leader" that he predicted would be "tremendously successful" as president of Colombia and as a ballast for the right in the South American nation. Trump previously said de la Espriella would have the "total support and strength of the United States behind him" in office.
On Friday, Washington said Colombia had also now joined the "Shield of the Americas," an alliance of governments stretching from the US across Central and South America that has vowed to fight narcotrafficking groups.
"The Trump Administration welcomes Colombia joining the Shield of the Americas and stands ready to support the de la Espriella Administration’s efforts to defeat the vicious narcoterrorist and criminal organizations that threaten U.S., Colombian, and hemispheric security and economic prosperity," the State Department said.
The US has become increasingly involved in Latin America during Trump's second term in the White House, carrying out strikes on alleged drug-trafficking boats coming from the region and launching an operation to capture former Venezuelan leader Nicolás Maduro. Maduro has since been held in custody in New York on charges including narco-terrorism and drug trafficking - to which he has pleaded not guilty.
