Trump’s self-deportation program sees hundreds of thousands of migrants leave US
- Project Homecoming, a U.S. program offering cash incentives and free flights, has led to over 200,000 migrants agreeing to "self-deport," with approximately half deciding while in detention.
- The program, which began in May 2025, initially had low participation but was significantly expanded following pressure from White House officials, including Stephen Miller.
- Cash incentives for migrants to leave the U.S. were increased from $1,000 to $2,600, with a temporary peak of $3,000 offered during a 10-day period in December.
- A staffer from the former Department of Government Accountability reportedly helped boost participation by introducing Project Homecoming case managers into migrant detention centers.
- The nonpartisan Migration Policy Institute expressed skepticism about the program's effectiveness, citing a lack of data transparency and historical evidence that similar "pay-to-go" initiatives have had limited success.

US posts photo of coastguard working with Taiwan after Beijing starts patrols
De facto American embassy says the two sides are coordinating on search, rescue, disaster relief and maritime law enforcement
The American Institute in Taiwan (AIT) – the de facto US embassy in Taipei – posted the undated image on social media on Tuesday, saying the two sides were coordinating on search, rescue, disaster relief and maritime law enforcement.
“The United States appreciates Taiwan’s partnership in maritime security and safety, and supports Taiwan’s meaningful contributions on global issues including maritime law enforcement exchange and international cooperation,” the AIT said.
The statement made no mention of Beijing, but analysts said the timing underscored Washington’s support for Taiwan.
The US released the photo weeks after Beijing announced that its coastguard had begun “routine law enforcement patrols” in waters east of Taiwan.

AI competition heats up as US targets Southeast Asia’s public sector
Washington’s launch of AI Spark is seen as a move to counter Chinese AI models that have made inroads in Southeast Asia, analysts say
However, the initiative is unlikely to force Southeast Asia to choose between AI Spark and the Chinese model, as the region is expected to integrate US know-how with Chinese and local systems to avoid dependence on any single provider, according to analysts.
On July 22, Washington said in a statement that AI Spark would support American AI exports to Southeast Asia as part of an overarching plan to advance “technologies of the future”.
The announcement came in the middle of US Secretary of State Marco Rubio’s visit to Manila during a meeting of Asean foreign ministers last week, where he said Washington was delivering US$2.5 billion in strategic investments in the region.
At a virtual press briefing on Friday, US Ambassador Kevin Kim said that while the investments would span various categories, there was an emphasis on advancing technologies in Southeast Asia given its interest in AI, data centres and other areas.
“AI Spark … will provide financial and technical support to Southeast Asian governments interested in deploying the US’ AI stack for public sector applications,” Kim added.
