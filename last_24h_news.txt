Truck explodes outside Colombia police base, injuring 11 officers and killing a dog
Defence Minister Pedro Sanchez blamed the National Liberation Army rebel group for the attack and offered a US$60,000 reward for information
Colombia’s government on Saturday blamed the nation’s largest remaining rebel group for an explosion outside a police base that injured 11 officers and killed a police dog.
The attack took place early Saturday in Cucuta, a city along Colombia’s border with Venezuela, where drug gangs and rebel groups have long fought for the control of smuggling routes.
General William Rincon, the director of Colombia’s police, said the blast came from a truck that had been left outside a building that serves as the headquarters for police in Norte de Santander Province. The predawn explosion damaged much of the building’s roofing and shattered its windows.
“This was a vile terrorist attack,” Rincon said. “We will not rest until we find those who are responsible and have them prosecuted.”
In a message on social media, Colombian Defence Minister Pedro Sanchez blamed the National Liberation Army rebel group for the attack, and offered a US$60,000 reward for information leading to those behind the car bombing.
The attack comes as Colombia prepares to inaugurate a new president. Next Friday, Abelardo de la Espriella, an ultraconservative who has promised to cancel peace talks with the nation’s remaining rebel groups, will be sworn into office in Cali, another Colombian city where rebel groups have staged attacks recently.
The National Liberation Army, or ELN, has been fighting Colombia’s government for decades, and is believed to have 6,000 fighters operating in Colombia and neighbouring Venezuela.

How people across the country, from Tasmania to Broken Hill, are finding solutions to housing crisis
Sun 2 Aug 2026 at 4:41am
At 69, Kym Lardner has teamed up with five other pensioners hunting for a big house to rent and share.
When they find one, they intend to have a hoot.
Louise Southerden lives in a tiny house on land she rents for a fraction of what she would be paying for a granny flat in a big city.
For the past 20 years, Madeleine Challender has lived and worked in remote towns across the country, where she has received housing and stamp duty relief.
Kym, Louise and Madeleine span the 45 to 70 years age group. They each have different life stories but all three have chosen to think outside the box to deal with the housing crisis currently enveloping Australia.
Louise and Madeleine have found their solutions. Kym is on his way.
It's not as rosy for many others, priced out of the rental or real estate markets by rising costs of living and for whom the situation is dire.
A far-reaching crisis
Among the many issues raised by people taking part in the ABC Your Say project, housing is the most common theme for all age groups and in all parts of the country.
People across Australia have told ABC Your say that the housing crisis is impacting the way they live, how they connect with family and friends, and they believe it's eroding social cohesion.
The effects are far-reaching and go well beyond the fact stable housing is financially out of reach.
An interim report from the Productivity Commission into housing supply regulation, is blunt: Australia does not build enough homes in the areas where people want to live, making housing unaffordable in many places; land-use controls prevent or limit housing being built; slow and complex approvals delay new housing.
The report notes that in the past two decades property prices have grown more swiftly than wages — it takes 11 years for the average household to save for a 20 per cent deposit, compared to eight years in 2005.
Pensioner revives the share house
The commission says people need stable housing to flourish — for safety, security, access to employment, education and social connection.
Social connection is one thing that retired Tasmanian artist Kym Lardner wants — as well as a roof over his head.
When Mr Lardner couldn't find anywhere suitable and affordable to rent, often feeling his pensioner status was a barrier, he took matters into his own hands.
He posted on social media looking for other pensioners in the same boat to join him to pay $200 a week each to rent a six-bedroom home he had seen advertised.
"I was overwhelmed by responses," Mr Lardner said.
There are now five pensioners who have banded together with him hunting for a share house to rent in their twilight years.
"Three of my pensioners are over 80. I am the baby of the group. I am 69. One is 85. One man has one month left to live where he is living and then he is out on his ear."
Every day, Mr Lardner sits down with his laptop, searching for houses to rent. He is excited about the prospect of the band of six flatmates finding their perfect home together.
The benefits of outback living
Madeleine Challender and her wife, Kelly Mottau, a truck driver, live in Broken Hill in the far west of New South Wales. They share a four-bedroom home they bought earlier this year for $405,000. And they love it.
Ms Challender is an art teacher and has spent more than 20 years living and working in remote towns across New South Wales, the Northern Territory, Queensland and Western Australia.
The benefits, like free or 90 per cent subsidised accommodation and remote teacher incentive payments, added up.
She says there are incredible opportunities in regional and remote Australia.
In Broken Hill, she received $10,000 worth of stamp duty relief, a $20,000 housing bonus and a $20,000 recruitment bonus "because no-one applied for the art teacher job".
"In a remote town the people care for each other because that's all you've got and there are sometimes challenging situations but in every town I've always made one really good friend," Ms Challender said.
"Younger people may not realise these opportunities exist because all they hear on the news is catastrophising about the housing crisis."
Ms Mottau had an ambition to drive trucks and became a FIFO worker in Western Australia. She said it had been an "amazing journey", making good money without any expenses and later, receiving residential bonuses.
"I would certainly say to people that are struggling to get into the housing market, think about perhaps even your career and if you could take that remotely, because there are just some great pay-offs that will make it easier for you. And then you kind of get addicted to the lifestyle and the people," she said.
"Now, in Broken Hill I'm still working long hours but making good money. We certainly don't have heaps of money because it's going into our mortgages, but we're making it work."
Owning a home, but not the land
Ms Challender and Ms Mottau are in their late 40s and are happy with where they are at.
Louise Southerden is happy too. The freelance writer lives in a tiny house that is 2.5 metres wide with 13 windows on land in the New South Wales Northern Rivers district.
She and her then partner built it themselves for about $80,000. The tiny house on wheels ticked all the boxes for her.
"I thought I would be renting my whole life. But when rent became unaffordable, I was kind of panicking … rents kept going up and my wages didn't. I always knew that I would have to find some solution and tiny houses came along just at the right time."
Ms Southerden now works for the Australian Tiny House Association.
"One of the interesting things about the tiny house situation is that it totally subverts the traditional housing model because land and the home aren't one package. So I own my own home but I don't own any land.
"I'm currently renting a spot from some friends and the rent is much less, probably half what I would be paying if I was renting an apartment or a studio or even a granny flat."
Ms Southerden uses less energy and water than she ever did before.
Her dream is to buy some land with three or four other people and form a community living in their tiny homes together.
Regulations do not reflect how people want to live
One issue is the lack of consistency within local government regions about the rules around tiny homes and caravans.
Ms Southerden said legislation needed to catch up with how people want and need to live in the future.
Economic research group, e61 Institute, in a paper released recently, said cutting minimum housing lot sizes was a lever governments could use to increase home construction in big cities.
"I think zoning would be the biggest tool that governments currently have to pull," Matthew Goldman, senior research economist at e61 Institute said.
But going smaller is not on the cards for Kym Lardner and his merry band of pensioners.
They are determined to find a house big enough for all of them and don't have time to wait for governments to fix their problem.
"We are six people together trying to find accommodation for the last part of our life."

Climate scientist caught up in Europe's 'dystopian' summer

Fires in France, Spain wear 'the fingerprints of climate crisis'
Sun 2 Aug 2026 at 4:39am
"What climate will this boy grow up in?"
It's the question on climate scientist Stefan Rahmstorf's mind as he takes this photo during his July holiday in southern France.
Flames have engulfed the region in what's described as the worst wildfires Europe has seen in modern history. But, as Stefan knows, this catastrophe did not come as a surprise.
Loading...
As a wind change sent smoke over this beach at Lacanau, north-west of Bordeaux in France, Mr Rahmstorf decided it was time to leave.
The impact of climate change had been part of his research work for decades. Now it had followed him into his summer holiday.
"There was a deep-red sun even at midday in the sky, and it turned the whole landscape into an eerie orange glow and looked like some kind of a dystopian movie," Mr Rahmstorf said.
"The beach life basically went on, the surf lessons went on … as holidaymakers we couldn't really do anything to help.
"That little boy moved me because I was thinking about his future and would he grow up in an escalating climate disaster? Or will we actually manage to get out of fossil fuels fast enough?"
Europe's cards were stacked against it
Western Europe is on fire. From the Atlantic to the Mediterranean Sea, blazes are burning in France, Spain, Greece and Türkiye. Authorities are already calling it the worst fire season in modern history.
The fires, which have burnt thousands of hectares on the outskirts of Spain's capital, Madrid, and Bordeaux, did not fall into a vacuum. Back-to-back heatwaves preceded them.
The heatwaves were not only startling due to their scale, but also their timing.
Before summer had officially started, at the end of May, Western Europe experienced daily average temperatures of more than 10 degrees Celsius above average across western France, England and Wales.
About a month later, large swathes of Europe sweltered through another heatwave, described by many scientists as the worst the continent had ever seen. It marked June 2026 as the hottest ever recorded for Western Europe and the second-warmest globally.
Some reports suggest there were more than 10,000 excess deaths during the record-breaking heatwave. The economic toll was estimated to be close to $2 billion. UN climate chief Simon Stiell said the June heatwave had "the fingerprints of the climate crisis all over it". 
Heatwaves have gripped Europe for much of the summer.
Reuters
Australian National University climate scientist Sarah Perkins-Kirkpatrick said the European summer brought back "bad memories" of what Australia experienced in 2019/20.
"It's eerily similar to what we experienced during Black Summer just six years ago," she said.
"To see the devastation of extreme temperatures, prolonged drying, and then the raging wildfires occurring directly after that … it's just so tragic."
Ms Perkins-Kirkpatrick said she felt "despair" for Europe, primarily because the conditions had not come as a surprise.
"We knew this was going to happen in parts of the world which aren't necessarily in tune to these sorts of events," she said.
"Why couldn't we have adapted quicker and more effectively?"
During the June heatwave, all-time temperature records tumbled at more than 250 weather stations in Europe.
It was the first time in the history of the UK's current weather warnings system that red alerts were issued for three straight days. France recorded its hottest day on record on June 24, with an average national temperature of 30C, while overnight temperatures set a new national record of 22C.
A separate heatwave gripped Spain in early June, with 40.5C recorded in Barcelona, the highest temperature in more than a century of data.
Why Europe is the globe's furnace
Europe is heating at more than twice the global average, and while ongoing research tries to determine why, some factors are already known.
The continent is influenced by its proximity to the Arctic, the fastest-warming region on the planet.
Temperatures in the Arctic are increasing by about 0.75C a decade, partly due to a feedback loop known as the albedo effect. Melting ice is exposing darker ocean water, which is able to absorb more sunlight. That water, in turn, releases more heat back into the atmosphere, leading to further ice melting.
The European temperatures recorded in June were driven by the highest sea surface temperatures on record for the month, including marine heatwaves across the western Mediterranean and Atlantic coasts.
Mr Rahmstorf, who is also an oceanographer at Germany's University of Potsdam, said the marine heatwaves provided an additional heating factor.
"The air masses that stream into Europe, often from the sea, don't get cooled as much by the water underneath," he said.
There are also studies suggesting climate change could be contributing to the increasing persistence of double jet streams, which are fast-flowing higher-altitude air currents that can trap heat over the continent.
Europe's summer temperatures have reached a "new normal". Looking at where the majority of today's temperatures fall compared to the past shows what is behind that shift.
The red bell curve shows that the majority of temperatures between May and July this year are about 4C hotter than they used to be. Heat extremes that have never been recorded before are now happening.
Even a slight shift in average temperatures sees a large increase in the frequency and intensity of heat extremes.
"I still hear it, people saying, 'I don't mind if tomorrow is a couple of degrees warmer'; we all hear about climate change and limiting global warming to 1.5 or 2 degrees Celsius," Ms Perkins-Kirkpatrick said.
"That small change in global average temperature means a much, much, much larger change in the frequency and severity of the actual temperature extremes that we'll experience, which is where all the impacts happen."
'Thirsty' air creates tinderbox
The extra heat in the system this summer dried out the land surface more rapidly than usual, contributing to an extreme drought across parts of Europe.
Ailie Gallant is a climate scientist with the Centre of Excellence for 21st Century Weather at Monash University.
She said blocking high-pressure systems had trapped warm, dry and "thirsty" air over Europe, which had effectively sucked the land dry.
"It sits over for a week or two, and so you get this kind of amplification of this drying signal and of course, that means particularly when it's coupled with extreme heat, plants just shut down," Dr Gallant said.
"They can hit their wilting point very quickly, they dry out very quickly, they lose that moisture, and that makes those conditions in the vegetation and forests just ripe for fire."
Dr Gallant said Australian vegetation had adapted to hotter conditions.
"Big eucalypts have these very, very deep roots that tap right into the ground to access the moisture that's really deep because they know they're going to go through dry periods," she said.
"Whereas, the oak forests of north-western Europe aren't really used to that, so that's also a way that they can become stressed more quickly and really dry out."
Drier soil can also exacerbate heatwaves because a lack of moisture means there is no evaporative cooling from the surface, which reinforces warmer temperatures.
When the land surface and vegetation dry out, it does not take much to ignite.
"As a climate scientist, as an Australian, we can kind of understand these types of mechanisms coming together because we've lived and breathed them here in Australia," Ms Perkins-Kirkpatrick said.
"But for some regions over there, it's not something that happens particularly often, at least to this ferocity."
While fires are part of life in Australia, Western Europe is now grappling with the reality of blazes hurtling towards major population centres and seeing more than 300,000 people flee.
Earlier this week, French President Emmanuel Macron described the wildfires as "completely unprecedented".
"The situation we are dealing with today is the most severe we have ever recorded, the toughest since the Second World War."
But he also warned the threat was not over, with Europe's fourth heatwave of the season hindering firefighting efforts this week, while intense fires are also impacting Greece.
What's in store for Australia's summer?
An extreme summer in the northern hemisphere doesn't mean Australia will have an equally bad season, and there are several factors that will determine what conditions in the southern hemisphere look like over coming months.
El Niño, which has been declared by the Bureau of Meteorology, increases the risk of heatwaves, droughts and bushfires in Australia. While some modelling suggests it could become the strongest on record, that doesn't mean strong impacts on the country's climate.
There are concerns a positive Indian Ocean Dipole could develop, which can reinforce dry conditions when teamed up with El Niño.
Ms Perkins-Kirkpatrick added that conditions could also depend on sea surface temperatures surrounding Australia, not just in the equatorial Pacific, but she emphasised that it was too early to tell.
"If they remain really warm, then we could potentially see a little bit more rainfall but at this stage, we just don't know," she said.
"So it's really, let's wait and see, let's hope for the best but I can't stress enough, we also need to prepare for the worst."
