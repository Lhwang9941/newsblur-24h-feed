Mahmoud Abbas's election promise won't bring democracy to Palestinians - opinion

Mahmoud Abbas, president of the Palestinian Authority, has recently announced that legislative elections will take place on November 28.
While some have welcomed this as a promising opening for democracy in the Palestinian territories, its timing and motives invite scrutiny. Before celebrating the news, we should first ask if these elections represent democratization or merely political preservation. Ultimately, even if Palestinians do vote, ballots alone will not be enough to bring about democracy.
These elections are long overdue, with Hamas’s victory in the 2006 legislative elections marking the beginning of a fierce Hamas-Fatah power struggle and an end to general elections. Since then, Abbas has ruled the PA-administered parts of the West Bank largely by decree. The past two decades have seen various promises made of fresh elections, none of which have materialized.
Recent skepticism stems from the postponement of legislative and presidential elections initially scheduled for May 22, 2021, and July 31, 2021, respectively. The justification given by Abbas was a claim that Israel refused to allow east Jerusalem residents to participate in voting.
Critics, however, emphasize Fatah infighting as the true driver of Abbas’s actions. Marwan Barghouti and Mohammed Dahlan are both potential rivals to Abbas’s Fatah. Barghouti maintains considerable support while serving multiple life sentences in prison, and had planned to challenge Abbas’s presidency in 2021.
These Fatah factions and offshoots were seen to have posed a tangible threat to Abbas’s popularity, making elections a significant political risk.
The lesson from past cancellations is clear: current PA leadership views the will of the people as a threat to legitimacy, not the source of it.
The timing of this new announcement is equally questionable. Some argue that the move represents an attempt to demonstrate popular approval amid growing Hamas popularity in the West Bank since the October 7 terror attack.
Others point to the internal discontent within Fatah, suggesting that the elections serve as an opportunity to manage his rivals and entrench authority.
As domestic pressure intensifies, these renewed promises of elections emerge in a time of PA financial hardship. In fact, the announcement comes just days before the EU’s second meeting of the Palestine Donor Group, raising the likelihood that this apparent democratization may reflect an effort to improve their international standing among donors.
These elections also raise the issue of Hamas – Fatah is far from the sole political actor in the Palestinian territories.
Under the current electoral framework, candidates must accept the PLO’s commitments under the Oslo Accords, a condition that would effectively exclude Hamas from candidacy. Hamas’s participation poses a fundamental paradox.
Should it be prevented from contesting, many will perceive the elections as illegitimate. If it is allowed to run, however, any major electoral gains could be disastrous for the security and institutional integrity of the West Bank.
How Hamas influenced West Bank elections in the past
Regardless of its official absence from the April 2026 municipal election, Hamas was able to informally back a variety of independent lists to expand its influence. Whether or not it officially stands in the coming elections, Hamas will seek to extend and deepen its networks in the social and political fabric of the Palestinian territories.
This would threaten both peace and representative government, given Hamas’s past descent into authoritarianism.
Even more striking than the announcement of legislative elections is Abbas’s promise of a 2027 presidential election. At 90 years old, the subject of succession remains a lingering taboo for many Palestinians.
If we assume Abbas will run – which he himself has not confirmed – then it appears to be a last bid for legitimacy as PA sovereignty crumbles.
Alternatively, in the event he does not stand for reelection, it could provide the transitional moment for a new political dynasty in the Middle East.
Currently, the vice president (Hussein al-Sheikh) is designated to assume power should Abbas end his term unexpectedly. Nevertheless, Abbas’s eldest living son, Yasser Abbas, has emerged as a rumored successor, becoming increasingly involved in Palestinian politics.
A controversial multimillionaire facing allegations of corruption, Yasser was elected to Fatah’s Central Committee in May, making him part of Fatah’s highest decision-making body. Although the motivations behind his appointment remain unclear, Yasser’s succession would safeguard both the family’s political authority and their extensive business interests in the West Bank.
In recent decades, dynasties have frequently accompanied dictatorships in the region. If the 2027 elections are designed to facilitate this, then they would fail to represent democratic revival, but rather a managed inheritance of power within the existing regime.
Despite the growing uncertainties surrounding these general elections, some are optimistic that they may be a positive step towards representation and pluralism. Indeed, holding elections demonstrates both a level of organizational competence and institutional commitment.
Yet performative reform does little to build the future of Palestinian governance. We should not forget that elections are only one feature of democracy. If meaningful progress is to be made, there are many other conditions that have not been met.
Where elections are leveraged, exploited, and manipulated to reinforce the status quo, they cease to be a form of popular participation. To believe that highly imperfect elections are better than none is to ignore how, without a strong democratic foundation, they can reinforce the very deficits they should address.
There are many possible outcomes, perhaps the most likely being that the elections are canceled altogether. Should they proceed, however, we could see anything from Fatah stagnation, an Abbas dynasty, or Hamas expansion. Of all possibilities, none point toward true democratization.
The writer is a British-Egyptian history and politics student at the University of Oxford. She is a policy fellow at the Pinsker Centre, a UK-based foreign policy think tank, and a 2025 Laidlaw Scholar.

From Hamas disarmament to governance in Gaza, the five challenges of the new Gaza plan - analysis

The announcement that Hamas has agreed to a plan for implementing the next stages of US President Donald Trump’s Gaza peace initiative is potentially a significant diplomatic development in Gaza.
If implemented, the agreement could lead to Hamas relinquishing its weapons, an Israeli withdrawal from part of Gaza, the deployment of an International Stabilization Force, and the assumption of governance by the National Committee for the Administration of Gaza (NCAG).
However, history shows that agreements often lack a “trust but verify” aspect. There are at least five major challenges that will determine whether this plan succeeds or joins the long list of ambitious peace initiatives that did not live up to their goals.
Verifying Hamas’s disarmament
The most problematic obstacle could be verification. Hamas has spent decades building a terrorist arsenal. Its weapons include rifles, explosives, anti-tank missiles, rockets, drones, intelligence infrastructure and an extensive tunnel network.
The terrorist group, along with other groups in Gaza, has also shown it can rebuild its capabilities after successive wars.
Announcing that weapons will be decommissioned does not answer the central question: who will verify that weapons have actually been surrendered?
The plan envisions an International Verification Committee, but inspections in conflict zones are difficult. Hamas could attempt to hide portions of its arsenal or transfer weapons to smaller allied terrorist factions. Even if the overwhelming majority of weapons are surrendered, the remaining stockpiles could undermine the entire process.
For Israel, verification will likely be the defining issue.
The sequencing of Israeli withdrawal
The agreement links Israeli withdrawal directly to Hamas’s decommissioning process. This sounds straightforward on paper but is much more complicated in practice.
If Hamas claims that Israel has not withdrawn quickly enough, implementation could stall. It will use this as an excuse to create a crisis. If Israel believes disarmament is incomplete, it may refuse to redeploy.
The plan attempts to solve this by requiring both processes to move in parallel under international supervision.
Nevertheless, almost every previous Israeli-Palestinian agreement has encountered disputes over sequencing. The US will have to keep the ball moving, a challenge when the US is also focused on Lebanon and Iran.
Can the Palestinian NCAG actually govern Gaza?
The NCAG has spent months preparing for this moment. It has pledged to restore public services, improve humanitarian conditions and rebuild institutions. Governing Gaza is far more difficult than administering it on paper.
The territory’s infrastructure has been devastated by years of conflict. Electricity, water, hospitals, schools and transportation networks all require enormous investment.
Civilians need to be able to move out of the Hamas-run area, and the NCAG will need to establish legitimacy among Palestinians while simultaneously working with Israel, the US and international donors.
If Gazans perceive the new administration as ineffective or externally imposed, it could quickly lose public support.
The committee therefore faces not only an administrative challenge but also a political one.
The International Stabilization Force
The plan depends heavily on the International Stabilization Force (ISF).
The ISF is expected to include personnel from countries such as Morocco, Kosovo and Albania, with additional states expected to participate.
Their mission may extend beyond traditional peacekeeping. They will help provide security, facilitate humanitarian assistance and support the transition to Palestinian governance. It is not clear how they will operate. They need more than a handful of troops.
This will not be an easy mission.
International peacekeeping forces have struggled elsewhere in the region. Lebanon’s UNIFIL mission illustrates the problem. Despite decades of deployment, Hezbollah was able to expand its military infrastructure throughout southern Lebanon.
The new force will need a much stronger mandate than previous international missions if it is to prevent armed groups from re-emerging.
Without credible enforcement powers, stabilization risks becoming symbolic rather than operational.
The regional political environment
The Gaza plan does not exist in isolation. The Middle East is undergoing one of its most significant strategic transformations in decades.
The US continues to face a crisis with Iran. Israeli focus remains divided among Gaza, Lebanon, Syria and Iraq. Iran-backed militias remain active across the region, while negotiations continue involving Lebanon and possibly also Syria.
Any major regional crisis could derail implementation.
Iran has consistently opposed efforts that weaken Hamas or reduce the influence of its regional network. Even if Tehran does not directly intervene, groups aligned with Iran could seek to undermine the agreement through violence or political pressure.
At the same time, regional actors that support the road map, including Egypt, Qatar, Turkey and the United States, will have to remain engaged for months, perhaps years.
Diplomatic attention cannot disappear once the agreement is signed. Meanwhile, some countries that back Hamas will try to keep it relevant in Gaza and the West Bank.
