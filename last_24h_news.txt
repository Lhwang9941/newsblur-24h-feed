The Wall Street Journal recently revealed that Israel is spending roughly $50 million on an American influence campaign involving artificial-intelligence-generated text messages, conservative media, online influencers, and former Trump campaign manager Brad Parscale.
According to the report, millions of Americans have received messages from seemingly friendly senders named “Emma” or “Sarah,” asking for their opinions about Israel, Iran, and global security.
This story was published only a matter of days after Vice President JD Vance accused some Israeli officials of attempting to manipulate American opinion in order to keep the Iran conflict going and frustrate US diplomacy via influence campaigns in America.
Israel is right to spend serious money defending itself on the cognitive battlefield. The failure is not the size of the investment. 
The failure is both that we didn’t expose those detractors running campaigns in America twisting the truth against Israel and that we treated an existential national-security responsibility as a marketing challenge that can be outsourced to advertising agencies, political consultants, and algorithms.
Fifty million dollars can buy messages, impressions, consultants, and data. As the famous Beatles song suggests, “money can’t buy you love.”
What our government forgot is that it also cannot buy you trust in a short-term campaign. Without trust, the campaign risks becoming another missile fired at Israel’s own legitimacy.
The accompanying cartoon captures the problem. 
In an American town square, people happily drink apparently pure water while polished robots assure them, “We just want to explain Israel to you.”
But beneath the ground, the information pipes have been fed by Qatar, Iran, China, and Russia for decades. Nearby, hostile operatives pour accusations of “genocide,” “war crimes,” and “death to the IDF” into the public reservoir.
The drinkers see the sparkling fountain. They do not see who is influencing the content of the pipes.
This is how cognitive warfare operates. The objective is not simply to persuade someone to accept one false allegation. 
It is to influence the entire information environment through which that person interprets every subsequent event. Once the water supply is contaminated, offering the public an explanation is not a winning strategy.
Israel’s enemies understood this long ago. Qatar did not merely purchase advertisements promoting its agenda. It invested for decades in media, universities, research institutions, political relationships, and cultural influence.
Iran, China, and Russia do not separate intelligence, diplomacy, information operations, cyber activity, and national strategy into unrelated assignments. They construct ecosystems intended to shape what societies see, whom they trust, and which political choices become acceptable.
Israel, by contrast, repeatedly behaves as if its problem is insufficient explanation (i.e. hasbara – public diplomacy).
When hostility rises, we produce another video, hire another spokesperson, launch another campaign, and count another million impressions. We measure how many people saw our content, not whether legislation changed, a university acted, a false legal allegation was defeated, or an ally maintained political support.
That is a communications activity. It is not strategic influence.
From hasbara to strategic influence
The new campaign does contain important innovations. Israel has correctly identified younger Americans – including younger conservatives – as a critical audience.
It recognizes that artificial intelligence and search engines are becoming gateways through which people understand history and current affairs. It is collecting audience data, producing content at scale, and attempting to compete in environments Israel neglected for too long.
Foreign Minister Gideon Sa’ar is also correct that public diplomacy must be funded as seriously as jets, bombs, and missile interceptors.
The latest Pew data explain why: 60% of American adults view Israel unfavorably, as do 57% of Republicans between 18 and 49.
This is no longer only a problem on the progressive Left. It has reached the emerging generation of the Republican Right and is weakening what was once a broadly bipartisan alliance.
But responding to an existential threat through a collection of commercial contracts creates a dangerous contradiction. If the cognitive war is truly a war, where is the war cabinet?
Where is the national intelligence assessment? Where is the chain of command integrating the Prime Minister’s Office, Foreign Ministry, intelligence community, IDF, Diaspora Affairs Ministry, and Israel’s diplomatic missions?
 
Who defines the strategic objectives, tests the messages, examines unintended consequences, and measures policy outcomes?
Most importantly, who is accountable if the strategy fails?
The IDF would never outsource the defense of Israel’s airspace to mercenaries and then evaluate them by counting how many interceptor videos received views. 
Yet Israel has placed significant elements of its defense against cognitive missiles in the hands of advertising firms, foreign consultants, and politically connected intermediaries.
These contractors may be talented. Parscale understands political data and digital targeting. Media firms understand distribution. Influencers understand their audiences. But none of them is responsible for the national security of Israel or the safety of Jewish communities worldwide.
Ultimately there are four major failures with the campaign that need addressing:
 anonymity instead of authenticity;
 impressions instead of policy impact;
 contractors instead of an integrated national command;
 persuasion without policy feedback.
The reported use of AI personas presents an additional danger. A recipient who believes she is communicating with a person named Emma, only to discover an automated system connected to a foreign government campaign, is unlikely to feel better about Israel.
She will feel manipulated. The tactic can validate the very allegation Israel’s adversaries promote: that support for Israel is manufactured through concealed influence rather than earned through truth and shared values.
In cognitive warfare, credibility is ammunition. Once expended, it is extremely difficult to replace. Israel needs a permanent cognitive-security directorate operating under a national-security authority.
It must research the networks contaminating the information supply, expose their funding and methods, communicate through credible and properly disclosed messengers, receive continuous feedback, and connect that feedback to policy decisions.
Its measurements must move beyond clicks and impressions to consequences: votes, regulations, diplomatic decisions, prosecutions, university policies, media corrections, and durable changes in public attitudes.
This command must also be capable of telling Israel’s political and military leaders when the problem is not merely how a policy is explained but how it is understood and experienced.
Public diplomacy cannot succeed if it functions only as the final packaging department for decisions already made. Strategic influence requires communication to be considered before, during, and after policy formation.
Israel is not facing an eighth “front” that can be addressed with occasional public relations.
It is fighting an all-out war – one that affects its freedom of military action, diplomatic alliances, legal legitimacy, economic relationships, and the physical security of Jews around the world.
The government deserves credit for finally putting serious resources into this battle. But purchasing reach is not the same as building influence, and neither is it the same as earning trust.
Israel does not need more robots to build trust and legitimacy. It needs to defend the reservoir, expose those poisoning it, and take responsibility for the entire system – from the source of the information to its ultimate effect on policy.
The national security of Israel and the Jewish people is too important to outsource.

Sally Reidman, founder and president of Reidman College of Complementary and Integrative Medicine, received the Sylvan Adams Nefesh B’Nefesh Bonei Zion Prize in 2023 for her achievements in education.
How did her life lead up to this prestigious moment?
Born in Pennsylvania in 1949 to Holocaust survivors, Sally grew up with her mother in California and studied psychology at California State University.
She founded Reidman College in1987, and it is now recognized as Israel’s largest institute of its kind. Today, more than 4,000 students are enrolled in the college’s five vocational schools: Traditional Chinese Medicine, Natural Medicine, Somatic Therapies, Holistic Psychotherapy, and Holistic Coaching.
The college has four campuses, with its main campus in Tel Aviv and branches in Jerusalem, Haifa, and Beersheba.
The Magazine recently sat down with Sally in her Tel Aviv office.
What influenced you to make aliyah?
I didn’t go to a Jewish school or belong to a Jewish youth group. Still, over the years, my mom would tell me how much she wanted me to go to Israel and that she would pay for the ticket.
One day, an Israeli friend - my only Israeli friend at the time - told me she was going to Israel to visit her dying grandmother. That was the trigger.
When the plane landed and my feet touched the ground, I became very emotional and started to cry. I ended up staying for two months, returned to California to wrap things up, and then made aliyah in 1983.
I had no money, only one distant relative in Israel, and no idea what I was going to do. I just knew I was meant to be here. I was a spiritual seeker and felt that here I could be a channel for love and light.
How did you get started and what do you recommend to new olim who want to succeed here professionally?
Since childhood, I have been used to hard work. I came to Ashdod with my massage table and initially lived in an absorption center. I would take the table with me on the bus as I traveled to work in three different places.
After 18 months, I moved to a small apartment in Tel Aviv and rented space in a gym for my practice. Eventually, I was running a small school, which later became Reidman College.
My advice to new olim is to work hard, love what you do, become good at it, and be passionate. Although I’m not religious, I am spiritual and have always had a connection to God. I believe God brings me what I need.
Was Reidman your family name?
It was the name of my former husband. We were married for less than four years, had no children, and divorced before I moved to Israel.
How would you sum up the mission of Reidman College?
We believe in the advancement of natural medicine by working side by side with conventional medicine through integration and cooperation.
At one time, natural medicine was considered alternative medicine. Today, the proper terms are “complementary” and “integrative” medicine.
The goal of natural medicine is to identify the underlying cause of a condition and promote long-term wellness, often reducing reliance on pharmaceutical drugs.
Natural medicine, therapy, various courses
How does the religious community relate to natural medicine?
In general, the religious community is very supportive of natural medicine. In Jerusalem, we have separate courses for men and for women. Because the demand is so great, we’re planning a dedicated wing at our Jerusalem campus.
Do you have courses in English?
The college is launching an English-language program of massage therapy, to include holistic Swedish massage and basic medical massage.
What advice would you give someone looking for a massage therapist?
Make sure your therapist is insured. Insurance companies will not insure someone who does not have a certificate from an accredited school.
Beyond credentials, there are the personal qualities: attention, intention, and love. We want therapists who give us their full attention, whose intentions are clear, and who genuinely love their work.
Does AI have any effect on your profession?
As AI takes over more and more professions, the idea of a career in therapy is becoming increasingly popular. Ours is a profession that AI is unlikely to replace. In fact, it is one of the fastest-growing professions.
In a Jerusalem Post article published in May, Rabbi Dr. Jonathan Lieberman wrote: ‘We need more therapists.’ What’s your view?
I totally agree. Especially since Oct. 7, the need has become desperate. That’s one reason our college continues to grow.
Not everyone comes here simply to find a job. Some want to raise healthier families. Some come to heal themselves. We also have many hi-tech professionals who work with computers all day and come to us to unwind and interact with other like-minded people.
In a 2026 message for Valentine’s Day in the ‘Algemeiner Journal,’ you wrote: ‘It is known that people with pets to care for and receive love from them live longer and are happier than most others. Dogs, in particular, are renowned for giving unconditional love.’ Would you add anything?
It may not be accidental that the Hebrew word for dog is ‘kelev.’ Lev means “heart.”
Your closing thoughts?
At Reidman, we focus on helping you become the best you, so that you, in turn, can give to others. As we heal ourselves, we help heal the planet.
Sally Reidman
From Los Angeles to Ashdod, 1983

Every Israeli abroad must realize they are an ambassador for their country - opinion

I didn’t want to leave Israel. Not now when the world hates us. Not when there are endless flight delays, and no one knows what tomorrow might bring.
Especially not to go to Greece - a country that deported its Jews to Auschwitz in World War II. On my last trip to Athens, in a sadly empty Jewish Museum, I learned that the once robust Jewish population in Greece of about 75,000 had dwindled to approximately 5,000 Jews.
But my American son was coming into Crete, ironically to decompress from a tour of Poland and Auschwitz, so my husband and I decided to meet him there. Traveling along with a plane load full of Israelis, watching some of the younger ones rudely cutting lines and behaving badly, it suddenly occurred to me - we are all ambassadors.
No matter where we travel, whether or not we are “identifiably” Israeli or Jewish, whether we accept the job or not, each and every one of us is a representative - and it is important to be aware of it.
“F*** the IDF” said graffiti on an electrical box as I trudged up one of Crete’s many uphill streets. “F*** Zionists” was blasted across a billboard near the airport. “Free Palestine” messages decorated the other side of the road.
'I was reminded of my identity everywhere I went'
Although I only saw three instances of graffiti in my 48 hours in Crete, I was reminded of my identity everywhere I went and with everyone I spoke to, as a representative of my people.
Israelis can be raucous, outspoken, and loud, and like most, they are ready to party, especially on vacation, but now, more than ever, I felt compelled to rein it in and remember - we are, each and every one of us, ambassadors for a greater cause.
Whether on line at an airport, checking in to a hotel, walking on narrow crowded sidewalks within a rush of other tourists, or enjoying a spa treatment, travelers should be keenly aware of the “tourist tightrope” we tread. We are being observed, judged, and measured - as a tourist, but more importantly as an Israeli.
That means even if you don’t do it at home, put garbage in the bin, clean up after yourself, and exercise good manners everywhere and with everyone - even when people seem impolite to us.
My kippah-wearing husband and son were gazing out at the beautiful sunset over the Mediterranean when they were approached by a gentleman from Denmark. They exchanged pleasantries, and he asked where my family was from. My husband acknowledged that he was from Israel.
“Some members of my family have big problems with what we are seeing on the news about Israel,” the tourist said. “We saw a story about a Palestinian family that had been living in their home for 45 years and got dragged out of their house by the IDF. It seemed so tragic.”
My husband asked for context - was the family in a legal habitat? Was it brought to a court of law? The Danish man didn’t know. My son, who is a lawyer in the US, spoke about different laws that may govern disputed residential habitats. Together, they explained that watching footage, most likely taken by advocacy groups with a particular agenda, doesn’t necessarily reflect the entire story - and can be easily taken out of context.
The gentleman listened attentively. No one lost their temper or raised their voice.
Then he told my family that when Israel was attacked in 1967, he was a medic in the Danish army. He said he was so moved by his feelings for Israel that he was ready to go and help defend the country. Fortunately for everybody, that war ended quickly and victoriously, but he regretted never getting the chance to assist Israel.
“The television and social media are set-ups for bias,” was my husband’s takeaway from the encounter. “I believe most people are open-minded, as this man was. I may not have convinced him to change his mind, but perhaps the next time he watches TV, he might realize there is another side to the story.”
“People in Greece respect our tourists,” Maria, a massage therapist at the Intercontinental Hotel, told me. “Crete goes dark in the winter, and we work hard to please people during the summer tourist season. I’ve met lots of Israelis, and we hear things about Israel. Meeting nice ones who talk to us helps us understand the situation better.
“I can’t imagine living with sirens and missiles,” she added. “Running for shelter along with my two-and-a-half-year-old daughter would be a terrifying experience.”
My revelation from the short trip was twofold.
First, I realized that even in these uncertain times, when I dread traveling, every Israeli must understand the responsibility of carrying Israel’s message to the world.
Second, traveling enables us to learn, grow, and connect with people we would never meet otherwise.
While it pulls us far out of our comfort zones in so many ways, it also allows us to accomplish far more, both for ourselves and for Israel, than we ever could from behind our keyboards.
