Heatwaves are increasingly taking a toll on Europe’s economy, reducing productivity, curbing consumer spending and raising operating costs. For insurers, such losses can be difficult to cover because they often stem from indirect operational disruption rather than property damage.
“Heat in itself is not a traditionally insured risk,” said Swenja Surminski, managing director for climate and sustainability at Marsh.
“Extreme heat rarely causes catastrophic physical damage the way a flood or a storm does, but the financial operational disruption that it triggers can be just as severe.”
A 2023 survey of 9,000 small and medium-sized firms for Europe’s insurance regulator found 28 per cent held business interruption cover as part of their property insurance, while 17 per cent had non-damage business interruption protection covering events such as strike action.
The protection gap is widening as the economic costs of extreme heat mount. Trains are delayed, agricultural yields fall and factory cooling costs rise, while workers often struggle to maintain productivity during prolonged spells of extreme temperatures.
Companies that flagged a hit from hot weather or warned about its potential future impact when reporting second-quarter earnings included Swedish shop-fitting provider ITAB Group, Italian cement producer Buzzi and French payments firm Worldline.
Compound risk
Heat often acts as a compound risk, interacting with drought, wildfire and water shortages, rather than triggering a single identifiable loss event. That makes it harder to model and insure than some other natural catastrophes.

“The real loss is the revenue you don’t make and the business activity that never takes place because of the outage,” Luni said.

“Parametric insurance can really play a role,” said Aidan Kerr, head of UK and Ireland public sector solutions at Swiss Re.

Großpörthen's pillory lime: 900-year-old tree with dark history
Unknown
Unknown Publisher
2026-08-16
06:10:34 UTC
15:10:34 KST

Up to 900 years old, almost ten metres thick, its trunk still carries an old iron collar. The Prangerlinde in Großpörthen, Saxony-Anhalt, is a unique natural and historical monument.
In the small village of Großpörthen, around five kilometres south-east of Zeitz in Saxony-Anhalt, stands a tree that has witnessed history over the centuries. The mighty winter lime tree beside the village church is estimated to be between 700 and 900 years old. Its trunk has a circumference of around 9.85 metres and it is about twelve metres tall. This probably makes it the thickest known winter lime tree in Germany.
But it is not just its age that makes the lime tree special. It owes its name to its presumed role as a "court" and pillory tree. A rusty iron collar can still be seen on its trunk today. It is a reminder of a time when public punishments were part of everyday legal practice.
Middle Ages: punishment by chaining offenders with iron collars
An iron collar was a metal ring that was placed around the neck of a convicted person and locked. Public pillory punishments involving iron collars are documented in the German-speaking world from at least the 14th century.
This form of so-called shame or honour punishment was particularly widespread in the late Middle Ages and the early modern period. However, it remained in use for several centuries and is documented in German legal sources well into the 18th century. The punishment was aimed above all at public humiliation and thus the destruction of the offender's reputation and, depending on the place and the judgement, was combined with corporal punishment.
In some cases, the offenders were given signs to wear stating their offence.
The pillory was historically used for a very wide range of offences. Sources mention, among other things, minor theft, insults, slander, disturbing the peace, violations of moral norms or cheating on measures and weights.
Court lime tree almost 1,000 years old
The exact age of the lime tree cannot be determined. For trees of this age, precise dating is difficult, partly because the trunk changes over the centuries. The unusual position of the winter lime on a slope has led to severe thickening and overgrowth in the trunk and root area. Experts from the German Dendrological Society estimate its age at between 700 and 900 years.
The tree has also survived serious damage. A larger part of the trunk is said to have broken off around the mid-20th century. Nevertheless, the lime tree has recovered and is considered to be in good health.
Germany's now second-oldest tree stands in Upstedt in Lower Saxony. It recently lost the top spot to a yew about 1,100 years old on an alpine pasture in the Allgäu. The Upstedt tree is also a lime tree, more precisely a summer lime, which is regarded as less robust. The historic square in the centre of the village where it stands was already used as a meeting place in the early second millennium; in the 13th century the lime tree became a court tree.
There is, however, no evidence that this tree, like the winter lime in Großpörthen, was also used as a pillory.

One of this summer’s biggest onscreen hits has had big beasts, great escapes and choppy waters, but it’s not Christopher Nolan’s The Odyssey – it’s a livestream of Alaskan brown bears catching salmon.
The Brooks Falls bearcam at Katmai national park in the US was watched 7.3m times between 22 June and 4 August – up 70% from the same period last year and more than double the views it attracted in 2024.
The cameras, run by the wildlife network Explore.org, show brown bears gathering at Brooks Falls waterfall to catch sockeye salmon as they make their way upstream. More than a dozen bears at a time can be spotted prowling around the rocky waters and chomping on the fish.
Candice Rusch, Explore.org’s director of new media, said the surge in viewers appeared to be driven by the bears returning to the falls in large numbers after a less concentrated season last year.
“Last year was a really high salmon run, which meant there was salmon all over the river and the bears didn’t need to congregate at the falls the way they’re doing right now,” she said.
“This year we’ve just had very good views of large congregations of bears.
“It’s like the whole world discovered they could put bearcam on their TV at the same time,” she said. “Maybe people just have it on while they’re cooking dinner.”
The cameras were first installed in 2012 as part of an effort to bring remote wildlife into people’s homes. Rusch said the idea was that people were more likely to protect animals and landscapes they had come to know and care about.
Dr Jeffrey Skibins, an associate professor at East Carolina University, said: “Recent studies have shown that nature-based webcams are beneficial to both wildlife and people. They benefit wildlife by improving conservation attitudes, behaviours and awareness.
“This is not to suggest they are replacements for getting out in nature, but rather serve as viable additions,” he said.
The stream has developed a dedicated fanbase. Viewers can identify individual bears from features such as their ears, noses and body shape, and some keep detailed records of their feeding habits.
One dedicated fan reported watching a bear called Otis eat 42 salmon over several hours, which Rusch said could equate to more than 100,000 calories.
The bears’ gorging will take on added significance next month, when Katmai’s annual Fat Bear Week begins in the autumn. The public votes in a knockout-style competition to crown the park’s fattest bear, as the animals pile on winter weight in preparation for hibernation.
“The bears are great,” Rusch said. “This is an amazing place and it deserves all the love it’s getting.”

Fire rangers of Mongolian steppe: climate crisis spurs revival of ancient knowledge – photo essay
Unknown
Unknown Publisher
2026-08-16
06:00:17 UTC
15:00:17 KST

Sodnomjamts Damchaabadgar squats in a shallow, rocky depression, working quietly with his hands. Inches above the rim of the pit, the wind whips across the grasslands. But in the marginal shelter of the dig site the air remains calm – essential for delicate operations.
Damchaabadgar, a research fellow at the Institute of Archaeology at the Mongolian Academy of Sciences, has erected a ger, a traditional nomad’s tent, nearby, which serves as his sleeping quarters and a field station.
“When we examine these gravesites, we see evidence of horse domestication dating back to the middle bronze age,” he says. “We now think that the ancient peoples of Mongolia may have tamed horses far earlier than previously expected, perhaps as early as 2,000 BCE, which makes sense because horses were critical to survival on the grasslands. They gave an advantage in battle – that’s well known – but perhaps just as importantly, horses gave them the speed that they needed to escape from the grassland’s greatest natural threat: fire.”
While fire has long been endemic on the steppe, the hazard has been steadily growing in the 20th and 21st centuries. The human-driven climate crisis has led to the heating and drying of the Eurasian grasslands, with a 2C rise in temperatures recorded over the last 80 years.
From 1981 to 1995, an average of 1.74 million hectares (4.3m acres) of forest and steppe was scorched each year during fire season. Then, from 1996 to 1999, more than 30m hectares was consumed by fire in four years. More recently, the fire seasons of 2022 and 2025 led to deepening public concern.
According to a recent study about 95% of steppe and forest fires in Mongolia are caused by human activity, whether from careless burning, discarded cigarettes or debris left to smoulder. What starts small rarely stays that way. Most fires move from north to south along prevailing winds, and when a blaze’s speed exceeds people’s ability to move livestock or escape, it becomes a matter of survival rather than containment. For rangers, being first on scene at a fire, however minor its origin, means racing the wind as much as the flames themselves.
The Eurasian steppe is the world’s largest swathe of grassland, extending more than 5,000 miles from Hungary to the western edge of China. The vast, open expanse created a natural land bridge between the continents, a highway of trade and conflict. It was here that the Mongol horse lords built the largest contiguous land empire in world history.
“Adaptability was key,” says Erdenesuvd Bayaraa, a cultural heritage expert at the National Centre for Cultural Heritage of Mongolia. “You have to move quickly to survive on the grass. The domestication of horses and a nomadic way of life was the solution. Our ancestors knew how to live on the grass and with fire because they had to.”
“I believe that the solution lies in our shared heritage,” says Bayaraa. “If we want to live with fire, we have to remember how to embrace it. Today, almost 70% of Mongolians live in cities. But only a few generations ago, it was exactly the opposite. These rural communities had teams of horseback rangers who would be on the lookout for fire.
“When fires occurred, they would ride quickly towards the flames and dig trenches to block the spread. Then they would cut openings for people to pass through and use animal fat to break up the fire line. If we want to stop fires from spreading out of control today, we need to respond the way that our ancestors did. I believe that people on the land are the solution.”
Bayaraa’s ideas are gaining traction. In 2024, she and other local leaders joined Preserving Legacies, a National Geographic Society-funded global initiative focused on creating climate adaptation solutions that bridge traditional cultural knowledge with emerging technology. Supported in part by the programme, the Mongolian government is strengthening its fire ranger corps, embracing a mix of traditional, horseback fire management techniques coupled with modern technology such as satellite detection and advanced weather modelling. Starlink services, which launched in March 2024, have further enabled communication between the frontline rangers, communities, and smoke jumpers who respond by parachuting behind fire lines.
Also in 2024, Mongolia launched a major initiative with the UN Development Programme and the China International Development Cooperation Agency to strengthen wildfire forecasting, reporting systems and fire suppression management. The project has delivered $350,000 (£260,000) worth of firefighting equipment to rangers working with the National Forest Agency and expanded training with fire jumpers from locations such as Alaska. The Mongolian government is also pursuing a variety of incentives to encourage people to remain in rural communities or return to them, including financial packages for those willing to relocate from cities back to the countryside.
“People have known how to survive on the grass for thousands of years,” says Bayaraa. “Remaining here means remembering who we are while continuing to adapt and bring new technology to the table. We can do that. Adaptation is central to our way of life.”

You are on a short break in Porto and post some pictures of your family on Instagram or Facebook. With a small section of the Douro river in the background, you think it could have been taken anywhere.
A few days later, you get a text saying that your card was compromised. “We detected unusual activity while you were travelling in Porto – please verify immediately,” says the message.
You click on the link to confirm your bank details. Since you have not put any details of your trip on any of your social networks – bar the indistinct pictures – you don’t suspect there is anything suspicious about the messages.
But the text was a fraud designed to extract your financial details. The criminals behind it figured out where you had been on holidays – lending credibility to their text – by using AI to analyse the image for the most sparse signs of where it was taken.
New research has shown that by picking up on details in the picture – such as the background, the architecture, the signage or the light – the AI agent can pinpoint where it was taken. For criminals, this information gives attempts to defraud by text message or email an added legitimacy.
McAfee, the producer of anti-virus software, used two freely available AI models to test more than 21,000 travel images. One of the models identified 91% of images accurately while the other got 87%. Staff were then asked to replicate the experiment with their own pictures and became uncomfortable with how easy their travels were pinpointed.
The company says that it shows that computers don’t need photos to be tagged or attached metadata to identify where they are taken.
“What AI does is give context … so that makes the scam [and] makes the threats credible,” says Vonny Gamot, the head of EMEA at McAfee.
What it looks like
Pictures are more likely to be identified by AI if they have recognisable landmarks, skylines, signage and street markings. Food stalls and storefronts can also pinpoint quickly where the picture was taken.
If the picture is taken on a beach or a hotel room, the accuracy of the AI is lowered. But McAfee reports that it is likely that the system can identify which country they were taken in – which is all the scammers need.
When a staff member tested ChatGPT to identify a picture of a river with some trees in the foreground, it correctly pinpointed it as Hastings-on-Hudson, an area in New York state.
Another picture of a group of flowers was identified as the Keukenhof gardens in the Netherlands. The AI agent correctly deduced that the layout of the tulips, along with smaller blue flowers planted between them, meant that it was the famous gardens in the picture.
Criminals can use the information to make their approaches more convincing. They might say that your card was flagged for unusual activity while you were somewhere. Or that they are calling you after your stay in a particular hotel. Or that they want to confirm your identity because there has been an attempt to log in to your account from that country.
What to do
If you want to post pictures, delay until after you get home and change your settings so that only the people you know can see them.
As with all scams, be wary about any urgency in the messages you are sent, such as being told that you need to act immediately. Fraudsters use this as a tactic in the hope that people react without thinking something through fully.
Don’t click on links that are provided in texts or emails; instead contact the company or bank through the details on their website or on the back of your bank card.

Das had his redemption and Bangladesh was on its way.
2. Webster falls to a beauty
Spin bowling is an art form built on deception, with guile and sleight of hand as important as pin-point accuracy.
On a pitch offering few surprises and only the minimum of turn, it is the impetus of a genuine spinner to trick their way into the minds of their rivals.
Beau Webster had played sensibly through the first 20 balls of his innings, alert to the threat of Mehidy Hasan Miraz's off spinners.
Mehidy was probing at the off stump of Webster, but the ball that deceived him was simply wonderful.
Angling the ball so that the seam would not bite, Mehidy just pushed the ball through a couple of kilometres per hour faster than the balls before.
With no seam to grip, the ball skidded on, perhaps a little lower, and took the top of his off stump.
A master magician couldn't have disguised his intents better and Australia's tail was exposed.

Why Iran’s threat keeps haunting the Middle East - and how to finally break the cycle - opinion
Unknown
Unknown Publisher
2026-08-16
05:34:28 UTC
14:34:28 KST

For nearly 50 years, the world has dealt with Iran under a straightforward premise: the sole source of danger is the theocracy installed by the 1979 revolution.
Western policies have split between containing the regime, changing its conduct, eroding its capabilities, or waiting for internal collapse. The record of these tactics, though, raises a tougher question.
Why does the Iranian threat return each time Tehran’s foes believe they have contained it?
The breeding grounds enabling centralized power
For decades, the world may have fixated on who governs Iran while ignoring the framework that lets central authorities rebuild their power project whenever conditions change.
The core issue is about more than the nuclear program, ballistic missile stockpile, and proxy networks. These are assets that can be disabled or partly destroyed.
They spring, however, from a large state with a huge population, energy reserves, a scientific and industrial foundation, geographic reach, and military institutions honed across decades.
Lost facilities, leaders, and munitions cannot wipe out technical know-how or erase recovery capacity. A single tool may break, but the underlying machine still can build a replacement.
History shows international dealings with Iran have targeted what can be destroyed; capabilities can be degraded. But such tactics leave unanswered the more fundamental question of how to stop a large, centralized state with people, resources and expertise from rebuilding losses once conditions ease.
So, the issue moves from disarmament to state structure, from who governs Iran to how power sources inside the country are administered.
One episode lays bare this disconnect. The July 2015 nuclear deal capped portions of Iran’s nuclear work. It left Tehran’s view of its regional standing intact. Missile work and proxy operations also proceeded without pause.
The January 2020 killing of Maj.-Gen. Qasem Soleimani jolted the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, although influence networks resumed activity once roles were reassigned.
Strikes on Iranian military sites have proved able to inflict damage and disrupt operations, but they cannot ensure permanent disablement.
Containment thus amounts to little more than managing repeated rounds of buildup, strike and rearmament.
Viewed this way, regime change in Iran offers at best a partial fix if presented as a final answer to the problem. The ruling system provides the country’s power project with its ideology, while the centralized state furnishes its resources and wealth.
Replacing the regime while leaving power administration untouched may soften rhetoric but offers no assurance of a different course. A more pragmatic, economy-focused government could emerge.
Geography, national interests and latent capabilities, however, survive leadership turnover. Long-held regional ambitions would likely resurface, framed in nationalist rather than revolutionary terms.
That said, casting the Iranian state as inherently hostile misreads reality. Most countries adjust conduct when their institutions, military doctrine, and balance between rulers and ruled evolve.
The objective is something other than indefinite weakening of Iran. It is blocking the convergence of unchecked central power, expansionist ideology, and exclusive control over military decisions.
Chaotic breakup of the Iranian state carries its own severe risks. Internal diversity, widespread weapons, and Iran’s strategic position could turn collapse into civil conflict, foreign meddling, and massive disruption of energy flows and shipping lanes.
Break the ‘buildup, strike, resurgence’ pattern
The most durable course therefore starts inside Iran, on the premise that Iranians alone hold the right to choose their governing system and its structure.
Broad decentralization or federalism, and possibly confederal arrangements if adopted through national consent and constitutional agreement, can curb the center’s monopoly on wealth and decisions, give regions greater say over resources and fiscal matters, and write institutional limits into the use of national capacities for foreign ventures.
Forcing such models from outside, by contrast, would let authorities cast themselves as defenders of national unity and turn reform into a pretext for harsher repression.
Decentralization hardly guarantees Iran will turn automatically into a peaceful country. Constitutional arrangements by themselves cannot dispel national ambitions or remove foreign frictions. Their worth lies in raising the price of decision monopolization, spreading oversight of resources, and making long-term foreign ventures far harder to pursue without broad consent and accountability.
Such rules constrain impulsive action while still falling short of an ironclad promise of responsible behavior.
No global requirement exists for a permanent Iranian adversary to justify alliances and defense budgets. Removing the regime with no plan for what follows promises an even more dangerous crisis.
What is required instead is ending the threat’s ability to regenerate, through four parallel steps: capping offensive capabilities, advancing institutional reform that disperses power inside Iran, upholding regional deterrence to block coercion, and pursuing diplomacy that links economic and political integration to revised conduct.
The world has repeatedly slowed Iran’s power project. It has never eliminated the country’s ability to rebuild it. The ruling system may fall one day, but its collapse would leave intact the building blocks of Iranian strength.
If a framework survives that lets central authorities marshal people, resources and expertise behind foreign ventures, any win will amount to a temporary pause between rounds.
A credible resolution comes when Iran’s strength changes from a source of fear for neighbors into a force bounded by institutions and directed by societal interests. Only then does the root problem resolve itself. The danger will not have sustained another setback – it will have lost the conditions that kept reviving it.

Africa's low-key high-stakes Strait of Hormuz diplomacy
August 16, 2026
One of the principal arteries of world trade has been chronically blocked for almost six months. Maritime traffic in the Strait of Hormuz has been reduced to a bare minimum since the first US-Israeli airstrikes on Iran at the end of February. Cargo ships have at times been fired on by Iranian forces; at others, threats from Tehran or Washington have been enough to bring shipping to a standstill.
So far, various rounds of talks, a joint memorandum and third-party deliberations about the creation of a maritime mission have not succeeded in stabilizing the situation. It remains to be seen whether a deal between Iran and Oman on establishing a shipping route through the strait will do so.
The war has prompted fossil fuel prices to soar all over the world, with inflation rising as a result. Despite its relative proximity to the Gulf region, some parts of Africa have experienced serious shortages because of the bottlenecks, with long lines of cars at gas stations that have run out of fuel. As many fertilizers are also sourced from the Gulf region, this year's harvests also threaten to be bad. African governments therefore have every reason to engage diplomatically in finding a permanent solution.
Cautious diplomacy
"I believe their long-term plan is still based on the assumption that the crisis in the Middle East may continue for some time," says Liesl Louw-Vaudran from the International Crisis Group, an NGO that works to prevent conflicts.
The US and Iran signed a memorandum of understanding in June, but Louw-Vaudran, an expert advisor working with the African Union, comments: "We don't see any evidence that there was some kind of optimism or revising of any economic planning after the MOU. I think everyone literally is just waiting to see how this is going to pan out in the next couple of months."
It's certainly the case that a low-key approach has dominated African diplomacy with regard to Hormuz in recent months. There have been numerous calls for negotiated solutions, praise for diplomatic initiatives and an emphasis on the rules-based order. Some African governments have also shown solidarity with the Arab Gulf states, which, as US allies, have come under fire from Iran.
The current African members of the UN Security Council — the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Somalia and Liberia — also backed a UN resolution adopted in March that strongly condemned Iranian attacks on the Gulf states.
The elephant in the room: Donald Trump
However, it is apparent from their communications that African governments are trying to avoid apportioning blame — especially to the US president, who, together with Israeli president Benjamin Netanyahu, was the one who started the war.
"Every rational human being in the world knows that this crisis has been occasioned by Mr. Donald Trump," says Dismas Mokua, a political risk analyst with the Kenyan advisory firm Tricarta. "But the majority of African leaders, and indeed across the world, fear to call him out because of the consequences. And I've seen European leaders tiptoeing around this issue, I've seen leaders from Asia — leaders all over the world. I've not seen a single leader who has come out to call Mr. Trump, because I guess they fear the consequences of their speech."
What if the whole of Africa were to speak with one voice — perhaps through the African Union? Louw-Vaudran from the International Crisis Group thinks a recent negative experience is still too fresh.
"The African Union Commission in Addis Ababa burned its fingers in the early days of the Ukraine war, in 2022, when the previous chairperson criticized Russia. And heads of state then immediately silenced him, and said that the commission doesn't have the mandate to speak on foreign affairs issues on behalf of the continent," Louw-Vaudran says.
She points out that the AU, with 55 member states, is "very much divided," and says that, since then, "when it comes to political statements in this big, rapidly changing geopolitical situation, the commission has been hesitant."
Ease the symptoms, don't create new problems
The crisis also requires governments to take action rather than just make statements.
"For most countries, or even companies, to be successful wherever there is a disruption in this global supply, you have to reevaluate your supply chains and find out which fits your interest," Mokua says. "And in my view, Kenya has managed to do that with a lot of success."
He comments that he hasn't seen anyone — and, in particular, commercial farmers — complaining about deliveries.
"And even for the supply of oil and gas products, people anticipated that there were going to be shortages. But there were no shortages. The suppliers have been constant and reliable, despite the increase in cost."
But the experience of neighboring Ethiopia has been very different. Delivery bottlenecks forced the government to temporarily prioritize security agencies, public transport and agriculture when distributing fuel. Private individuals were stuck waiting at empty gas stations.
Before the war, Ethiopia was one of Iran's most important African trading partners. That connection is now also at risk, since Ethiopia also relies on maintaining good relations with the United Arab Emirates and increasingly with Saudi Arabia, too.
Countries like Ethiopia and Kenya may also be about to feel another, delayed effect of the Iran war. If the rich Gulf states' economies falter, East African migrant workers there will transfer less money back home.
Opportunity for African countries to work together
But there are also some African countries for whom the uncertain situation in the Gulf might actually be helpful. Nigeria's huge new Dangote refinery came online at just the right time. Intended to ease fuel shortages in the oil-rich country, it's now a mainstay of the world market. In April, the refinery became the world's largest exporter of aviation fuel.
Responding to the Hormuz crisis, Nigeria has offered itself as a partner for African clients. The Dangote corporation is already planning another refinery in Kenya. And the government is standing with the Gulf states: On the oil market, countries seek to be partners, not competitors.
"Nigeria has been very smart, at least in terms of that economic positioning, having the crude itself," says Liesl Louw-Vaudran. "But obviously Nigeria has got huge governance and security issues that are very difficult to overcome."
Perhaps the least visible issue in the short term but one that is likely to have the most lasting impact: The crisis provides another argument for pressing ahead with the implementation of the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA).
"There are a number of countries that do not understand the meaning of dismantling these artificial borders. They fear that they are going to lose political control," says the analyst Dismas Mokua.
But he added that he believes many African countries will eventually embrace the dismantling of arbitrary borders drawn up by European colonizers at the infamous Berlin Conference in 1885. "If you want to immunize yourself from the vagaries associated with the global disruption, then you must learn to trade within Africa," he says.

"They are still found where there are residential buildings and where there are rubbish bins. You could say that the first step in controlling rats is to look at the places where they get food – whether the areas around our rubbish bins are clean, whether entrances to buildings are closed – if we make life as uncomfortable for them as possible, there will also be fewer rats," Põhja-Tallinn district elder Manuela Pihlap said.

Pest control specialist Meelis Lepp said it should not be delayed.
"Right now is the last chance. If you start in November or, even worse, in February, it's already too late. By then they're in your home, or they've already chewed through the wires, or they've eaten the vegetables," Lepp said.
In apartment buildings, poison bait boxes can be placed near the building.
"A recommendation here as well is that if you start using poison, residents of several neighbouring buildings should do it at the same time, because rats are smart animals. If they realise that bad things happen to them in one place, it's easy enough for them to move to another building or woodshed," central Tallinn resident Indrek Hirs said.

How Germany supports recovery of Berlin Pride attack victims
August 16, 2026
On the evening of July 25, according to German authorities, Abdul Ballout, drove a white van into a crowd in Berlin's Tiergarten park near the Brandenburg Gate, where thousands were celebrating Berlin Pride. He then got out of the vehicle and began attacking people at random with a large knife. He killed a 65-year-old woman from Poland and injured her daughter, along with 30 other people, some of them seriously.
Many details have emerged about the perpetrator, his links to extremists, his criminal record and his family. Two days after the incident, he was shot dead by police officers while trying to flee during an arrest attempt.
The identities of the victims, and how they are coping after the attack on the event celebrating LGBTQ+ rights, are not publicly known. Still, many are likely to live with its consequences for months, if not years, to come. The only details disclosed by Berlin's governing mayor concern the age and nationality of the woman who was killed. Otherwise, Germany's strict data protection laws protect victims' privacy.
Victims can approach the media themselves at any time if they choose to make their stories public, Roland Weber, the German federal government's commissioner for victims of terrorism and extremist violence, told DW.
Support for the victims
In Germany, people affected by such attacks are often reluctant to come forward publicly or identify themselves as victims, says Julia Kühne. She should know: Kühne is the spokesperson for the Association for Victims of Terrorism (VoT Germany), which is part of an international federation of terrorism victims.
"The perpetrator needs to disappear from the media, now that he has been caught and killed. Those affected should naturally be brought much more to the forefront," Kühne told DW.
How are those who were injured in the attack doing today? Weber has visited some of them in hospital.
"What I saw there was distressing. But thanks to modern medicine, and because the doctors are optimistic, there is reason for hope that everyone will recover as fully as possible," he said. "In some cases, however, that remains uncertain. Only time will tell. Some of the injured have already been discharged from hospital and are now receiving outpatient treatment. Others will begin various forms of therapy and rehabilitation."
How are the victims being supported?
The Federal Commissioner for Victims and "Weisser Ring", a victim support organization, arrange trauma treatment, provide immediate financial assistance, and advise victims on long-term care and victims' pensions.
Weisser Ring is a volunteer-run organization that works closely with the police to provide advice and support to victims of crime in Germany.
"In cases of terrorist attacks, there is also a special provision: victims can apply for hardship payments," says Weber. "These are funds made available by the Bundestag. Hardly anyone knows about them, and making people aware of them is part of our job. We provide guidance and help ensure that applications are submitted as quickly as possible."
Weber is also responsible for supporting the relatives of the Polish woman who was killed and her daughter, who was injured in the attack. Nationality is not a factor in this regard. He assists victims with follow-up costs and compensation whenever an act of terrorism or extremist violence has been committed on German soil.
Number of traumatized victims still unclear
The victims of the attack are not only those who sustained physical injuries. According to VoT Germany's Kühne, even people who witnessed the attack from several hundred meters away may be considered victims.
"There are people who do not develop problems until years later," she said. "They may suddenly begin to suffer from sleep disturbances, experience nightmares, develop panic attacks or struggle with social phobia. In some cases, these symptoms do not emerge until years afterward because those affected were initially either still in shock or had returned to their everyday lives and were therefore able, at least to some extent, to suppress what they had experienced."
Kühne also notes that survivors of previous terrorist attacks may be retraumatized by media coverage of the current attack in Berlin and find themselves in need of support once again. She knows this from personal experience: she herself survived the 2016 attack on Berlin's Christmas market, which ultimately claimed 13 lives and left more than 70 injured.
Victim support: Problems in practice
Weisser Ring, which supports victims of crime across Germany, believes that adequate compensation schemes and pension entitlements for victims of terrorism are now in place. However, not everything runs smoothly in practice, says Rolf Schmachtenberg, the organization's state chairman in Berlin.
"In cases where people have suffered permanent harm, the assessment procedures are often quite lengthy. We also continue to encounter administrative bottlenecks, with some authorities still relying on paper files and fax communications. They then have to wait for information from other agencies, which can significantly delay the application process. In my view, the main problem is one of implementation."
Over the past decade, Germany has experienced 13 terrorist attacks motivated by Islamist or political extremist ideologies, leaving 39 people dead and 475 injured. The support provided by the federal government's commissioner for victims has no time limit. Roland Weber continues to assist people affected by attacks dating back to the 1980s.
Victims seek answers
For many victims, coming to terms with the horror and suffering also means finding answers to the question of why the perpetrator acted and why they, in particular, were targeted, says Weber. In some court proceedings, perpetrators have remained silent. In the case of the Berlin Pride attack, however, the perpetrator was killed, meaning there will never be complete clarity about his motives.
In Germany, investigations are discontinued once a suspect has died. No court will ever determine whether Abdul Ballout selected his victims for one particular reason or another.
"As a result, some questions will remain unanswered,"Weber told DW. "The search for answers is something most victims have in common, but it cannot always lead to certainty."

The Hallertau, in the heart of the southeast German region, is the largest continuous hop-growing area in the world. Dating back to at least 860 AD, it is home to 722 of the country’s remaining hop farms.
One of them is run by fourth-generation hop grower Josef Wimmer. “The years are getting hotter and drier,” he tells Euronews Earth.
To secure the future of his farmstead in the small hamlet of Neuhub near Osseltshausen, drastic action was a necessity.
Could solar 'hats' save German hop farms?
Seven metres above the ground on Wimmer’s farm, solar panels glint, forming a sea of pale blue.
“We wanted to shade the hops because hops are a shade-loving plant,” the farmer explains. Heat slows the production of alpha acids, the compound that gives beer its pleasantly aromatic bitterness. Heat-related drought and the ensuing decline in soil health can also spark dramatic drops in yield.
Of course, the photovoltaic modules come with another key benefit: providing renewable energy for the farm. “In the first step we covered five hectares of hops with PV,” says Wimmer. “That gives us an output of two megawatts at its peak.”
He first came up with the idea 10 years ago, after seeing the benefits of rooftop solar he installed in 2009. His neighbours were awash with skepticism: “There were voices saying ‘Wimmer’s lost it now, that’ll never work!’,” he recalls.
“When we started building, we became the hot spot of the Hallertau. Everyone came by to have a look. And after every big storm… everyone wanted to know whether the installation was still standing or had been blown away,” Wimmer laughs. “But everything is still standing, even after the storms.”
After the curious neighbours came the hop farmers from more distant villages to inspect the system. They were followed by local councillors and district officials, right up to ministers from the Bavarian state capital Munich.
Now Wimmer plans to expand his successful project over 20 hectares and add a one-megawatt battery storage system.
How red tape is slowing farm solar installations
He got there with the help of Bernhard Gruber, a former Airbus engineer turned solar innovator who ran with Willmer’s unconventional idea.
After two years of planning and permitting negotiations, a five kilometre power cable is being installed to connect the panels to a substation capable of sending excess energy to the grid.
“Long approval procedures are a problem. They put some farmers off going for agri-PV on a larger scale, because the bureaucratic effort is simply too great for many of them,” Gruber tells Euronews Earth.
“Especially for hop growing, but also for other agricultural specialty crops, the decisions and approvals could be made much more easily. Why things aren’t moving faster is honestly a mystery to me,” he adds.
Agri-PV keeps soil moist – and hops like that
“We’ve found that the soil has become wetter since the agri-PV modules were installed,” Wimmer says, pointing to a moisture sensor tucked among the trellises, which takes measurements both at the surface and deeper down.
“Evaporation is lower, we can keep the water from rainfall in the soil for longer – and that is good for the hops, especially in June, July and August. That’s when the hops need water.”
Balancing the right amount of shade with a solar panel angle conducive to energy production from the sun is no easy feat.
Gruber puzzled over daily and seasonal movements of the sun, hourly fluctuations in power prices, and the outcomes for the crops.
Too much shade resulted in the hop plants producing bushy shoots in the shaded upper zone – but fewer cones. Overall yields fell and less hop extract was produced.
After two years of tweaks, he achieved a happy medium, settling on a 45-degree angle for the panels instead of the original 20 degrees. This means the panels receive slightly less direct sunlight, but the hops produce more cones.
Less energy but higher income
Despite producing less energy, the steeper panels actually earn Willmer more money.
If the modules are almost flat (as in the first prototype), the PV system generates most power around midday, precisely at the time when there is already surplus power in the grid and producer prices are accordingly low.
Positioned at a steeper angle, the solar system starts working efficiently from the early morning, as soon as the sun rises, or, depending on orientation, in the late afternoon and early evening – the times when electricity prices are high.
The 45-degree solution also boosts energy production in the winter, when the sun sits low on the horizon for longer periods.
“And in winter in particular you need every kilowatt hour,” says Gruber.
“At the moment we’re looking at a payback period of around 14 years, and the PV system will last at least 30 years,” the engineer calculates. “So you can imagine how much you can earn.”
Cutting-edge research in the hop garden
Wimmer and Gruber are not alone out in the fields. Their pilot project is the subject of several research programmes. Their cooperation and funding partners include the University of Applied Sciences Weihenstephan–Triesdorf, the internationally renowned Fraunhofer Institute for Solar Energy Systems in Freiburg, and the Federal Office for Agriculture and Food.
The Technical University of Munich, with its agricultural research station at Dürnast, and the Hop Research Centre in Hüll are also providing scientific support for the agri-PV project. Last but not least, the Agriculture Ministry is funding the three-year trial phase with €1.4 million in research money.
Analysis by Weihenstephan–Triesdorf university reveals that Wimmer’s agri-PV installation has resulted in a 10 to 20 per cent reduction in hop yields. But the farmer says these losses are more than made up for: “The profit from electricity generation is excellent, really very good. The strong power yield offsets the lower hop yield… It’s worth it.”
And with only two grams of hop extract needed to brew one litre of beer, one kilo of hops can be turned into 500 litres.
“In the end this is a win-win situation,” agrees Gruber. “You’re not only earning money from electricity, you’re also earning secure money from the hop harvest.”
“Even in the part of the year when no hops are growing, the soil is still partially shaded by the PV modules,” he adds. “This keeps the soil’s moisture profile more uniform overall – and that means the land is better prepared for the next planting season.”
Researchers are now testing whether the significant improvements in soil health beneath 20-degree panels are retained under a 45-degree shade.
Could agri-PV slow the decline of farming in Europe’s south?
As human-caused global warming continues to shift Europe’s climate, traditional growing areas, developed over centuries, may have to be abandoned for cooler climes.
It’s a pattern already troubling winemakers, and bringing new opportunities for vineyards in places like Scandinavia and the UK.
Bavaria’s hop farmers jokingly quip among themselves that in 30 years’ time the hops for good Bavarian beer may have to be imported from Norway. Sooner or later, some hop farmers in the Hallertau fear it could turn into bitter reality.
Farmers are desperately searching for solutions but not everyone can afford to install costly irrigation systems, which are also politically controversial due to their high water use.
Some hop growers are experimenting with cover crops between the hop rows to reduce evaporation, while researchers are breeding varieties that can cope better with drought.
Others have been forced to bid a reluctant farewell to the hop business – in some cases carpeting their former farmland with solar panels instead.
Wimmer has thought about this too: “I could have said, fine, I’ll rip out my hops and install ground-mounted solar instead of hop gardens everywhere. But then I’d no longer be a farmer, just a PV entrepreneur,” he says.
‘Energy must never compete with food production’
“For me it’s about preserving hop-growing on my land,” says Wimmer. “I want to hand the farm on to the next generation in a way that gives it security and a future – but with additional income from photovoltaics.”
On a broader scale, adds Gruber, “Energy must never compete with food production” – and agri-PV could be an important way to ensure both thrive in harmony.
Already, agri-PV trials have cropped up over vineyards, berry patches, apple orchards and asparagus fields in Germany.
“What we’re doing could become a model for the whole of Europe,” says Gruber, as French hop farmers battle sandy, drifting soils and drought, while Czech, Slovenian, Slovakian, Polish and Austrian hop growers face similar challenges.
With extreme dry spells worsening in Europe – Germany’s ‘drought monitor’ at the Helmholtz Centre for Environmental Research shows extreme droughts in 1976, 2003 and then almost continuously since 2018 – solutions like Wimmer’s could be a lifeline.

Exploring Andorra: The best things to do in the microstate aside from skiing and shopping

The highest capital city on the continent, Andorra la Vella boasts historic buildings, impressive sculptures and the largest thermal spa in southern Europe.
When I told my hairdresser I’d just come back from a trip to Andorra, their immediate response was, “I couldn’t point to it on a map”.
Given how little the country hits the headlines, that’s hardly surprising.
Located in the east Pyrenees between France and Spain, some 85,000 people call Andorra home, with around 8 million tourists descending on the country each year. To put that into context, France, the world’s most-visited country, has 102 million tourists a year, with Spain also potentially hitting 100 million visitors in 2026.
Spanning some 468 square kilometres, it’s technically the largest of the six European microstates (the others being Vatican City, Monaco, Malta, Liechtenstein and San Marino), but it’s not exactly on the typical tourist route – unless you’re a skier, that is.
Ahead of my trip in July this year, its ski resorts were the only thing I associated with the country. And, given I have zero experience in the winter sports department, it’s not exactly been on the top of my to-visit list.
Still, with more and more of us opting for lesser-known destinations as tourist hotspots grapple with overtourism, I thought it was high time – quite literally, as Andorra la Vella is the highest capital city in Europe – for me to see what’s on offer outside of the slopes.
Things to do in Andorra la Vella
Snap a photo with Salvador Dalí’s La Noblesse du Temps
In tourist destinations across the globe, there are certain “shots” that are a must-capture when visiting. In Santorini, it’s the blue domes of Oia; in Dubai, it’s the Burj Khalifa as seen from near the fountains.
In Andorra, the frame centres on Salvador Dalí’s La Noblesse du Temps statue with the Puente de Paris in the background, helpfully decorated with a large “Andorra la Vella” sign, in case it wasn’t clear where you were.
Thankfully, you won’t have to battle crowds to get a good shot of the sculpture, which was installed in its current spot in 2010 having been gifted to the Andorra government by Enric Sabater, a close friend of the artist, in 1999.
The bronze work, which weighs around 1,400 kilos, features a melting clockface set on a tree trunk. The crown above the clock “represents the dominion of time over humanity”, according to the Andorra La Vella tourism board.
Walk the Rec del Solà
For another spectacular view of Andorra La Vella, consider walking the Rec del Solà.
The short route runs along an irrigation channel in the hills near the capital, offering views of the city from above, and you can find exact details for the trail on the Visit Andorra website.
The website advises it takes up to three hours, but I completed it in less than an hour. From the end of the trail, you can reach the pretty old quarter in just 15 minutes.
Learn about the country’s history at Casa de la Vall
Speaking of the Barri Antic, aka the old quarter, if you only visit one museum or gallery during your trip, make it Casa de la Vall.
The historic home dates back to 1580, and served as the seat of the General Council of Andorra until 2011.
Here, you can learn about the origins of the co-principality, with the current heads of state being the Bishop of Urgell and the President of France, as well as the history of the flag and coat of arms. You’ll also be able to see the cabinet of the seven keys, which has locks for each of the parishes of Andorra.
Next door, you can see the new parliament, and you can also visit Església de Sant Esteve, one of the country’s most historic churches.
See the 7 Poets
In the plaza below the new parliament, you’ll be able to see another of Andorra’s most recognisable artworks: 7 Poets by Jaume Plensa.
Installed in 2014, the seven fibreglass figures have been placed on pedestals and are lit up at night.
According to the artist, “Poets are an integral part of society. They are condemned to failure, because they know they won’t even sell three books, but they are a source of fertility, like a light drizzle that penetrates the earth. In my opinion, this long process, akin to that of a farmer, is highly courageous.”
Splash the cash on the Shopping Mile
With some of the lowest rates of VAT in Europe, Andorra is known for its shopping.
Plenty cross the border to do their weekly food shop, but the main Shopping Mile is also home to many well-known international clothing brands, if that’s your cup of tea.
Explore the country’s spa heritage through the Caldes Project
The final ‘S’ associated with Andorra after skiing and shopping is spas.
The natural hot water springs found in Escaldes were originally used for the country’s textile industry until the first thermal hotels were opened here in the mid-19th century.
Today, you can explore that history with a walk alongside Javier Balmaseda’s Water and Form, which features a series of pipes, fountains and washhouses next to the river between the Engordany Bridge and the Tosca Bridge.
If you care less about the history and more about experiencing the springs for yourself, book a visit to Caldea, the largest thermal spa in southern Europe.
See the fountain show in front of Puente de Paris
As night falls, the Passeig de les Fonts de la Rotonda promenade in front of Puente de Paris plays host to a water and light fountain show.
The time differs depending on the season (more information can be found on the Andorra La Vella website), as does the choice of music, but no matter which songs play, it’s a nice way to end a day of exploration.
Where to eat and drink in Andorra
Andorra is known for its bordes, aka farmhouse restaurants, which are typically found outside of the city centre.
If you’re looking for a quick energy boost via carbs that won’t cost an arm and a leg, swing by Laganum, which serves freshly made pasta.
Next time I’ll visit Andorra for…
As picturesque as Andorra is in the summer, I imagine a fresh sprinkling of snow makes the scenery even prettier.
Just don’t expect to see me on the slopes. Unless it’s for après-ski drinks…
How to get to Andorra
The nearest major international airports to Andorra are Toulouse–Blagnac and Josep Tarradellas Barcelona–El Prat.
On the way to Andorra, I took a bus from Lleida, which took around two and a half hours. For the return journey, I went to Barcelona, which took around three hours. I booked both buses through Andbus.

Some developers would rip the soul out of a 1930s banking hall, but Kinz, a new all-day Lebanese brasserie housed in a former Lloyds Bank in Notting Hill, instead makes a feature of its chunky vault door. Where W11 residents once kept their cash, diners can now take a seat and sip Lebanese wine from the Bekaa valley. After all, what visit to the bank wouldn’t be improved by a cold glass of Chateau Musar Jeune?
This triple-height room with mezzanine seating is a very different proposition from all those tiny, homespun Lebanese restaurants typically anchored around Edgware Road since the 1970s. Kinz may offer kibbeh nayyeh (finely minced raw lamb and beef with bulgur, spices and pine nuts), shish barak (tiny lamb and herb dumplings in yoghurt sauce) and warak enab (lamb-stuffed vine leaves and baby courgettes), as do many places in pockets across west London, but there is something altogether more cinematic about this elegant setting.
I sat with my pot of mint tea thinking that the place has all the hallmarks of a 1980s Duran Duran video, in which the boys pitch up somewhere sophisticated in Lebanon for mysterious hijinks. Kinz’s mezzanine seems almost built for John Taylor to sip a babunaj negroni (Hayman’s gin, Lillet Blanc, Suze and chamomile) under his tousled fringe. Large-format photographs of Lebanese life line the walls, including one of an impressive matriarch on a visit to the hair salon.
Even so, Kinz is far from showy – in fact, it feels a bit like an upmarket but still everyday Lebanese canteen. Pop in for a plate of spinach fatayer and end up staying for the Sunday special molokhiye – slow-simmered jute mallow with chicken, rice, pitta and a sharp, mignonette-style sauce.
Fans of Akub, the well-loved modern Palestinian restaurant just a few streets away, may well recognise the name of that formidable restaurateur, Rasha Khouri Bruzzo. Here, she has joined forces with brothers Jad and Karim Lahoud, both Londoners raised by Lebanese chef parents. The result is a deeply personal space, where the chocolate cake is the Lahouds’ mother Elene’s recipe – she’s even been known to turn up in the kitchen of a morning simply to test that this gooey, dark chocolate sponge is up to her standards.
The vibe across much of the menu, meanwhile, is elegant sparseness: nothing is over-garnished or served with frippery. Mint tea comes in tiny, functional, stainless-steel pots. Cheese rikakat (filo pastry cigars) arrive on a plain plate looking for all the world like M&S party food … until you bite in and find them hot, crisp and delicately seasoned with fresh mint. I reckon I could eat at least 17 of these things in one sitting. It would not be pretty, but it would be a testament to their beige deliciousness.
Rich hummus comes topped with tahini, with the option of adding pine nuts or awarma, a spiced, slow-cooked minced lamb. There is baba ganoush, obviously, but also moutabal, or smoky chargrilled aubergine with tahini. Cod fillets come with walnuts and tahini sauce. All are understated, yet effective: no bells, no whistles, just Lebanese food cooked with immense pride and an urge to be accurate.
The staff are a genuine delight – warm, knowledgable, unhurried – and the food is definitely decent. You might even mistake the fattet aubergine, baked in tomato sauce and served on yoghurt with broken toasted pitta, for parmigiana, but they are very different beasts. Parmigiana is rich, oily, cheesy and baked into submission. Fattet, however, which in Arabic means “crumbs”, is built on crisp bread soaked in yoghurt, and as a result is much lighter, sharper and altogether more refreshing.
The slow-cooked spiced chicken with rice is a standout: home cooking in the most complimentary sense, with properly stewed bird, roast almonds, a sharp sourness running through the rice, and a rich gravy served from a long-handled, copper-coloured pan.
For pudding, we ordered osmalieyeh, or crisp kataifi pastry layered with ashta clotted cream and orange blossom syrup, which is delicate, not over-sweet and a gentle way to end. But it is Elene’s chocolate cake that is the real star of the dessert show: dense, dark, damp and oozing. We even ordered a second piece to take home, complete with specific instructions not to refrigerate it or Elene would be cross. I’ve never met the woman, but I was suitably chastened.
  Kinz was busy as heck on the lunchtime we visited, and it’s plain to see why. I’ve been recommending it to anyone with a large group to feed in west London. There is a very real sense that this re-loved bank and its new inhabitants are here to stay. Kinz will get my trade any time I’m passing – and I will always order that chocolate cake.

Kėdainiai: A Lithuanian cultural capital shaped by Scots and the Radziwills

14:00:00 KST

This year, the title of Lithuanian Capital of Culture belongs to Kėdainiai, a historic town once ruled by the powerful Radziwill family and shaped by centuries of encounters between different peoples and faiths.
Located in central Lithuania, Kėdainiai is perhaps best known as the country’s “cucumber capital”. But its compact historic centrr offers a richer story, with centuries-old churches and synagogues, distinctive architecture, a forgotten river port and traditional crafts that are still practiced today.
The town’s multicultural heritage is particularly visible in its houses of worship.
According to Rimantas Žirgulis, director of the Kėdainiai Regional Museum, the town was especially diverse during its period of prosperity in the 17th century, when six ethnic and religious communities lived there.
Today, visitors can see an Orthodox church housed in a brick building dating to the second half of the 19th century. The Church of the Transfiguration of Christ retains early 20th-century paintings and valuable icons.
Two Catholic churches also form part of Kėdainiai’s architectural heritage.
The Gothic Church of St George, built in the 15th century, is one of Lithuania’s oldest churches. It contains notable works of art, late Baroque altars and a relic of Blessed Teofilius Matulionis.
The wooden Church of St Joseph, dating to the 18th century, stands with its bell tower near the centre of town. It is known for paintings of St Joseph and the Virgin Mary with the infant Jesus, both featuring ornate metal coverings.
“The Church of St George is a unique 15th-century brick church, one of the oldest in Lithuania. The Church of St Joseph is wooden and dates to the 18th century,” Žirgulis told LRT.lt. “It is also unusual that what looks like a village church stood in the middle of what was, for its time, a large town.”
Kėdainiai’s religious heritage also reflects its once-powerful Protestant communities.
A 17th-century Evangelical Reformed church contains a mausoleum of the Radziwill family and historic pipe organs. The mausoleum was the first restored 17th-century burial site of Lithuanian Grand Duchy nobles in the country. It contains the sarcophagi of several members of the Radziwill family, including Krzysztof I “Perkūnas”, Janusz II, Mikolaj, Jerzy, Stepan and Elzbieta.
The church was once the principal Evangelical Reformed church in Samogitia and later became the main church of the Lithuanian Reformed community, Žirgulis said.
Another landmark is the 17th-century brick Evangelical Lutheran church, which retains original frescoes depicting the apostles Peter and Paul and the four evangelists.
The church reflects the influence of Kėdainiai’s German Lutheran community, Žirgulis said. It stood in Jonušava, then a suburb or separate settlement named after Radziwill nobleman Janusz Radziwill.
The town’s Jewish heritage is equally prominent.
On Senosios Rinkos Square stands a synagogue complex consisting of the Great Summer Synagogue and the Small Winter Synagogue. The Great Synagogue was rebuilt in the Baroque style after a fire in the 18th century and now houses the Kėdainiai Art School. The 19th-century Neoclassical Small Synagogue houses a multicultural centre with an exhibition about the town’s Jewish community and the Holocaust.
The complex is one of only three surviving synagogue complexes in Lithuania.
A historic town centre
The entire Old Town of Kėdainiai is a major attraction. It is a state-protected urban heritage site and one of just seven such sites in Lithuania.
Its compact streets preserve elements of Baroque, Gothic, Renaissance and Neoclassical architecture. Four historic market squares – Senosios Rinkos, Didžiosios Rinkos, Knypavos Rinkos and Janušavos – testify to the town’s role as a regional commercial centre.
Among the landmarks is the former 17th-century town hall on Didžiosios Rinkos Square, one of only three surviving historic town halls in Lithuania.
The Old Town also preserves houses associated with the Kėdainiai Scottish community, including the Grey House.
Another important landmark is Šviesioji Gymnasium, which dates to the middle of the 17th century. The Radziwills planned to develop it into a Protestant institution of higher education, although the ambitious project never came to fruition.
Janusz Radziwill had elevated Kėdainiai’s four-grade school to gymnasium status and hoped eventually to turn it into a Protestant academy capable of competing with the Jesuit academy in Vilnius, Žirgulis said.
When the River Nevėžis was a trade route
Kėdainiai’s history was also shaped by the Nevėžis River.
The site of the former Kėdainiai port, near the present-day Mikalojus Daukša Public Library, recalls the town’s history as a centre of commercial shipping.
Flat-bottomed sailing cargo vessels known as vytinės once carried goods along the river. From Kėdainiai, they travelled to Konigsberg, carrying local and Lithuanian raw materials and returning with a variety of goods and luxury products.
The town’s history of craftsmanship can still be experienced at the Traditional Crafts Centre, housed in the former Arnet House associated with the Scottish community of Kėdainiai.
The centre displays folk art and hosts workshops where visitors can learn traditional crafts that are increasingly rare, Žirgulis said.
The building also has a long religious history. From the second half of the 17th century until the end of the 19th century, it served as the residence of the superintendents of the Evangelical Reformed Church in Samogitia – effectively a regional religious centre.
Kedainiai was also an important centre of Jewish life in Samogitia, with the region’s chief rabbis living in the town.
Traces of that heritage can still be found along Žydų (Jewish) Street, including a surviving house at No. 2 with a wooden annex used for the Jewish festival of Sukkot.
The roof of the structure could be opened, allowing residents to pray beneath the sky without leaving the house, Žirgulis explained. Sukkot commemorates the biblical story of the Israelites’ 40 years of wandering in the desert after leaving Egypt.
A year as cultural capital
Kėdainiai was designated Lithuania’s Capital of Culture for 2026. The yearlong program, titled Kėdainiai Chronicles in Cultural Symbols, highlights the town’s history as a Radziwill city where different ethnic and religious communities lived side by side and where crafts, education and cultural exchange flourished.
The program includes more than 30 projects and 150 events.
August’s program includes the exhibition Universe From a Spiral by Nijolė Šivickas de Mockus, a national painters’ plein air event titled Kėdainiai on Both Sides of the Nevėžis, an exhibition of Impressionist graphics, a traditional weaving project titled Threads That Connect, and a ceramics workshop, Radziwill Symbols in Clay, among other events.

Athens is cleaning its all-marble Olympic stadium for the first time in more than a century
ATHENS, Greece (AP) — It has welcomed marathon runners, rock stars and the first modern Olympic athletes. Now it’s time for a monumental scrub.
Athens’ U-shaped Panathenaic Stadium — made entirely of white marble — is getting its first systematic cleaning in more than a century.
Rebuilt for the first modern Olympics in 1896, the site is the ceremonial send-off point for the Olympic flame, including for Los Angeles 2028.
A fresh look in Athens ahead of LA 2028
The stadium is made of the same glittering marble used on the Acropolis, making the cleanup job delicate.
The work is revealing a monument changed by time, weather and millions of visitors. Cleaners in white and blue overalls move across the stadium’s 47 steep tiers with thin spray lances, using clean water at carefully controlled, low pressure. Machines vacuum up the runoff as they go.
“The goal of cleaning is not to make everything white. It’s to remove the dirt and the first layers of organic material that are deposited on the surface,” said Dimitris Selemis, a senior conservator at the site.
“The idea is to clean all the material that (contains) dirt from the surface, so we have a better understanding of the monument,” he said, noting that there is more weed growth and dirt in areas that receive less direct sunlight.
Dealing with chewing gum, high heels and soft-drink stains
Crews are also using steam to remove blotches of chewing gum, which have turned black over time. Stadium officials say chewing gum, high heels and soft-drink stains are among the main culprits that stain the marble.
Now slowly being revealed, the original gleaming look of the venue earned it the widely used Greek nickname “Kallimarmaro,” or beautiful marble.
“We call it Kallimarmaro because it is made of the finest marble, the best that was ever produced ... We are very proud of it,” Isidoros Kouvelos, president of the Hellenic Olympic Committee, told The Associated Press in an interview.
“It needed to be cleaned of everything that has accumulated over all these years,” he said. “This way the Panathenaic Stadium will show all its brilliance and will once again be the foremost monument of humanity for the Olympic Movement.”
Built into a natural ravine, the stadium’s site was used for ancient Greek athletic contests and later Roman spectacles before falling into centuries of disuse.
An African celebration planned in Athens
Since its 1896 revival, it has staged fashion shows, rock concerts, boxing matches and national celebrations — as well as lavish patriotic ceremonies ordered by a military dictatorship in the 1960s.
Today, it’s a popular draw for tourists. Apart from the cleanup, the Kallimarmaro is also getting a new track, upgraded lighting and fresh support facilities. The project is privately funded by a shipping company and overseen by the Hellenic Olympic Committee with guidance from Culture Ministry experts.
The work will continue through 2026 but is currently concentrated on the stadium’s “sling,” or curved end, in time for the next torch-passing ceremony on Sept. 10.
That will be for the Dakar Youth Olympics, scheduled for Oct. 31-Nov. 13 in Senegal, which will be the first Olympic competition ever hosted in Africa.
IOC President Kirsty Coventry, a swimming gold medal winner from Zimbabwe, is due to attend the Sept. 10 ceremony, Kouvelos said.

MIAMI (AP) — The Miami Marine Stadium, considered a masterpiece of midcentury Brutalist architecture with its massive cantilevered roof and a stunning view of the city, opened in 1963 as the nation’s only race venue for powerboats, a major symbol of South Florida.
The historic gem designed by a Cuban refugee was the backdrop of Elvis Presley’s 1967 film “Clambake” and a beloved venue of Gloria Estefan and the late Jimmy Buffett, who performed on a movable, floating stage.
But since closing in 1992 after suffering serious damage during Hurricane Andrew, the 6,500-seat venue has fallen into disarray and today is fenced off and covered in graffiti despite being listed on the U.S. National Register of Historic Places.
On Tuesday, voters will decide whether to allow the city to partner with a private company to return the unique stadium situated on Virginia Key across the water from downtown Miami to its former glory.
President Richard Nixon and Sammy Davis Jr. appeared together at the stadium during a 1972 campaign rally to support Nixon’s reelection bid. And residents would gather for Easter sunrise services, as well as celebrate Our Lady of Charity, a representation of the Virgin Mary honored by many Cubans and Cuban Americans.
“It encapsulated a time period in Miami that’s incredibly unique with an incredible story that touches all Miamians,” Miami Commissioner Damian Pardo said. “So this is really a place that connects with Miami at a very deep and personal level.”
The stadium has a rich history in Miami
Hilario Candela was in his late 20s and a recent refugee from the Cuban Revolution when he designed the structure. He spent decades designing buildings around Miami and led community efforts to restore the marine stadium until his death in 2022.
When it was added to the National Park Service’s National Register of Historic Places in 2018, then-Secretary of State Ken Detzner called the stadium “one of the state of Florida’s most unique architectural and historic resources,” and “an ambitious example of mid-century Modernist architecture” that represented the “multi-cultural influences evolving in the city of Miami during the 1960s.”
The year before his 2023 passing, Buffett wrote a letter to Miami officials, urging them to save the “little cathedral of musical fun.” The Mayor of Margaritaville described a 1985 concert at the marine stadium that ended with him jumping off the floating stage into the surrounding water, joining cheering swimmers and boaters.
“Those were the ‘Miami Vice’ days, and Don Johnson introduced us,” Buffett wrote of the actor in the popular 1980s television crime drama. “WITCO Desperadoes from Trinidad opened the show, and the Coral Reefer Band finished the night with what I think was one of the most fun shows, for band and fans, that I have ever done.”
Buffett’s daughter, Savannah, continues to work with Restore Miami Marine Stadium. The group’s co-founder, Donald Worth, began a mission to save the stadium in 2008, shortly after city officials at the time announced plans to tear it down.
“The architecture gives it the wow, the history gives it its soul,” Worth said. “It’s just a special, magical place, and so many people in Miami have had such memorable experiences there.”
Voters will help decide the stadium’s future
Breakwater Hospitality Group co-founder Emi Guerra, who’s working with the company selected to partner with the city, said the stadium’s view of the city skyline and its location right off Biscayne Bay could make it an entertainment destination that rivals Denver’s Red Rocks Amphitheatre, an open-air venue built into the side of a mountain.
“There’s just something here that can’t be duplicated, and that’s the view,” Guerra said of the marine stadium.
Guerra said he can understand the cynicism felt by some voters, but he points out that this isn’t a developer land grab to build more condos. The city of Miami will retain complete ownership of the stadium and surrounding area, and the management company will be stewards of the city’s vision, Guerra said.
“When it’s something that’s historic, you have two options: restore it or let it rot,” Guerra said.
Ernesto Cuesta, head of the Brickell Homeowners Association, sees another option.
He said the group supports the preservation of a historical landmark, but members are worried about how noise and traffic from a large-scale event space will affect residents across the bay and nearby wildlife. He said he’d prefer a park with more green spaces.
Restoration plans aim to honor the stadium’s past
Despite sitting unused for more than three decades, multiple studies have shown that the stadium remains structurally safe.
Project architect Richard Heisenbottle, who has previously worked to restore other Miami landmarks, said he hopes reviving the marine stadium will honor its original architect.
He said renovations will have to include modern amenities and accessibility requirements, but the galvanized steel rebar used to reinforce the concrete during its original construction has protected the stadium from serious deterioration. Completed construction documents for the restorations have already been permitted by the city of Miami, he said.
Miami Commissioner Pardo said a significant hurdle to reopening the stadium previously has been the city government simply not having the capacity to properly run a major event space.
The referendum going before Miami voters on Tuesday would allow the city to enter into a management agreement.
The private company would develop the stadium’s large parking area into a flex park that includes public recreational facilities and a special events space. The city would collect revenue from events to fund the stadium repairs and upgrades, while paying the company a management fee. The total cost of the project hasn’t been determined, but Pardo insisted it will not be borne by taxpayers at all.
“It’s the right time to finally do this,” Pardo said.

Mahuchikh wins gold in high jump at 2026 European Athletics Championships
According to Ukrinform, Poland's Maria Zodzik won silver with a clearance of 1.95 meters, while Montenegro's Marija Vukovic took bronze at 1.95 meters, finishing lower based on the number of attempts.
Ukraine's Iryna Herashchenko finished fifth with a result of 1.92 meters.
This is Ukraine's fourth medal at the 2026 European Athletics Championships. Earlier, Mykhailo Kokhan won bronze in the hammer throw, Oleh Doroshchuk took silver in the high jump, and Liudmyla Olianovska won bronze in the half marathon race walk with a national record.
At the previous European Championships in Rome in 2024, Ukrainian athletes won six medals: one gold (Yaroslava Mahuchikh), one silver (Vladyslav Lavskyy), and four bronze medals (Oleh Doroshchuk, Mykhailo Kokhan, Iryna Herashchenko, and Liudmyla Olianovska).
The next European Athletics Championships will be held from June 3 to 8, 2028, at the Silesian Stadium in Chorzow, Poland.

Russia attacks Kyiv with ballistic missiles: 3 injured, Petrivka book market on fire – photo
Russian forces attacked Kyiv with ballistic missiles on the night of 15-16 August, causing fires in the capital's Obolonskyi and Holosiivskyi districts.
Source: Kyiv Mayor Vitali Klitschko; State Emergency Service of Ukraine; Kyiv City Military Administration
Quote from Klitschko: "In the Obolonskyi district, debris fell and fires broke out in non-residential premises at two locations as a result of the enemy attack. Several cars are on fire near one of the buildings.
In the Holosiivskyi district, a fire broke out in a non-residential area."
Update: Kyiv City Military Administration reported in the morning that three people had been injured in the Russian attack.
The State Emergency Service reported that a large fire had broken out at the Petrivka book market near Pochaina metro station in the Obolonskyi district.
At another location, the blast wave caused minor damage to a building but no fire. A non-residential building was also hit at a third location.
Background:
- On the night of 15-16 August, Russian forces launched a large-scale attack on several regions of Ukraine using ballistic missiles and attack drones, targeting Kyiv, Kryvyi Rih, Kamianske and Kremenchuk.
- At least five explosions were heard in Kyiv at 02:47.
