A fugitive colonel is finally jailed in Chilean Víctor Jara’s murder case, decades after the crime
A fugitive colonel is finally jailed in Chilean Víctor Jara’s murder case, decades after the crime
SANTIAGO, Chile (AP) — More than half a century passed before some measure of justice was served over the weekend for the murder of popular Chilean singer-songwriter Víctor Jara.
Chilean police on Saturday arrested a retired military colonel who had been a fugitive for three years after being convicted of kidnapping and murdering Jara during Chile’s military dictatorship and sentenced to 25 years in prison.
Despite the arrest, the musician’s family says they are not completely satisfied.
“We learned that retired Colonel Nelson Haase Mazzei, who had been a fugitive from justice since 2023, was found and imprisoned. We appreciate this news,” said Jara’s daughter, Amanda Jara, in a letter published Sunday night. However, she added, “it is difficult to consider this justice.”
The prominent military figure had been sentenced to 25 years and two days in prison for the kidnapping and murder of Jara and then-Director General of Prisons Littré Quiroga Carvajal.
Haase Mazzei, 80, was convicted in 2018 and his sentence was increased in 2023, along with those of six other military officers. The crimes are considered to be among the most emblematic examples of the brutality that marked the 17-year dictatorship of late Gen. Augusto Pinochet.
The former official fled before beginning his prison term. He was finally located and arrested this weekend — three years later — with Judge Paola Plaza of the Santiago Court of Appeals ordering his “immediate imprisonment,” the Judiciary said in a statement. The institution added that Haase Mazzei was the last of those convicted to begin serving his sentence.
One of the symbols of the so-called New Chilean Song, a musical and social movement born in the 1960s, Jara was murdered on Sept. 16, 1973, just five days after the coup that overthrew former President Salvador Allende.
Jara, then a 40-year-old member of the Communist Party of Chile who also worked as a professor at the State Technical University, was arrested on campus and taken to Chile Stadium, now renamed Víctor Jara Stadium. There, he was separated from the nearly 5,000 other prisoners and taken to a private area. Along with Quiroga, he was tortured, violently assaulted and finally executed. The singer suffered multiple fractures and his body had 44 bullet wounds, according to the findings of an investigation into his death and other crimes of the period.
In 1991, months after Pinochet’s downfall and the restoration of democracy, an official report by the National Commission for Truth and Reconciliation found that Jara was the victim of a political execution and classified his killing as a grave human rights violation. It also found that, in just three months, 890 people were killed as a direct consequence of the military coup.
In recent decades, Chile has established a series of truth commissions to investigate and determine responsibility for the violations committed between 1973 and 1990, ranging from murders and torture to forced disappearances and the illegal adoption of children.
Official documents estimate that during its 17 years, the military dictatorship left some 40,000 victims, including more than 2,000 people who were executed, 1,469 victims of enforced disappearance and thousands more tortured. The courts have convicted hundreds of state agents and civilians for the crimes committed during the darkest period of Chile’s modern history. However, there are no single, consolidated institutional figures that include all those sentenced.
Since 2024, the country has also promoted the so-called National Search Plan aimed at unifying the figures and outlining a road map for the search of disappeared detainees whose whereabouts remain unknown — a task that for years has largely fallen to the families of victims who to this date are still trying to figure out what happened with their loved ones.
“We hope that the police will manage to find all those who fled for crimes against humanity,” Amanda Jara concluded in her letter. She now heads the Víctor Jara Foundation.

Russia’s ‘Red Book’ exists to keep rare species alive. After September 1, builders who claim ‘no other site available’ get a legal green light to wipe one out.
The independent Russian data project “To Be Precise” has published an article explaining how Russia’s “Red Book” is compiled, why it exists, and why it periodically touches off scandals. We’re retelling it here, with a few examples and details of our own.
Why the Red Book exists
The Red Book is a list of animals, plants, and fungi whose survival is in doubt. Russia has regional Red Books in addition to the national one. The International Union for Conservation of Nature also maintains a global Red List.
Under Russian law, the state is obligated to protect the species on the national list. Killing, storing, transporting, collecting, keeping, acquiring, or selling a listed animal or plant — or destroying its habitat — carries a fine of up to 5,000 rubles for individuals and up to 1 million rubles for companies and organizations. Russia’s Criminal Code sets out punishments for anyone who:
- destroys habitat that is critical to organisms listed in the Red Book (Article 259);
- destroys, damages, collects, stores, sells, buys, transports, or ships “especially valuable” plants and fungi (Article 260.1);
- kills, keeps in captivity, buys, stores, transports, ships, or sells “especially valuable” mammals, fish, and birds, or their parts (Article 258.1).
Some offenders have been given prison terms rather than suspended sentences: in one case for killing an Amur tiger, in another for selling a tiger carcass.
Who decides what goes in the Red Book
Russia’s Natural Resources Ministry maintains the Red Book and makes the final decision on whether to add a species, refuse to add it, or remove it. The ministry acts on recommendations from the Commission on Rare and Endangered Animals, Plants, and Fungi, which accepts proposals both from scientific institutions and from ordinary members of the public.
The fiercest disputes over what to add and what to drop involve species prized by trophy hunters. Hunters argue that listing a species does nothing on its own to rebuild its population, whereas breeding it on game preserves, where people pay to shoot, does help.
Tatyana Aramileva is probably the loudest voice in that camp. She spent years heading the Russian Hunters and Anglers Union before the Natural Resources Ministry appointed her to head its department for wildlife protection and hunting policy. In 2025, responsibility for the Red Book passed to that department, setting off a major scandal. Oversight ultimately returned to the ministry’s department for specially protected natural areas.
Sometimes Red Book animals cause problems for people, and that can sway the commission. The Volga-Ural population of saiga antelope, for instance, was removed from the book because the animals trample fields and eat crops.
As “To Be Precise” notes, the removal of certain invertebrates — mollusks or annelid worms, for example — may have less to do with their numbers growing than with scientists getting better at finding them.
Which animals are most at risk
The latest edition of Russia’s Red Book of Animals came out in 2020; the edition for plants and fungi followed in 2023. The book now lists 443 animal species, 152 of them added for the first time. A further 122 species were dropped from the previous edition. Half of the new entries are invertebrates and half are vertebrates, mostly birds and reptiles.
Russia’s Red Book classifies animals by rarity into five categories:
- Probably extinct
- Endangered
- Declining in number and/or range, a category accounting for about half of listed species
- Rare
- Status undetermined
Russia currently counts 11 species as probably extinct. The category covers species not seen in their known habitat for a long time — 100 years for invertebrates, 50 years for vertebrates. “The dhole, or Asiatic wild dog, used to be found in southern Siberia and the Far East, but its presence in Russia hasn’t been confirmed for several decades,” the authors of “To Be Precise” write. “Some reported sightings were probably mistaken; the animals were being confused with reddish-coated gray wolves. No one knows for certain why the species disappeared. Scientists point to the use of poison, a decline in hoofed-animal populations, disease, and competition with the gray wolf. The striped hyena met a similar fate: it once lived in southern Dagestan but was last seen there in 1953. Like the dhole, the hyena suffered from a shrinking food supply and from poisoning.”
Animals that have died out altogether are not listed in the Red Book. Over the past 400 years, nine species and subspecies have disappeared from Russian territory, among them the aurochs, the steppe tarpan, and Steller’s sea cow.
What can be done to save these animals
The Red Book also sorts animals, plants, and fungi into a second set of categories that describe how a species is faring in the wild:
- extinct in the wild;
- extinct in the Russian Federation;
- critically endangered;
- endangered;
- vulnerable;
- near threatened;
- of least concern.
And it groups them by the kind of help their populations need:
- immediate protective measures, plus a conservation strategy that is drawn up and carried out;
- one or more specific measures to save the species;
- nothing beyond the measures already in place (the work of nature reserves, for example).
When more drastic action is needed, one option is captive breeding followed by release into the wild, the approach used with Przewalski’s horses.
Since the previous edition of the Red Book, conditions have improved for 19 percent of the animals listed, enough to move them into a different category. The authors of “To Be Precise” offer this example:
The humpback whale was classified in the first category (‘threatened with extinction’) in 1997, but by the 2020 list had moved to the fifth (‘recovered and recovering’). Commercial whaling had almost wiped the species out in Russian waters. But after an international moratorium on whaling took effect in 1986, the humpback population began to recover, and sightings became more frequent, especially since the mid-2000s. There are no exact figures for the population in Russia, but experts estimate that more than 2,000 animals now live in the Far East.
How the rules on Red Book animals, plants, and fungi change in 2026
”Rules allowing the taking of Red Book species in exceptional cases, including for monitoring population status, had been in force since 1997,” the authors of “To Be Precise” write. “But the 2009 law on hunting directly prohibited this, creating a legal contradiction.” Amendments passed at the end of 2020 lifted that ban. New rules on the taking of Red Book species were issued in 2026 and take effect on September 1.
The list of justifications for killing Red Book animals or destroying Red Book plants and fungi has grown considerably. It now covers not only “monitoring population status” but also, for example, “the construction and reconstruction of facilities of federal and regional significance, … as well as facilities provided for under the investment agreements of Russian regions” — provided there is nowhere else to put the project. The change opens major opportunities for developers and mining companies.
“Instead of preserving Red Book species in their natural habitat, the state is proposing a procedure for removing them from the land slated for development,” the Russian environmental outlet Kedr writes. “This is especially dangerous for plants and fungi. They cannot be protected simply by ‘collecting’ or ‘transplanting’ them: many species depend on specific soil, hydrological conditions, mycorrhizal fungi, neighboring species, and the wider ecosystem. Destroying a habitat often means destroying the species itself in that area.”
