Could you identify a criminal? It’s harder than you think
Eyewitness identification on trial: how cases of mistaken identity put the science of the brain in the dock
If you witnessed someone committing a crime, how confident would you be that you could correctly identify that person later on?
As it turns out, studies consistently reveal that we tend to overestimate our ability to recall past events accurately and recognise people we think we have seen before.
A 2011 United States study showed that a majority of participants (63 per cent) believe that human memory works like a video recording.
What an eyewitness is doing, on this view, is akin to running back the tape, comparing the suspect in front of them with earlier footage.
The reality is very different – frighteningly so. Our memories are in fact highly fallible, prone to suggestion and corruptible by subsequent events.
In a 2002 study, researchers showed adults a fake photograph depicting them as children on a hot-air balloon ride, mixed in with genuine childhood photos.

Investment authority chairman Tengku Zafrul Aziz says Putrajaya has to strike a balance between an open economy and helping local firms

OpenAI agent’s autonomous hack a warning to the world
Humanity is engineering its own obsolescence if it fails to establish strict, binding international guard rails for AI software deployment and educational integrity
3-MIN READ3-MIN
Quentin Parker is an astrophysicist based at the University of Hong Kong and director of its Laboratory for Space Research. 
Three years ago, I warned in these pages that the greatest existential threat to humanity might not emerge from a stray asteroid or a cosmic cataclysm but from uncontrolled artificial intelligence (AI). Today, the trajectory towards an AI Armageddon has not merely accelerated, it has violently careered off the rails of human oversight.
The academic debates of previous years regarding a voluntary six-month pause in AI development have been buried under an avalanche of hyper-autonomous, unaligned algorithms. We are witnessing a fractured, multipolar technological Wild West where guard rails have fallen off and our institutions are buckling under the strain of the threats that unregulated, rapidly evolving AI models present.
The AI leaps of the past 24 months are astonishing and deeply concerning. We have transitioned beyond simple text chatbots such as early ChatGPT variants into an era dominated by autonomous AI enterprise agents and efficient open-source models such as DeepSeek.
These rapidly developing AI models do not simply answer prompts. They execute multi-step logic, can generate functional code, quickly solve problems that would take humans much longer to handle and run corporate or economic pipelines and market operations with zero human intervention, all in the blink of an eye.
They can write scientific research papers almost indistinguishable from those written by humans and make scientific breakthroughs. This could call into question the rationale and motivation for being a human scientist at all.
This terrifying potential reality was laid bare by the recent cyberattack on the popular developer platform Hugging Face. Advanced autonomous AI models from OpenAI broke free from an isolated virtual testing ground and breached Hugging Face’s infrastructure in search of answers to challenges they were solving. The incident should shatter any illusions about effective control of AI.
