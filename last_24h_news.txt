The mile world record had been stuck at four minutes, 1.4 seconds for nine years when England's Roger Bannister stepped onto the track at Oxford in May, 1954.
Near the same university where he earned his medical degree, Bannister operated with surgical precision to reach his ultimate goal of becoming the first man to dip under four minutes for the mile.
But, as is so often the case, once a barrier is broken, it reminds others they too can do what just yesterday seemed impossible.
And, sure enough, 45 days later Australian John Landy went around the Turku track in Finland four times in 3:58 flat.
It set the stage for the so-called Miracle Mile at the 1954 British Empire and Commonwealth Games.
It was a moment when the sporting world's eyes were firmly locked on Vancouver, regardless of any connection to the Commonwealth, to watch the best middle distance runners to that point in history face off.
Eight runners made it to the final, but all eyes were on just two as Bannister, who set his sub-four-minute mark of 3:59.8 on a home track with friends pacing for him, would face Landy in the purest competition settings.
The Australian took the lead after one lap and led for the next kilometre and change, going out hard in an attempt to use his superior stamina, as Bannister called it, to negate the Englishman's famous final burst.
Having felt Bannister on his heels and seen his shadow sticking firm under him for the entire race, Landy took one look over his left shoulder about 100 metres out on the final bend, and in that moment Bannister burst past unsighted on the right and flew home to victory.
Both runners finished under four minutes, marking another piece of history.
A 60-year sabbatical
Despite that glorious event, and a race of almost exactly 1,600 metres seeming to make sense on the world-standard 400m track, the mile was only run at three more Commonwealth Games before swapping it out for the 1,500m after 1966.
New Australian record holder Cameron Myers said it can become prestigious once again.
"I think it's a way we can attract more people into the sport," he said.
"Americans still love it, so I think it's just something that regular people can resonate a little bit more with."
At the time of its last Commonwealth outing in Kingston, the Caribbean nations were yet to become the sprint powerhouses we now know them to be, so the mile had been considered the blue-riband event at the Games.
And it returns 60 years later, with Glasgow hoping to host another historic night tonight.
"It's obviously a very prestigious part of the Commonwealth Games and its history, so it's really exciting that it's come back in this year and allowed us to attack it," said Abbey Caldwell, one of three Australian competitors in the women's final.
Caldwell will run alongside Jessica Hull and Claudia Hollingsworth from 6:45am (AEST), and she believes they have a genuine chance of a podium sweep.
"There's always the pressure of you want to go and achieve those medals, and you want to be the first Australians to do that," she said.
"But we're also in a very fortunate position to have three very strong Australians going into this, so it's a really nice opportunity for us to go take those history books and cement them as Australians."
Whether they win them or not, the men (from 5:55am) will get the honour of running for the first Commonwealth Games mile medals in six decades.
And in that race lies a chance for Australia to reverse the misery of Bannister's late pass in 1954.
Young Aussie wants to spoil the party
Including Bannister's first trip under the milestone mark, the mile record was broken 19 times in 44 years, when the great Moroccan Hicham El Guerrouj ran 3:43.13 in 1999.
His record stood for 27 years, which the astute among you may recognise as bringing us up to the here and now.
Well, two weeks ago, to be exact.
Just before the start of the Glasgow Games, Scottish 1,500m world champion Josh Kerr ran 3:42.66.
Just two weeks earlier at prestigious Prefontaine Classic, 20-year-old Myers broke Ollie Hoare's Australian record, running 3:46.06, setting up a tantalising shootout on the mile's return.
"Kerr's going to want to make a move at some point, and he's shown he's the fittest in the field," Myers said.
"I think he'll want to make a move and I'm not going to let him go."
Landy described the Miracle Mile as "like a title fight", and just like a battle for a belt, styles make fights.
We saw a preview in their separate heats, where both won but Myers was five seconds faster than Kerr.
Myers jumped to the front of his pack and led the whole way home with clinical efficiency, while Kerr sat in the pack until the last lap before ambling to the front and revving up the crowd with 150 metres to go.
The Scotstoun Stadium crowd will be baying for Kerr and 2022 world champion in the 1,500m Jake Wightman, but young buck Myers is out to prove himself in an elite field.
"I'm a competitor, I don't want to lose," he said.
"I'm not going there to come second, so I'll have a crack at him the best I can and if I blow up trying, so be it.
"Everyone's cheering for the Scots, and I'm sure they want to go 1-2, so I'm trying to spoil that."
Myers will also be running alongside teammates, with reigning 1,500m Commonwealth champion Ollie Hoare and 24-year-old Adam Spencer making it through.
The final night of the athletics program on the penultimate day of the Games feels like the perfect time for a clash between the hosts and the runaway medal tally toppers.
If the Aussies can ruin the party, they would have done what Landy couldn't 72 years ago.
And Myers is well aware of the opportunity in front of him.
"Hopefully I can reverse the roles."

The United States and Israel are considering implementing a land blockade against Iran to further Tehran's economic isolation, The Telegraph reported on Friday, with US President Donald Trump believing that the naval blockade in the Strait of Hormuz has reached its limit.
According to the report, Trump discussed the plan with Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, along with several other "kinetic and non-kinetic" options, during their meeting in Washington on Tuesday.
“What if you just blocked the land? Suppose Iran can’t bring anything in and cannot take anything out," a senior Israeli official told the Telegraph. "Anything will happen.”
“We are protecting not only our lives but yours as well, and we’re fighting the battle of civilization against barbarism,” the Israeli official said of the Trump-Netanyahu meeting, referring to previous US and Israeli strikes on Iran.
“We took out that lump of cancer because if we hadn’t, we’d be dead, and we didn’t have a choice," the official continued. "But it doesn’t mean that we don’t have to keep on taking out the attempts to do this thing to you know to come back. We’re doing that, and we’re able to do that. But it’s still a challenge.”
A land blockade would likely involve cooperation with Iran's neighboring states, which include Iraq, Turkey, Pakistan, Afghanistan, Turkmenistan, Armenia, and Azerbaijan, the report noted, all of whom would face retaliation from Iran if they chose to participate in the blockade.
Therefore, the plan would require the US and Israel to persuade these states to enact restrictions on the flow of goods bound for or coming from Tehran, according to the Telegraph.
The report comes as Iran attempts to rearm itself following the US and Israeli Operations Epic Fury and Roaring Lion against them earlier this year, including a recent order for Chinese shoulder-launched missile systems.
Retired US general: Economic isolation can bring regime 'to their knees'
The report cited retired US three-star general Sean MacFarland, who noted the significance of a land blockade against Iran, despite describing the endeavor as "almost impossible."
"If you take away Iran’s ability to trade... then you are isolating them economically," said MacFarland. "That’s the way to bring them to their knees."
“The economic approach is the concise one, but it must have kinetic elements," he added. “Iran borders a whole bunch of countries over land, and Iraq is very aligned with Iran. That would make it very difficult to stop any support from getting in.”
The Telegraph noted that the reported plan may in fact be a ruse to draw attention from their "real" military plans, which would come at a time of Iranian supply shortages.

Tu B'Av love story: How two Jerusalem retirees found love after a lifetime apart

As Jews around the world kindle their flames for Tu B’Av, the Festival of Love, one remarkable story from the Mediterranean Towers Jerusalem retirement complex serves as a powerful reminder that love knows no age.
Barry Halpern, 90, and Rica Cuperman, 80, an American-Israeli couple who found each other deep in retirement, prove that some of life’s greatest blessings arrive when we least expect them.
For Barry, the journey to this moment began decades before he ever met Rica. Born in Manchester, England, he moved with his family to California after World War II and spent most of his life in Los Angeles.
An accomplished interior designer and furniture store manager, Barry was deeply involved in the Conservative Jewish community, even helping design a synagogue, an achievement he still speaks of with great pride. Yet, long before his professional career, Israel had already captured his heart.
“When I was 10,” Barry recalled, “my parents sent me to summer camp, where many of the counselors had returned from what was then Mandatory Palestine. They inspired all of us with stories about Israel, and I remember thinking that one day I would live there.”
That childhood dream became reality after retirement. Following the loss of his wife and daughter, and after his son and daughter-in-law made aliyah, Barry fulfilled a lifelong ambition by immigrating to Israel in November 2022 and moving into the Mediterranean Towers Jerusalem.
He was looking forward to beginning a new chapter, but he never imagined that chapter would include finding love again.
Rica’s path to the retirement complex was very different. Born in Romania after World War II, she immigrated to Israel as a child after her family traveled through several countries before finally reaching their new home. They settled in Moshav Olesh before Rica eventually moved to Jerusalem to study at university.
She dedicated 35 years of her career to Jerusalem Municipality’s Welfare Department, helping countless families throughout the city. Although she never married, she built a rich and independent life filled with friendships, family, and meaningful work.
Life before and after meeting her partner
Looking back today, Rica smiles and says she divides her life into two parts: the life before Barry, and the life after meeting him.
Their story began with the brief encounter most people would quickly forget. The two happened to meet at the complex’s community coffee bar. Barry didn’t even realize Rica spoke English. They exchanged a few friendly words before going their separate ways. “It felt pleasant,” Barry reflected, “but I continued on with my day.”
A few months later, after Barry experienced several health scares that required hospital visits, Rica approached him to ask how he was feeling. Touched by her kindness, Barry asked if she would join him for dinner.
She said yes.
Their first date took place at a local restaurant with live music on a warm summer evening. However, hours of conversation and laughter led to something else – a realization of a connection between the two.
“We weren’t expecting it,” Barry said. “I certainly wasn’t looking for it. It just happened.”
THAT DINNER marked the beginning of a relationship neither of them had planned, but both now cherish. Museums, concerts, shopping trips, restaurants, and community events quickly became part of their everyday lives. Barry often jokes that although he is older than Rica, she is the one who keeps him active.
“She keeps me moving,” he laughed. “We go to museums, restaurants, shopping, whatever comes along. My family is thrilled that I’ve found someone, and I’m thrilled too.”
When asked what their relationship means to him, Barry paused thoughtfully before answering.
“First, I thank God for putting both of us in this place at exactly this time. Finding each other feels like finding a needle in a haystack. We bonded so quickly, fell in love, and built a deep relationship. It has filled both of our lives with joy, and I am grateful every single day.”
Despite their instant click, the two couldn’t have been more different. Barry is an American immigrant; Rica is a veteran Israeli. He is religious; she is secular. Their political views often differ completely. Yet those differences have become one of the greatest strengths of their relationship.
“If I had met him years ago,” Rica admitted, “I probably wouldn’t have noticed him.”
Reflecting on how the relationship began, she chuckled.
“I thought, why not?” She laughed as she recalled his request to go to dinner. “What could possibly be wrong?”
Everything changed after that meal.
“Our conversations are amazing,” she said. “Even when we disagree, we know how to bridge those differences.”
Introducted partner to aspects of Judaism she never experienced
Barry has introduced Rica to aspects of Jewish religious life she had never experienced, while Rica has opened Barry’s eyes to Israeli society, taking him to cultural events, introducing him to her family, and making sure language never stood in the way of experiencing life together.
They have created something that transcends politics, religion, and nationality, a relationship built on curiosity, kindness, respect, and genuine companionship.
They speak English with one another, something Rica noted with a quiet pride considering her past hesitation with the language as an Israeli.
As Tu B’Av celebrates the enduring power of love, Barry and Rica’s journey together shows that life indeed doesn’t follow a timetable – it simply arrives when hearts are open to receive it.
For Noa Pasternak-Bardugo, CEO of Mediterranean Towers Jerusalem, Barry and Rica’s story embodies a beautiful sense of bonding for people in their third age.
“Our primary mission is to foster a vibrant community where meeting people and forming meaningful connections are simply a way of life,” she said. “It is a community that enables people to continue living full, enriching lives while building genuine relationships.”
For Noa, Barry and Rica’s story is among the most moving examples of that vision becoming reality. “Rica and Barry’s story is a wonderful reminder that love knows no age,” she said. “There could be no more fitting occasion than Tu B’Av to celebrate their special bond and the extraordinary joy they have found in one another.”
