It was hardly an offensive video. Amid the buzz of the Tour de France finale, Oliver Hooson filmed himself whipping up a delicious-looking flat white for a fellow cycling enthusiast. But after posting the footage on social media, the comments that soon rolled in were a real shock. “PERV,” read one. “Out here pushing creep glasses,” said another. His mistake? Documenting the moment with Meta’s “spy glasses”.
Despite being marketed as the latest, must-have gadget, Meta’s smart glasses, which can record video footage while being worn, have faced a vicious backlash, particularly relating to concerns about surveillance. There are widespread fears about the glasses being used to film people, especially women, without their consent, and the resultant footage being posted on social media. This has earned them the label of “pervert glasses”, and content creators, no matter how they’re used, have faced a flood of negative comments for deploying them.
Things reached a peak earlier this summer when Kylie Jenner collaborated with Meta to launch a pair of the spectacles, leading to a flurry of influencers showing off their trendy, Kardashian-approved goggles. Some content creators have been forced to apologise for plugging the specs. Rida Tharana, a content creator with 1.6 million followers on Instagram, “took accountability” for taking part in a Meta campaign to promote the glasses, criticising men who misuse them to “record and harass women”. Self-described pickup artists have used the glasses to film themselves trying to chat up women in public without their knowledge, sometimes making disparaging, misogynistic comments afterwards.
As for Hooson, while some of the comments left on his video were “savage”, he is fully in support of their message. “It definitely caught me off guard. It was a real ignorant move,” he says, adding he hates that the glasses are being used against “women mainly being filmed against their knowledge”. The backlash was even coming from those close to home. “Funnily enough, my wife predicted it. She was going to post a banterous comment in a similar tone to the others but she didn’t press send.”
After posting the video, Hooson, who shares his passion for home design and cycling to his 224,000 Instagram followers, says he lost a “good 50 to 100” followers. He called the term “pervert glasses” a “defence mechanism” to dissuade people from using them. “If everybody gets round that they are pervert glasses, which evidently they have, then it’s a really easy way of limiting sales and the usage of them,” he says. “People do not need any more of their privacy stripped away. I’m fully in support of that.”
Though his interest in the glasses, which he received at a PR event, was far more innocent. “I saw high-end restaurants filming themselves with the glasses on, showing off their process. It was stupid, poetic and beautiful to watch. I wanted to have a play about and show people the things that I enjoy doing,” he says, adding it’s very hard to capture a point of view (POV) angle otherwise. “It was intended as a simple way of capturing a human POV of my morning coffee routine or my fast cycling descents. I think that’s vastly different from somebody going into the centre of Soho and asking somebody a divisive question and clipping them up for views without their knowledge,” he says.
But, for now, it’s not worth using the glasses. “Would I rather just not have that little fun point of view shot for the greater good? Yes, I would definitely get rid of them,” he says.
While the glasses have proved unpopular with some online, they sold 7m pairs in 2025 alone. Meta says the tiny, flashing LED light that activates on the glasses will let people know when they are being filmed but there have been multiple instances of people complaining they have been videoed without prior knowledge.
In one instance, a woman told the BBC a man recorded her, and then demanded money to remove the videos from social media. Another woman described being recorded during sexual encounters without her consent. Last month, police in Scotland said they were investigating a far-right TikToker who allegedly filmed himself making sexualised comments to young women and telling a Sikh shopkeeper about a plan to “breed out the white race” without their knowledge.
Restaurants, theatres and pubs in the UK, including Wetherspoons, have even joined in on the backlash, banning the Meta glasses over privacy fears.
Deena Lang, a parenting influencer, first got her hands on a pair of the glasses from Meta. “I didn’t really know what they were capable of,” she says, but thought they could be a cool gadget for her son. This was especially true as the family were heading to Europe for a trip and the glasses could, very usefully, translate foreign text live.
But things took a turn when Lang wore them to a cooking class. After looking at the footage she had captured, she says she was “horrified because I just kept catching people giving me dirty looks or side-eye. For me, that was a real awakening.” She posted the footage to her 110,000 followers on Instagram with faces blurred out to act as a “cautionary tale”. She was soon flooded with comments criticising the glasses.
She says she felt “a lot of embarrassment” for using them in a public place in the first instance but understood where the criticism came from, especially after looking at how they’re used in nefarious ways. “I still think they can be used for good. People who are blind said the glasses had helped them navigate life better but the negatives just far outweigh the positives,” she says. “I heard from some people who were dancers that men were invading their privacy and wearing them into the clubs. Others said they caught their waxers wearing them during appointments.”
“I don’t believe that we are in an age where technology like this can exist without people with bad intentions making use of it,” she says.
As for now, the glasses remain out of sight. “They’re in a drawer,” she says.
A Meta spokesperson said: “We designed our AI glasses with privacy and safety features built in from the start – informed by engagement with safety experts, including women’s safety organisations. Our glasses have always had an LED light that activates whenever someone captures a photo or video for their gallery.
“They also feature tamper detection technology, and we are updating the glasses to disable the camera if they detect the LED was physically tampered with or destroyed. No other kind of camera has done this and we’re proud to lead the industry forward.”

UAE extradites suspected cartel boss to Ireland
Authorities in the United Arab Emirates handed over alleged transnational cartel boss Daniel Kinahan to face trial in Ireland, the Gulf country's official media reported on Sunday.
The 49-year-old Irishman allegedly ran a group out of Dubai that has been linked by US and European authorities to a vast criminal enterprise dealing in drug smuggling and money laundering.
The report published by the Emirates News Agency said justice ministers from the Gulf state and Ireland discussed Kinahan's extradition during a recent phone call.
Kinahan "was extradited by the UAE to the Irish authorities at their formal request to face prosecution for offences related to transnational organised crime, including allegations of leading a criminal organisation, murder and illicit drug trafficking," said the report.
Kinahan was arrested in Dubai in April after a warrant was issued in Ireland for "alleged serious organised crime offences" and lost appeals against extradition.
Irish national broadcaster RTE reported his extradition flight had departed the UAE earlier Sunday but did not specify what time it was due to land at Casement Aerodrome in Baldonnel.
On arrival in Ireland, Kinahan was expected to be taken to the Special Crime Court in Dublin amid a large security operation, according to an earlier RTE report.
The transnational group, known as the Kinahan Organised Crime Group (KOCG), is alleged to be the most powerful criminal organisation in Ireland with wealth estimated by Irish police at over 1 billion euros (£856 million).
Kinahan would be the second senior figure in the cartel to be extradited from Dubai.
Sean McGovern, described by a judge as "senior lieutenant in the cartel", was the first senior associate to be extradited after his arrest in 2024.
In June, he was sentenced to 24 years in prison in Ireland after pleading guilty to directing organised crime and attempted murder.
The case related to a long-running violent feud between the Kinahan cartel and the rival Dublin-based Hutch gang.
The US government in 2022 imposed sanctions on Kinahan and other members of his family identified as leaders of the cartel.
It designated them as running a narcotics trafficking organisation active in Ireland, the United Kingdom and throughout mainland Europe, and engaging in "money laundering, firearms trafficking, and murder".
The US Treasury said Kinahan was believed to run the cartel's day-to-day operations, while his brother Christopher was also designated.

CAIRO, August 9. /TASS/. Syria and Russia have signed a memorandum of understanding on the future of Russian military bases in the Arab republic, the Syrian Foreign Ministry’s public relations department said.
"After a year and a half of negotiations, the future of the Russian military bases in Khmeimim and Tartus has finally been determined, with the sides signing the relevant memorandum," it said, according to SANA.
