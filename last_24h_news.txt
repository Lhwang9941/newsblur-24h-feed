Russia bars anti-war party from parliamentary election
The move by the Supreme Court sidelines the liberal Yabloko party, the only officially registered party that opposes the war in Ukraine
Russia’s Supreme Court barred the liberal Yabloko party on Monday from contesting September’s parliamentary elections, sidelining the only officially registered party that opposes Moscow’s war in Ukraine.
More than four years after Russia’s 2022 invasion of Ukraine, the decision highlights the authorities’ intolerance of anti-war views and further narrows the already limited space for dissent in Russian politics.
“The decision will be appealed. Friends, let’s not despair,” the party said in a statement.
The court ruled in favour of a lawsuit filed by the small pro-Kremlin nationalist party Rodina, which sought Yabloko’s exclusion from the election. 
Rodina alleged that Yabloko had received undeclared campaign support, including from Western sources, among other accusations.
During the hearing, Yabloko leader Nikolai Rybakov said there was no evidence to support the allegations and described the case as an unconstitutional attack on freedom of thought by political opponents seeking to clear the field of rivals.

Loneliness before the Digital Age: How Ancient Rome invented the architecture of alienation

Unknown Publisher
2026-08-10
17:32:31 UTC
02:32:31 KST

For our ancestors, separation from the tribe often carried fatal consequences. A lone individual faced constant threats from predators, starvation, and exposure to the elements. Through natural selection, loneliness evolved as a vital internal alarm, a biological warning that social bonds were weakening and survival was at risk. Like hunger or thirst, loneliness was nature's way of driving us back toward the safety of the group and for millennia, human societies were structured to answer that call.
From hunter-gatherer bands to agrarian villages, life was anchored in daily interdependence. Within these small communities, anonymity was nonexistent. Everyone knew one another – their families, histories, and roles.
Relentless proximity came at the cost of privacy, but it also provided a powerful psychological anchor. Everyone had their place within the group, and no one disappeared unnoticed.
Over time, however, these small-group dynamics began to collapse under the weight of rapid urbanization.
That man who talks in the bath
Nowhere was this shift more dramatic than in ancient Rome. As the Republic expanded from a city into a vast empire, the very fabric of the human community underwent radical transformation.
Rome itself became the Western world's first cosmopolis, drawing hundreds of thousands into its densely packed capital. Displaced farmers, provincials seeking economic opportunities, and freedmen poured into the Eternal City, creating an unprecedented urban crush.
At its peak in the 1st and 2nd centuries C.E., Rome is estimated to have had around a million inhabitants. For many, the sudden influx was likely psychologically disorienting. Amid a sea of strangers, Romans both new and original became the first in the West to experience anonymity amidst the crowd – stripped of traditional kinship networks, and left profoundly isolated in the ancient world's first brush with modern alienation.
The city's architecture only deepened this sense of detachment.
Rome was built less on a human scale than as a visual projection of imperial authority and expansionist ambition. Monumental structures loomed over narrow, choking alleyways, a deliberate design that reinforced the immensity of the Roman Empire and the insignificance of the individual. While wealthy elites could retreat to spacious, quiet domus, most Romans lived in cramped high-rise buildings called insulae.
These apartment blocks could reach up to 70 feet in height, the maximum allowed by Augustus' decree. Forced to live alongside strangers from across the empire in cramped and overcrowded conditions, residents may have regarded those around them less as neighbors with whom they shared a community and more as part of the city's incessant background noise.
Seneca vividly captured this experience while living directly above a public bathhouse, where the sounds of strangers penetrated the privacy of his flat: "Imagine now every kind of sound that can make one hate one's ears: I hear their groans, and then the sharp hissing whenever they let out the breath they have held. Or again, I may notice some lazy fellow content with a cheap and ordinary massage, and hear the smacking of a hand on his shoulders… Add to this the quarrelsome man, the thief caught in the act, the man who likes to hear his own voice in the bath."
Seneca's story illustrates how the close physical proximity of Rome's inhabitants could erode the boundaries between private and public life.
Juvenal presents a similar problem, yet on a much greater scale. He portrays Rome as a sleepless city, where constant movement made tranquility almost impossible. The crowded urban environment was particularly oppressive at night, when the noise of traffic and passing crowds kept residents from sleeping: "Here many a sick man dies from lack of sleep. For what rented rooms admit sleep? Sleep in Rome costs a fortune."
The overpopulation of Rome produced a constant sensory presence from which individuals could scarcely escape. In this sense, physical proximity did not necessarily translate into social connection. The same density that brought hundreds of people into close contact could also make them feel intrusive, and even hostile to one another.
Furthermore, the extreme concentration of people facilitated the rapid spread of disease, making Rome perpetually vulnerable to epidemic outbreaks.
In his "Satires," Juvenal offers a grim portrayal of these living conditions, describing the perpetual fear that haunted the urban poor: the threat of their homes collapsing on them or of perishing in a fire while trapped inside. When a building burned down or crumbled, hundreds would instantly become homeless.
To preserve their sanity, Romans learned to tune out the noise. Faces blurred into the background, and emotional detachment became a form of survival.
In the end, the very structures designed to pack humanity together ended up forcing it apart, transforming close physical proximity into profound emotional distance. This withdrawal was not merely a Roman coping mechanism; it anticipated the emotional landscape of the modern metropolis. Beyond the physical architecture of the city, the social landscape offered little relief. Even for those who attained status within the capital, genuine human connection remained elusive.
Rome was, above all, a city sustained by relentless transactional networking. The elite cultivated elaborate daily rituals such as the salutatio, wherein crowds of citizens from almost all levels of Roman society gathered at a patron's doorstep each morning to offer him their services in return for his protection and help. Unsurprisingly, such bonds proved exceptionally fragile: when an individual's fortune or influence dried up, their entourage was gone overnight.
Philosophical refuge
Recognizing the hollow nature of urban noise and transactional social ties, ancient thinkers turned to philosophy, distinguishing between unwanted isolation and the deliberate withdrawal that could restore the mind and cultivate the soul. The intellectual elite, in particular, drew a clear line between solitudo and otium.
Solitudo referred to involuntary physical or social isolation, a condition Romans often regarded with suspicion as a possible sign of deviance or madness. Otium, by contrast, denoted purposeful and elevated leisure: a conscious retreat from the turbulence of the city to read, write, reflect, and pursue intellectual cultivation.
Stoic thinkers such as Seneca wrote extensively on the value of turning inward. The philosopher argued that life amid a crowd offered no guarantee of genuine human connection, urging his readers instead to: "Withdraw into yourself, as far as you can. Associate with those who will make a better man of you. Welcome those whom you yourself can improve. The process is mutual."
Decades later, Emperor Marcus Aurelius embodied this same ideal. While composing his Meditations during prolonged military campaigns along the Danube frontier, he reflected that one could always find a place of quiet retreat within the soul.
For the Stoics, intentional solitude was not a withdrawal from civic responsibility but a disciplined practice of self-examination that restored the mind and strengthened one's capacity to re-engage with the world.
Yet, while philosophers could choose to turn inward, voluntary otium was a luxury of the elite. In the Greco-Roman world, an individual's identity was not an internal, self-contained project; it was a communal construct derived entirely from active participation in the Greek polis or the Roman res publica. To be separated from this collective fabric was to suffer social death. Consequently, for those forcibly cast out, whether through legal action, civic punishment, or forced migration, loneliness ceased to be a quiet background ache and became a profound personal tragedy.
Lost in translation
While neither ancient Greek nor Latin possessed a specific word for "loneliness" that conveys today's precise psychological weight, the experience of forced isolation was a terrifying reality.
The Greeks spoke of eremia, a term spanning desertion, wilderness, and desolation, which captured the eerie void of existing beyond the city walls. Classical Athens formalized this systemic expulsion through ostracism. Once a year, citizens gathered in the Agora to scratch onto broken pottery shards (ostraca) the name of any figure deemed too dominant or dangerous. A successful vote meant immediate expulsion: ten years of exile beyond the city's borders.
Rome inherited this practice of social banishment, elevating it into a formal judicial sentence, known as exilium. Among the gravest punishments short of execution, it inflicted a devastating psychological blow, forcibly severing individuals from the culture, politics, and sense of self that defined them.
The poet Ovid embodied this devastation when Emperor Augustus banished him to Tomis, a remote outpost on the Black Sea. In his Tristia, Ovid lamented the crushing isolation he explicitly equated to a living death. Marooned in a harsh climate among people whose language he could not understand, he was stripped of the intellectual world he once dominated. Surrounded by crowds yet entirely alone, Ovid famously observed that he had become the "barbarian" to the locals simply because they could not find a common language. The writer was lost in translation.
His experience exposes a distinction that remains relevant today: solitude is not simply the absence of people but the absence of meaningful human connection. One may be surrounded by others and yet remain profoundly alone when deprived of a shared language, common customs, and mutual understanding.
Ovid's exile demonstrates that true belonging depends not merely on being among others but on participating in a community where one is recognized and able to share one's identity and thoughts.
Yet his banishment, for all its suffering, remained the tragedy of a privileged Roman citizen whose grievance was immortalized in literature. Countless millions across the Empire suffered a far colder exile, without status or legacy. For many, displacement was not a solitary decree but a desperate, forced flight in search of work opportunities in foreign cities. Many were pushed to the margins by economic collapse, fleeing wars, ruinous taxation, or simply enslavement. Stripped of their ancestral soil, cultural roots, and legal protections, these nameless exiles experienced the same profound alienation Ovid described, but without the luxury of artistic expression or the hope of imperial pardon.
Long before algorithms and screens, Romans were already dealing with the anonymity of crowded cities, the challenges of cultural assimilation, and relationships that could feel increasingly transactional. Looking back at the first apartment blocks or at Ovid's lonely exile, we are not simply studying the ancient world, we are looking into a mirror. Technology has changed, but the human condition has not. Rome reminds us that belonging has never been guaranteed by proximity, visibility, or constant interaction. Human connection requires more than simply living alongside others. Two thousand years separate us from ancient Rome, and yet we suffer from the same condition – the absence of belonging.

Colombia: Dozens killed after large earthquake rocks region
Published August 10, 2026last updated August 10, 2026
A powerful earthquake shook western Colombia early on Monday morning.
Around five hours after the quake struck, officials around the country had reported at least 73 deaths, 67 of them just in two of the worst-hit states. Rescue teams were working to free residents after buildings collapsed in Cali and other cities.
The epicenter was on the eastern tip of the Choco province on Colombia's Pacific coast.
What is the latest?
The mayor of Cali, Alejandro Eder, said that at least 20 buildings had collapsed, in some cases with people trapped inside.
"Residents of Cali, we currently have at least 20 collapsed structures in the city with people trapped inside; we are already managing this emergency alongside all of Cali's rescue agencies," Eder said on Instagram, posting an accompanying video statement. "I have requested support — specifically rescue personnel — from the mayors of Bogota and Medellin."
Authorities in the Valle del Cauca, whose capital city is Cali, later said that at least 27 people had died in the quake.
Officials in Risaralda said that 40 people had died across the state, at least 18 of them in the capital Pereira based on earlier comments from the mayor.
And the mayor of Manizales, Jorge Eduardo Rojas, said in a radio interview that two people were killed in his city, in the Caldas region not far northeast of Pereira.
The governor of the Choco region where the quake's epicenter was located, Nubia Carolina Cordoba-Curi, said there were four dead and 69 wounded in her district as of late morning local time.
Colombia's civil aviation authority reported damage at six relatively minor airports and said flights were suspended while it was examined.
"Damage is reported at the airports of Pereira, Manizales, Quibdo, Armenia, Cartago, and Buenaventura. For safety reasons, air operations at these terminals remain suspended until structural damage to the infrastructure is assessed," it wrote online, later adding Cali's airport to this list.
Colombia's newly-inaugurated President Abelardo de la Espriella said that he had personally taken charge of the government's response to the emergency in San Jose del Palmar following the earthquake that struck the country.
"You are not alone. The state is present and taking action," he said.
What do we know about the quake's size and location?
The US Geological Survey (USGS) initially measured the quake at magnitude 7.4. Colombia's national authority at first logged a smaller figure but later revised its reading up to 7.4 as well.
The epicenter was located just south of the Tatama National Nature Reserve, near Choco's eastern state border.
The epicenter was roughly equidistant from Colombia's three main cities, Bogota to its east, Medellin to its north and Cali to its south.
The closest major population center, Cartago, is home to about 140,000 people and around 30 kilometers (roughly 20 miles) from the quake's epicenter.
The USGS said the quake was also quite deep, striking around 107 kilometers below the ground. It estimated that most of the western half of Colombia would feel the tremors.
Tremors could also be felt in neighboring Ecuador to the south, and to the north and east in Panama and Venezuela.
USGS also recorded a fairly large aftershock, at magnitude 5.0, a little under an hour after the main quake.
The so-called Ring of Fire — the world's most active belt of tectonic plates and volcanoes encircling the Pacific Ocean — runs along Colombia's coastline not far from the epicenter of Monday's quake.
