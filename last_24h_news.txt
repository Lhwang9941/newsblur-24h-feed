How China is using film diplomacy to project a better image across Africa
From Burundi to Kenya to Morocco, film screenings, co-productions and cultural exchanges are helping to shape a new frontier of influence
The Chinese embassy in Bujumbura, the economic capital of Burundi, said the July 15 event offered “a beautiful example of cultural exchanges between the two countries” and that both sides were determined to “make cinema a vehicle for Sino-Burundian friendship”.
Zhu Kewei, China’s ambassador to the East African country, called cinema “a force capable of bridging language barriers, borders and cultures”, adding that “film carries a nation’s historical memories, cultural traditions, values and the emotions of its people”.
In addition to the film screenings, China and Burundi plan to expand cooperation through creator exchanges, artist mobility, university partnerships, co-productions and skills transfer.
As part of the China-Africa Year of Human and Cultural Exchanges, the festival in Burundi lined up seven Chinese films, including The Wandering Earth II, Changan and The Snow Leopard.

Pentagon spends $333 million to keep fighter pilots’ helmets combat ready

- The U.S. Defense Logistics Agency awarded Rockwell Collins ESA Vision Systems an estimated $332,623,964 contract for Joint Helmet Mounted Cueing System spares and repairs.
- The six-year contract covers F-15, F-16 and F-18 aircraft for the Air Force, Navy and 13 allied nations, running through July 23, 2032.
The U.S. Defense Logistics Agency awarded Rockwell Collins ESA Vision Systems, a Fort Worth, Texas company, an estimated $333 million contract to supply spare parts and repair services for the Joint Helmet Mounted Cueing System, a fighter pilot’s helmet that doubles as a targeting computer.
The system, known in defense circles by its acronym JHMCS, tracks exactly where a pilot’s head is pointed and projects targeting information directly onto the visor, letting the pilot lock a missile onto an enemy jet simply by looking at it rather than pointing the entire aircraft’s nose at the threat first.
Paired with the AIM-9X Sidewinder missile, that capability lets a pilot engage targets in a cone stretching more than 80 degrees to either side of the aircraft’s nose, a technique the military calls high off-boresight targeting. In plain terms, a pilot can shoot at something well off to the side of where the jet is actually flying, a battlefield edge that has mattered in close-in dogfights for more than two decades.
Rockwell Collins ESA Vision Systems is not a household name, but the joint venture behind it certainly is. The company pairs Collins Aerospace, the aerospace and defense electronics arm of RTX, with Elbit Systems of America, the U.S. subsidiary of the Israeli defense technology firm Elbit Systems, and the partnership has built and supported Joint Helmet Mounted Cueing Systems since 1996. Over that span the venture has delivered thousands of these helmet displays to more than 20 air forces worldwide and now serves as the current provider for the F-35’s own advanced helmet display system, giving the company deep institutional knowledge of exactly the kind of parts and repair work this new contract covers.
This new award covers what the Pentagon calls consumable spares, meaning parts that get used up and replaced during routine maintenance, along with what the contract terms depot-level reparable spares, the more expensive components that get sent back to a repair depot and fixed rather than simply thrown away and replaced. Both categories keep JHMCS units flying on three of the U.S. military’s workhorse fighters, the F-15 Eagle, the F-16 Fighting Falcon, and the F/A-18 Hornet, aircraft that together form the backbone of American and allied tactical airpower even as newer stealth fighters like the F-35 enter service alongside them.
The Defense Logistics Agency structured this as a sole-source acquisition, meaning it awarded the work directly to Rockwell Collins ESA Vision Systems without opening the contract to competing bidders, citing a specific legal justification under federal acquisition rules that permits skipping competition when only one source can reasonably supply the parts. That makes sense given the company’s decades-long role as the sole provider of JHMCS hardware, a position that leaves the Defense Logistics Agency with little practical alternative if it wants parts that fit the helmets already worn by thousands of pilots.
The contract runs as a six-year base period with an additional six-month option, stretching performance out to a completion date of July 23, 2032, and the work will happen at sites in Texas, Oregon and Israel, a footprint that reflects both Rockwell Collins ESA Vision Systems’ domestic operations and Elbit Systems’ Israeli engineering base. Funding flows from fiscal year 2026 through fiscal year 2032 defense working capital funds along with military service and Foreign Military Sales funds, spreading the financial burden across the Air Force, the Navy, and a long list of allied nations that also fly JHMCS-equipped fighters.
That international customer list is itself a snapshot of how widely this American helmet technology has spread. The contract names Greece, Morocco, Singapore, Chile, Denmark, Norway, Thailand, Oman, Taiwan, Portugal, Canada, Switzerland and Australia as Foreign Military Sales customers who will draw on this same contract for their own JHMCS spares and repairs, meaning a single supply chain in Fort Worth, Texas now underpins fighter pilot readiness on nearly every continent. The Defense Logistics Agency Weapon Support office in Warner Robins, Georgia, is managing the contract on behalf of all of these customers, a level of logistical coordination that rarely makes headlines but keeps allied air forces flying missions that depend on split-second targeting decisions.
The contract announcement does not specify how many individual helmet units or repair jobs the $332.6 million ceiling is expected to cover over its six-year span, nor does it break down how funding will be split among the Air Force, Navy and the thirteen allied nations named as customers. What is clear is that a technology first fielded more than two decades ago, one that lets a pilot kill a target simply by turning their head, remains central enough to modern air combat that keeping it supplied and repaired is worth a nine-figure Pentagon commitment stretching into the next decade, a reminder that some of the most consequential pieces of military hardware are not the jets themselves but the gear strapped to the pilot’s own head.

A trip to the 'boy aquarium': Discovering Israel's thriving hockey scene
Unknown
Unknown Publisher
2026-07-25
10:52:35 UTC
19:52:35 KST

Several months ago, I, like many others, bought into the hockey show hype, and since then, my social media feed slowly shifted to… real hockey.
Before I knew it, I found myself on the edge of my seat following actual National Hockey League games, learning about stats and trades, and for the first time in my life, I was kind of enjoying watching a sport.
So when I learned that Israel has its own hockey league, I knew what I had to do.
The Jerusalem Post was going to the boy aquarium, and I was going to get us there.
I’ll admit, I was quite nervous to meet Isaac Levy, a prominent figure in Israel’s hockey scene and the captain of the Jerusalem Capitals, but Levy - and the entire Israel Elite Hockey League - welcomed me with open arms as their newest fan.
The craziest thing is: the hockey is good. Like, it's really good
Despite going hard on the ice, Israel’s greatest hockey players are all good friends when off it, and Levy actually rooms with Maverick Cortes, a defenseman for Tel Aviv’s team, the aptly-named Hockey Club Tel Aviv, who joined us for a quick chat before the season began.
After the pair confirmed they had not seen Heated Rivalry - though they did share their respect for anything that encourages diversity in sports - the two explained how they ended up in Israel.
“I was born in Israel but grew up in Canada,” Levy said. “When I was about 15, I was recruited to play for Israel’s national team because I have dual citizenship. I’ve played for Israel ever since, from the U18s all the way to the senior national team for more than 20 years.”
“I grew up in Denver, Colorado,” Cortes added. “I always heard about Israel growing up, went to Jewish school, had my bar mitzvah here, and loved it. A few years later, I was scrolling through Instagram and found the league. I thought, ‘This is perfect.’ It combines hockey with my connection to Israel.”
Cortes and Levy are prime examples of the people that the league is looking for: high-level players from abroad with a connection to Israel, combining a love of both the game and the country.
Levy took his love for both Israel and hockey to another level, finding love here and working alongside his brother to open Ashdod’s Blue Ice Arena, where the southern division competes.
The league was formed about five years ago, and has already grown from four teams to eight, with a northern and southern division.
The craziest thing is: The hockey is good. Like, it’s really good.
Like a tournament, with a giant trophy and bragging rights
Israel's hockey season is more like a tournament, with games happening one after the other until one can be crowned the winner, complete with a giant trophy and bragging rights in the group chat.
The Jerusalem Post’s breaking news desk was honored to be invited to the opening game, to watch Jerusalem’s Capitals - for whom we were practically contractually obligated to cheer - take on the Ashdod Dolphins.
I’ll admit, this was the first time I’ve ever watched a sports game of any kind in my adult life, and I could not believe how much fun it was. Unlike with major league sports, Israel’s players have more than just respect for each other; there’s real love there.
Each pass, save, and goal was met with cheers and hugs as if they’d won the Stanley Cup.
Our group was on the edge of our seats the whole time as the teams went into a first, and then a second overtime. Seriously, we hadn’t realized sports could have such drama.
A sudden death shootout? With knives taped to their feet? Immediately obsessed.
In the end, Ashdod clinched it, but we didn’t leave with any real feelings of loss. Sure, we were a little upset that our home team had lost, but even in defeat, our group was already making plans to come back - or at least catch a livestream.
What struck me most, though, was that as the final buzzer neared, we saw players from other teams watching from the sidelines, on their feet, cheering for their friends on both sides of the ice.
By the end of the night, no one seemed to care for whom they were supposed to be rooting. It was the kind of thing that made it obvious this isn’t a league built on rivalries - heated or otherwise - so much as it’s built on camaraderie, and people who genuinely love the game and love each other.
Our trip to the boy aquarium was better than we could’ve imagined, proving that you can find genuine, ice-taped-knives-and-all sports drama right here at home. Consider me a Capitals fan for life - shootout losses and all.
