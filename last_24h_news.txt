Labor's national conference triumph had an unseen trade-off
Mon 27 Jul 2026 at 4:50am
Journalists spent a lot of last week's Labor Party conference lamenting the absence of heated debates on the conference floor.
A bit of biffo over issues like same sex marriage or boat turn-backs has been a lightning-rod moment in recent years, as the ALP has sought to settle divisive debates that have big implications for the party's ability to be competitive at elections.
It is easy to see why the media, hanging on the sidelines of Labor's three-day camp, were frustrated they didn't have a fiery stoush to help fill air time and column inches. For Labor, the more lasting issue is what sacrifice comes with its command and control culture.
Much has been said about Labor's rigid discipline under Anthony Albanese. It comes as little surprise to party insiders who often joke about the fact that the prime minister, who came through the ranks of the NSW left faction, managed to end up not only in charge of the left, but also exerting control over its rival, the right.
Potential debates over a gas tax, gambling reforms, AUKUS and the Middle East were all settled behind closed doors. The only public pickle for the PM came courtesy of an errant escalator.
That is aside from former minister Ed Husic, who spent much of the lead-up to the conference arguing the Labor caucus was effectively gagged from offering any view that contradicted sanctioned government talking points.
Speaking to me on Afternoon Briefing at the conference, Husic said Labor had become "allergic" to disagreement.
His attempt to amend the party platform to take a tougher line on Israel in relation to the conflict in Gaza and the West Bank failed on Saturday, after the Maritime Union withdrew its support. Husic hit out at his colleagues and said he was "surprised" at the energy applied to shut down his proposal.
Senior Labor figures rejected his claims and said that Husic ultimately couldn't muster enough support for his resolution.
They point to the fact that the party adopted a position that was supported by Victorian MPs Josh Burns, who is Jewish, and Basem Abdo, the son of Palestinian refugees. Both appeared on stage and spoke in support of the draft platform, its wording consistent with the official government position.
But Husic warned that a party that doesn't allow democracy to flourish loses connection with voters.
A broader point at play
He has a broader point, but it is worth noting the conference is not a town hall public policy forum, but a rank-and-file meeting of Labor members, delegates, unions and politicians.
And Albanese is not the only leader to have an iron grip on proceedings.
For those nostalgic about conferences past, Bob Hawke delivered a cracker of a show in 1990.
Hawke sprung a surprise conference on the party to effectively rubber stamp a cabinet decision to privatise Australian Airlines and part of Qantas.
There is a key difference. Hawke brought on the conference, but he also opened the floor to give opponents to privatisation an opportunity to make their case. Hawke won the day, but members had the chance to argue the toss.
In shutting down Husic's proposed amendment, Albanese risks sending a broader message that the party floor is no longer a place for a genuine contest of ideas, with real decisions made behind closed doors.
That goes back to Husic's point about Labor closing down disagreement.
One Nation has become a place for voters to go who feel like the major parties aren't listening to them. But the reason it's gained popularity is hardly that its internal mechanisms are filled with democratic expression.
One Nation is Pauline Hanson's baby, and unlike parties of the centre, it doesn't have to settle debates about whether it should support Muslim immigration or not — it hardly has to balance another flank of opinion. It's Hanson's way or the highway.
Voters are drawn to One Nation because Hanson has modelled herself as the antithesis of mainstream politicians, less so because of a robust internal policy process. Yet the risk for Albanese is that what critics argue is him stifling conference debate bleeds into how he registers voter complaints.
Let's look at the counterfactual. Imagine a conference with fierce debates on AUKUS, attacks on Trump or drawn-out debate on trans rights. Now imagine how that would be weaponised against the government.
Under Albanese, Labor has been obsessive in avoiding handing its opponents a victory on culture wars and being accused of focusing on niche issues. Albanese believes that being a party of government means parking topics that don't align with mainstream issues like the cost of living.
Labor figures will tell you they will gladly cop the media condemning their lack of public debate and democracy. They care about what voters see. And in their assessment, voters not engaged with the daily rhythm of politics see a party that is willing to put aside its differences to pursue its overwhelming aims.
The Liberals' electoral headache has coincided with its own lack of discipline and a growing obsession with issues that are out of whack with mainstream Australia. Debating immigration is fair game in a country that feels under pressure. Flirting with the idea that multiculturalism has failed is much sharper and risks alienating scores of voters for whom multiculturalism is their reality.
Cosplaying unity
Labor powerbroker and Trade Minister Don Farrell told me the display of unity at conference was a deliberate contrast to the Liberal Party, which he painted as deeply fractured and fed into a broader theme of the conference — linking the Liberal Party to One Nation.
You can understand the bigger play. If your entire political messaging and strategy is to paint the opposition as bitterly divided, you need to cosplay extreme unity.
That strategy has so far worked for Albanese; he has won two elections, the last securing a historic number of Labor seats. He will no doubt welcome coverage of the conference being dull and stage-managed.
The PM said as much on ABC yesterday. Appearing on Insiders, he offered this: "We are more cohesive, more united than we've ever been before. And I think that's a good thing."
Where Hawke stitched up the numbers, yet still gave members the microphone, the PM's ruthless control risks looking like taking the win and not bothering with the debate.

Berlin attack is a ‘very real reminder’ of why Pride is still needed, say heartbroken community
Pride is not just a celebration - it’s about visibility, safety and solidarity. Events in Berlin has reinforced what it means for the LGBTQ+ community, as Jacqui Merrington reports
- Bookmark
- CommentsGo to comments
When a suspected terrorist drove into crowds in Berlin, it was a ‘very real reminder’ of why Pride is still so important to communities, according to organisers at one of the key events around the world this weekend.
The German capital was one of many cities transformed into a sea of rainbow celebrations for the LGBTQ+ community and allies over the past few weeks.
And the impact of the attack, which killed one and injured up to 29 others, was to reinforce the core mission of Pride, which is not just a celebration, but about visibility, safety and solidarity for gay people.
‘While we came together in solidarity yesterday, our community was under attack in Berlin,’ wrote organisers of Folkestone Pride in Kent, where thousands were also celebrating on Saturday.
‘We are devastated for the person killed, those injured and all who have been affected. The impact of such a horrific act is felt throughout our entire community.
“As we reflect on this tragedy alongside moments of solidarity and togetherness both locally and afar, let it be a reminder of the power and importance of connection, unity and visibility. We are stronger together.”
A vigil is taking place in Germany on Sunday evening for those killed, injured and impacted by the incident in the Tiergarten park area of Berlin.
German chancellor, Friedrich Merz, said: “I want to say this to those taking part in Christopher Street Day here in Berlin, and indeed across the whole of Germany: do not let yourselves – let us not – be intimidated.”
Newcastle Pride came together on Sunday and also organised a candlelit vigil for the victims in Berlin.
In a statement, they said: “We are heartbroken to read the news of the tragic events at Berlin Pride. Pride is about joy, community and the freedom to exist without fear. We are sending love, strength and solidarity to the victims, their families and everyone in Berlin. Sadly it is a very real reminder of why Pride is still needed.”
Ella’s global community, which represents queer women + non-binary people, echoed those statements.
“One day after celebrating Pride, we're reminded why Pride still matters. Berlin Pride (Christopher Street Day) ended in tragedy after a vehicle drove into the crowd near the closing celebrations,” the organisation wrote in a social media post.
”Today, our thoughts are with the victims, their families and everyone affected. Pride has always been more than a celebration.
“It is about visibility. It is about safety. It is about standing together.
“Berlin, we stand with you.”
Brighton Pride, the UK’s largest pride event taking place next weekend, said: “We are deeply saddened by the attack in Berlin. We stand in solidarity with the Berlin LGBTQ+ community. This is why we march for our rights. Pride should be a place of celebration and joy. Brighton stands with Berlin.”
German authorities launched a manhunt for the suspect, named as Abdul B. Berlin Police said on Sunday evening he had been shot dead on the outskirts of Berlin.

ST. PETERBURG, July 26. /TASS/. Trajectories of ballistic missiles pass through the Northern Sea Route, where Russian submarines are also on duty, Russian President Vladimir Putin said.
"Trajectories of ballistic missiles run through the Northern Sea Route where our submarines are on duty. Other countries also seek to enter the area," he said at a meeting with servicemen of the Russian Navy.
"We have developed this region, and we will continue to develop it. It is very important from an economic standpoint," he added.
Russia’s armed forces are deployed to the Arctic region to safeguard the country’s interests and are threatening no one, Putin said.
"We have already developed a comprehensive program and will continue to do so, creating conditions for people to live and work there. This includes urban renewal and the creation of good living conditions in key cities of the Arctic region. It is a major undertaking. And, of course, the presence of our armed forces there, which pose no threat to anyone but safeguard the interests of the Fatherland, is a very important component of our work in the Arctic," he said at a meeting with Russian Navy servicemen.
Russia's wealth will grow through the Arctic, which is an extremely important region for the country, Russian President said.
"We have developed this region, and we will continue to develop it. It is extremely important from the standpoint of our economic interests. We all know Lomonosov's (the 18th-century Russian scientist - TASS) famous words that Russia's wealth would grow through Siberia, and now, without any exaggeration, we can say that Russia's wealth will grow through the Arctic. It is difficult even now to measure the Arctic's resources, and it is difficult to estimate their value in monetary terms," the president said.
