Inside Ceuta: Residents face fear and uncertainty as migrant numbers soar; bread is scarce, shops are closed

Most shops are shut, bread is scarce, and local festivities have been cancelled after tens of thousands of migrants arrived from Morocco. Residents and local authorities describe growing fear and uncertainty as returns continue.
The autonomous city of Ceuta has ground to a halt following the mass arrival of migrants from Morocco. Most shops remained closed on Friday, fresh bread was becoming difficult to find and the Ceuta government suspended this weekend's patron saint festivities as authorities struggled to regain control of the situation.
As AFP observed on the ground, the usual bustle of the central Calle Real has given way to groups of migrants gathered in the streets, while the shutters of many businesses remain down. In the city centre, home to around 84,000 people, only a handful of pharmacies and a restaurant belonging to a well-known chain were open.
The Ceuta Business Confederation has advised shops and other businesses not to reopen "until security can be guaranteed", amid continuing uncertainty and the deployment of additional security forces.
Health sector sources contacted by Euronews said medical services were under severe pressure despite reinforcements being called in. The most common cases being treated include hypothermia, anxiety attacks and fractures.
"We are running out of bread. There was no fresh bread today because the bakeries are closed," municipal street sweeper Miguel Ángel Campana told AFP. He said some residents feared migrants could try to enter homes "asking for water and other things" and urged neighbours to "keep an eye on their balconies". Across the city, migrants could be seen stopping passing vehicles to ask for food, money or to borrow mobile phones to contact their families.
Local authorities estimate that around 60,000 people have crossed irregularly from Morocco into Ceuta since the early hours of Thursday, many swimming around the border breakwater. The Spanish government says more than 37,500 have already returned to Morocco.
Police have been directing large groups of migrants, many of them young people, towards the border crossing to facilitate their return. The crisis has also triggered an intense political and diplomatic response, with the army deployed in Ceuta and several European countries criticising the way the situation has been handled.
"It remains a heartbreaking situation from a humanitarian point of view, seeing so many people with nowhere to go, without food or water, because the few services available for them are completely overwhelmed," a Ceuta resident interviewed by Euronews said.
At the same time, concern among residents is growing.Mohammed, a taxi driver from Ceuta, believes many migrants have decided to return to Morocco after seeing the conditions awaiting them in the city."They thought they knew what they were going to find here and they have been confronted with reality," he told AFP. Some had told him they were returning "because they had no food".
"There is nothing here. Those of us who are from here are struggling to get by. We are having a hard time," he added.
Mohammed said he and other neighbours stayed awake until the early hours of the morning for fear of possible robberies. "I have nieces who are afraid," he said.
That sense of unease is shared by 56-year-old Yolanda Moreno, who said she had felt "afraid from the start" of the migration crisis. "It was like during the pandemic: the feeling that it was better to stay at home than to go out onto the streets," she told AFP.
Another resident interviewed by Euronews also expressed concern that tensions could escalate, saying people increasingly feared violence. "This morning I was shocked to see people from here, from the neighbourhood, with lots of knives, trying to take justice into their own hands," she said. "I just saw one boy with a katana running after a Moroccan boy, without really knowing what was going on."
The scale of the crisis has forced the Ceuta government to cancel the patron saint festivities that were due to begin on Saturday. At the fairground, rides remain covered with tarpaulins and motionless — a striking symbol of a city whose normal rhythm has been brought to a halt as it struggles to cope with one of the largest migrant arrivals in its history.

The great powers are no longer calling all the shots

analysis
Global politics is being driven in disturbing directions by more than just strongmen
Sat 1 Aug 2026 at 5:00am
For much of the past decade, a lot of what has unfolded in the world has been seen through the idea of the rise of strongmen in global politics.
Donald Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladamir Putin have headed the list, along with Recep Tayyip Erdoğan in Türkiye.
And that's not to mention the ones who have come and gone in the meantime: Hungary's Viktor Orban, Jair Bolsonaro in Brazil, Rodrigo Duterte in the Philippines.
But so much has been transformed in the past year.
In the United States and Russia, two of the strongmen may still hold unprecedented power within their countries, and seek to exert on the rest of the world, but they do so with rapidly diminishing credibility and effectiveness.
Trump's rambling pronouncements — someone described them this week as "fantasy announcements" — barely rate any immediate quiver in world markets, let alone diplomatic circles.
For example, there was cautious early reporting in international media on Friday morning, Australian time, of his announcement on social media of a "HISTORIC agreement for the COMPLETE DISARMAMENT of Hamas and all other armed groups in Gaza".
As is so often the case these days, everyone immediately wanted to know if anybody else had actually agreed to this agreement.
As the New York Times reported: "There was no immediate comment from Hamas or Israel, and it was not clear whether either had signed off on the plan after years of war and a fragile cease-fire."
A new conflation of important factors
It's not just that some of the strongmen don't seem so strong anymore, that they often just seem to be lashing out reasonably ineffectively, or even that the "Great Powers" don't seem to be running things.
It's that there is a new conflation of factors proving to be just as important, if not more so, in driving world events in increasingly disturbing directions.
These include the dividend of years of greater powers' efforts at destabilising other states; the rise of states that have never quite got to be stable in the first place; and of the role of non-state actors.
More recently there has been the rapidly and relentlessly expanding story of how technological change in warfare — and rapidly diminishing stockpiles of defensive weapons — are transforming geopolitical power.
Just in this week alone, we have seen the conflicts in the Middle East and Ukraine spread inexorably: conspicuously dragging in Saudi Arabia as well as conflicts in Iraq, Syria and Yemen continuing to escalate.
But Egypt has now also found itself under attack.
Also, Ukraine directly attacked an Iranian vessel in the Caspian Sea, a move which linked the Iranian and Ukrainian conflicts. It also reflected Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy's apparent attempts to highlight Russian support for Iran to the US president days before he went to see Trump in Washington.
Then there are reports — denied by the Chinese — of a major deal to deliver Chinese arms to Iran.
In Europe, NATO jets scrambled this week as a Russian missile entered Polish airspace and crashed.
Ukraine has expressed alarm that North Korea is about to send another 30,000 troops to Russia to support its campaign.
Yet even with all this, the impacts of the major conflicts of the time don't just involve crossfire and manpower.
Trump scuttles nuclear power deal with Saudi Arabia
Russia, one of the world's largest resource economies, is now importing oil from India because of the damage Ukraine has inflicted on its energy infrastructure.
The European Union is struggling with a dilemma about placing sanctions on an aluminium smelter in Ireland owned by Russian interests because European nations also need the aluminium.
There are reports that Ukraine has been buying Chinese drone components from an EU defence loan.
The chaos that Trump's increasingly erratic interventions create was highlighted this week by the announcement of a nuclear power deal with Saudi Arabia — a 30-year legal framework which would let US companies build nuclear infrastructure and export civilian nuclear technology to Saudi Arabia while blocking foreign competitors like China and Russia.
Critics pointed out some of the alarming shortcomings of the deal. It would allow Saudi Arabia to develop a full-blown civilian nuclear industry (okay so far) but without international safeguards. Unlike stricter historical precedents like the US deal with the United Arab Emirates, reports indicate this agreement may permit Saudi enrichment on its own soil over time.
As former Director General of the Australian Safeguards and Non-Proliferation Office John Carlson wrote this week: "This deal compromises both the non-proliferation standards that up to now have been applied by the United States, and the contemporary standard for International Atomic Energy Agency safeguards applied under the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT)."
And, just saying, it would set up exactly the same ecosystem which the US and Israel find so threatening in Iran when it comes to nuclear power, presumably on the presumption that Saudi will always be a friendly state.
Just to confuse things further, Trump then effectively scuttled the deal by saying Saudi would have to sign up to being friends with Israel under the Abraham Accords if the deal was to proceed. Which Saudi won't do.
In doing so, he only highlighted how insane the whole situation in the Middle East really is.
The irony of Israel's invasions
The Arab world in general is going to remain conflicted about Israel, and possibly only become more so, as Israel escalates its brutality and ambitions in what are still technically Palestinian territories, and against Palestinian people.
That is despite the Gulf states' continuing anxiety about Iran.
The ambiguity of Trump's meeting with Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu this week showed what a mire both countries are now in, partly because of this conflicted, ambivalent position of so many of Israel's neighbours.
Israel may have felt briefly that it had emerged as the new hegemon in the region. And it continues to redefine the language of international affairs by describing invasions into other territories — now Lebanon and Syria as well as Gaza and the West Bank — as "buffer zones".
There is an irony in this, as analyst Gregory Gause pointed out in the latest issue of Foreign Affairs magazine.
"Netanyahu believes that Israel is safer when its neighbours are weak and divided," Gause writes.
"But he misses the fact that Israel's peace treaties with Egypt and Jordan, two consolidated states that can control their borders and prevent the rise of violent non-state actors in their territory, have been the enduring pillars of Israel's security for decades.
"Rather than embrace the prospect of a cooperative Lebanese government willing to work against Hezbollah, Israel renewed its war in Lebanon in 2026.
"In Syria, Israel captured a so-called buffer zone, encouraged Syrian Druze to resist the new government, armed Druze militias, and struck Syrian army positions — including the headquarters of the Ministry of Defence — in the name of protecting the Druze."
Supporting nascent governments in the region, rather than destabilising them, at some point must become an issue.
"Iran's allies, although weakened, still wield significant influence across the Arab world. In Iraq's elections in November 2025, Shiite parties aligned with Iran and the Iraqi militias it backs performed well," Gause writes.
"In February, March, and April of this year, those militias lobbed missiles at US bases in the region. The new Iraqi government under Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi, like its predecessors, has filled important positions with many of Iran's local allies."
Which brings us to Iran.

A more sophisticated assessment of Iran
Two major analysts of Iran, Narges Bajoghli and Vali Nasr, both of Johns Hopkins University, have also written in the latest Foreign Affairs, offering a somewhat more sophisticated assessment of the Iranian leadership and its strategy than we get from Trump's expletive laden assertions of imminent collapse.
The new generation of Iranian leadership, the two men say, "has separated revolution from statecraft".
"At home and abroad, it neither espouses revolutionary grandiosity nor advocates revolutionary activism."
The new leaders, Bajoghli and Nasr write, are "establishment actors: pragmatic, hardened nationalists operating with a clear-eyed assessment of Iran's capabilities and vulnerabilities".
"Unlike their predecessors, they can exercise strategic patience and act decisively. They look at Iran's weaknesses frequently and publicly — something the founding generation was too insecure to do honestly — and they treat them as problems to be solved."
The Islamic Republic had long operated through a chaotic maze of competing power centres, which produced unending internal debate and sclerotic inertia.
But they say this chaos has given way "to organisational discipline and resilience" with a structure involving defence, civilian and economic bureaucracies.
As a result, Bajoghli and Nasr say, US Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Vice President JD Vance "are wrong" in their fundamental assessment of Iran".
"Tehran's defiant approach reflects neither ideological rigidity nor factional infighting," they write.
"The country's leaders understand that the United States is seeking to get from talks what it could not achieve in war and that Washington is not interested in a deal but in Iran's surrender."
The only problem is "Iranian leaders already claim they have won the war".
"This self-confidence — rooted in the belief that the war has empowered Iran rather than weakened it — is informing their international outlook."
Abroad, they say this does not mean it will abandon its regional allies, like Hezbollah, Shiite militias in Iraq and the Houthis in Yemen. "But it will manage them with more strategic discipline and less ideological romanticism."
The new Iranian leadership will not sacrifice Iran's interests at the altar of revolutionary solidarity, Bajoghli and Nasr say.
"These alliances will be deployed as part of a coherent regional strategy designed to sustain Iran's strategic depth against sustained US and Israeli pressure."
The most important message in that assessment, of course, is that Iran actually has a coherent regional strategy. Something that cannot necessarily be said for the great powers who might like to think they can still call the shots.
Laura Tingle is the ABC's Global Affairs Editor.
