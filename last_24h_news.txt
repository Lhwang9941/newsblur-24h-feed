The toll of the tragedy one month after Venezuela's double earthquake

Unknown Publisher

15:52:05 UTC
00:52:05 KST

One month after twin earthquakes in Venezuela, the official toll is over 5,500 dead and 16,700 injured; damage is put at 19.6 billion dollars. Thousands remain homeless and the number of missing has not been updated since late June.
Thirty days have passed since the two earthquakes, of magnitude 7.2 and 7.5, shook northern Venezuela within just 39 seconds on the night of 24 June. What the authorities described at the time as an unusually rare seismic phenomenon, a seismic doublet, has, as the weeks have gone by, turned into one of the worst natural disasters the country has experienced in more than a century.
A month on, the figures are still moving, although at a much slower pace than in the first few days, and there are still gaps the government has yet to fill. The most recent official toll, released last Friday by the president of the National Assembly, Jorge Rodríguez, puts the number of dead at 5,546 and the number of injured at 16,740.
The latter figure has barely budged for weeks, which suggests medical teams are no longer receiving new serious cases, while the death toll continues to rise as debris removal work progresses and forensic identification of bodies advances, many of them buried without being identified in mass graves set up in cemeteries in La Guaira.
The most sensitive figure, however, is the one the Venezuelan government is not updating. Since 25 June, the day after the seismic doublet, the authorities have not provided a new official figure for the missing, which at that point stood at 157 people. In that vacuum, a citizens’ initiative has emerged, Desaparecidos del Terremoto de Venezuela, which centralises reports from relatives and keeps a register of nearly 29,500 people whose whereabouts are unknown. That gap between what the state acknowledges and what citizens themselves are documenting is one of the most questioned aspects of the way the emergency has been handled.
Material damage and displacement
Beyond the lives lost, the earthquake has left a significant economic scar. A World Bank report published last week estimates direct physical damage at around 19.6 billion dollars.
Of that amount, 9.3 billion correspond to homes destroyed or severely damaged, 5.2 billion to public infrastructure, including hospitals, schools and other essential facilities, and nearly 5 billion to commercial and industrial buildings.
On the ground, the latest official count records:
- 856 buildings affected, 190 of which collapsed completely
- The number of people left without housing stands at 17,907, of whom 23,122 remain in 107 temporary camps set up in Caracas and La Guaira,
- 128,324 families have received some form of assistance from the authorities since the start of the emergency.
Since the main quake, 1,405 aftershocks have also been recorded, some strong enough to complicate work on structures that had already been weakened.
One month on, life is trying to resume
As the weeks go by, the search-and-rescue phase has given way to debris clearance and reconstruction, a process which the authorities themselves acknowledge will take months, if not years.
Some basic services, such as electricity in certain parts of La Guaira, were restored about two weeks after the quake, but normality remains partial. Organisations such as Médecins Sans Frontières are keeping mobile clinics operating at ground zero to meet healthcare needs that the public system, already weakened before the earthquake, is unable to cover fully.
The tragedy also struck at a politically sensitive moment for Venezuela, which is undergoing a transition following the arrest of former president Nicolás Maduro at the beginning of the year, with Delcy Rodríguez heading an interim government that has yet to set a date for elections.
The way international aid is being managed, including cooperation from countries such as Israel and efforts to unblock Venezuelan assets with United States support, is emerging as one of the factors that will determine the pace of reconstruction in the coming months.

Trump orders warning signs outside Smithsonian to claim national museum ‘does not present American history’
The signs will be installed by the National Parks Service along sidewalks and walkways near the museum
- Bookmark
- CommentsGo to comments
President Donald Trump has ordered that the Smithsonian Museum of American History post signs telling visitors its exhibits are presenting inaccurate information.
Trump issued an executive order on Friday ordering that the "warning" signs be placed in front of the museum informing visitors that they are about to be subjected to "inaccurate information presented in the Museum."
The decision comes on the heels of a July 4 report from the White House that ruled the Smithsonian “cannot be trusted to tell America’s story honestly and in a way that is inspiring, unifying, and worthy of our great republic.”
“Smithsonian leadership does not present American history as a shared national inheritance to be taught and celebrated, but instead views American history as a ‘prime tool’ to advance ideas of social justice and the radical transformation of our society,” Trump wrote in the executive order.
Trump has reportedly tasked his Interior Secretary, Dough Burgum, to install the signs on "sidewalks and walkways used by the public to access the Museum."
The signs will reportedly not only warn visitors against the Smithsonian, but also correct “inaccurate information presented in the Museum” and point readers to “locations and resources for accurate information regarding America’s history.”
The Smithsonian's Secretary Lonnie Bunch told NBC News that he believes White House report “is not a fair characterization of the work and totality of the National Museum of American History," adding that the institution's work is "driven by scholarship, accuracy and an uncompromising commitment to tell the fullness of America’s story."
Trump's signs disparaging the Smithsonian are only the latest in his efforts to repaint American history in MAGA colors.
In 2025, Trump issued an executive order aimed at restoring "truth and sanity to American history." The order directs federal officials to scrub national parks and federally-managed lands of any suggestion that the U.S. has ever been "inherently racist, sexist, or oppressive."
NPS workers had to pore over every sign and brochure and watch every film produced by the parks to ensure they were ideologically in-line with Trump's wishes. Disqualifying language included anything that "inappropriately disparages" Americans or any information "unrelated to the beautify, abundance, and grandeur of the American landscape."
The topics that were flagged for review by the Trump administration most frequently involved Native American history, slavery, the climate crisis, Black history, and the civil rights movement, according to a Guardian analysis of the materials.
George Washington's home in Philadelphia is one of exhibits affected by the order. A slavery memorial was installed at the site — where Washington enslaved nine people — but was ordered removed under Trump's executive orders.
The city of Philadelphia sued to have the memorial returned. In July, the NPS installed new informational panels at the slavery memorial while the lawsuit is litigated, but advocates claim the new panels minimize slavery to protect Washington's image.
The U.S. military has also been targeted for ideological cleansing under Trump. Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth barred military members from using any official resources to celebrate Black History Month, Women's History Month, and Asian American and Pacific Islander Heritage Month, and barred any "instruction on Critical Race Theory, DEI, or gender ideology."
Schools on military bases were also instructed, per Trump's ideological purity test, to remove hundreds of books from their libraries because they promoted "gender ideology and discriminatory equity ideology."
In October 2025 a federal judge ruled against Trump's order and demanded that the books be returned.
