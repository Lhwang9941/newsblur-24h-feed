At least 132 dead, 570 injured after Colombia quake: Cali and Pereira impose curfew

The cities of Cali, Pereira and Caldas have imposed night-time curfews after extensive damage from the 7.4-magnitude quake, to deter looting and support emergency crews.
The Colombian Association of Capital Cities reports a provisional toll of at least 132 dead and 570 injured in the departments hit by Monday's 7.4-magnitude earthquake -and its subsequent aftershocks-.
Cauca and Risaralda, located to the west of the so-called Coffee Axis, are the regions with the highest number of victims, according to the newspaper "El Espectador", with at least 60 and 15 deaths respectively, plus around 13 in Chocó. Other affected regions include Caldas and Quindío.
undefined
The epicentre was in San José del Palmar, in the south-east of Chocó, bordering Valle del Cauca and Risaralda and roughly equidistant from the cities of Cali and Medellín, with the former suffering the most damage to its infrastructure. Seismographs indicate that the natural disaster originated within the Nazca plate, at a depth of around 103 kilometres. This plate sinks beneath the South American plate that forms the Andes mountain range, causing friction and events of this kind.
Abelardo de la Espriella, who only recently took office as president of Colombia, travelled on Monday afternoon to Quibdó, capital of the department of Chocó, and later to Cali. As part of the reinforcement of emergency efforts, the latter city will receive 60 rescue workers from Bogotá and a further 32 members of the armed forces.
The far-right leader has announced that government representatives will head the unified command posts in the affected areas, with the departments ceding their powers on the matter. "I don't want anyone going off on their own", said the president, who will travel to the Coffee Axis in the coming days. "Information will come from the Office of the President of the Republic; from no other body of the Colombian state".
Cali and Pereira impose a curfew
The capital of Valle del Cauca has imposed a curfew between 20:00 on Monday and 6:00 on Tuesday and ordered the militarisation of the city in response to the emergency, which has left numerous structures damaged in Cali.
Mayor Alejandro Eder has justified the move as a way to allow relief agencies to focus their efforts on searching for and rescuing those people who may still be trapped under the rubble. "We are facing threats of looting, which is why I have ordered the curfew and the militarisation of Cali", Eder said, calling on citizens to report any attempted looting or incidents that threaten public safety.
undefined
His counterpart in the Colombian city of Pereira, Mauricio Salazar, also declared a curfew on Monday to prevent looting. "As of today at 18:00 we have imposed a curfew until 5:00 to protect businesses in the city. This curfew will apply in the city centre, in Comuna Universidad and in the Cuba subcentre", explained the mayor of the departmental capital of Risaralda.
In Caldas, governor Henry Gutiérrez Angel has likewise opted to impose a curfew to prevent looting and protect the population.
undefined
At the onset of the earthquake, this area suffered a blackout affecting electricity and mobile phone coverage. Up to six airports in the west of the country (Pereira, Manizales, Quibdó, Armenia, Cartago and Buenaventura) still have operations suspended. The tremor was felt in neighbouring countries far from the epicentre, such as Ecuador and Panama.
From Mexico, President Claudia Sheinbaum has expressed her solidarity with Colombia and said her country is ready to provide whatever support is needed in the emergency. Mexican media report that the Morena leader has made available to the Colombian authorities any assistance they may require.
Among those sending messages is María Corina Machado, who has conveyed her solidarity with those affected and underlined Venezuelans' experience of this type of disaster. "We know from first-hand experience how vital international solidarity is in the face of a tragedy of this magnitude", wrote the opposition leader 'de facto' in exile. This earthquake follows two consecutive quakes of magnitudes 7.2 and 7.5 off the Venezuelan coast at the end of June. Those quakes destroyed hundreds of buildings and claimed the lives of more than 5,000 people.
From Spain, Pedro Sánchez has also expressed his solidarity with the country and offered whatever assistance is needed (source in Spanish). For the time being, there are no reports of Spanish nationals among the fatalities, although the tally remains preliminary.

Russia is back in table tennis, flag and all
Unknown
Unknown Publisher
2026-08-11
06:43:27 UTC
15:43:27 KST

When Sweden’s Anton Källberg walks into the arena on Tuesday to face Vladimir Sidorenko at Europe Smash, the match carries a weight that reaches beyond the table.
Sidorenko is Russian, and unlike the Russian players who spent recent years competing as neutrals, he now takes the table representing Russia, under its flag and anthem.
The change follows a July decision by the International Table Tennis Federation (ITTF) to lift the restrictions on athletes holding Russian passports. It took effect on 28 July, and Russian players can now enter under the same ordinary rules as everyone else.
For critics, the question is hard to avoid: what has changed in Ukraine to justify the change in sport?
What changed in Ukraine? Nothing
Russia’s full-scale invasion has not ended. Yet the penalty imposed in its wake—that Russian athletes compete without representing the Russian state—has now been lifted in international table tennis.
The Swedish Table Tennis Association has openly opposed the move. Its chairman, Tomas Eriksson, said in July that he saw no change in Ukraine that would justify shifting Swedish sport’s position toward Russian participation. The association has said it will not boycott the tournaments.
That tension now lands on Swedish soil.
Källberg and Sidorenko were drawn against each other at Europe Smash, held in Malmö from 8 to 16 August.
For Swedish supporters of Ukraine, the evening presents a real dilemma. Nobody needs to aim hostility at Sidorenko himself, and no one is claiming individual Russian athletes bear responsibility for their government’s actions. The question is what an athlete is asked to represent.
A flag is never neutral
A neutral athlete competes as an individual. Someone introduced beneath a national flag stands in, however indirectly, for a state. Flags and anthems are written into international sport precisely because they carry national and political meaning.
So restoring the Russian flag is more than an administrative tweak. It is a statement—and a pointed one while Russia’s war on Ukraine continues.
The dispute has an unmistakable Swedish dimension. ITTF is headed by Petra Sörling, the Swedish official who was re-elected president of world table tennis in 2025 for another four-year term. She is a former president of the Swedish Table Tennis Association.
That does not make the reinstatement hers alone to decide; the ITTF Executive Board considered the issue on 10 July. But leadership carries responsibility. ITTF has justified the change by citing equal treatment and proportionality—yet proportionate to what change in circumstances? With Russia’s war on Ukraine still ongoing, the federation has yet to explain what has materially shifted to make the earlier restrictions disproportionate now.
On the evening of 11 August 2026, all of it narrows to something plainer: two players and a table, in front of a Swedish crowd.
Källberg deserves what any Swedish athlete would want at a major home tournament: loud, passionate backing from the crowd. And those who object to the return of the Russian flag have every right to voice it, peacefully, from the same seats.
Symbols cut both ways
Sport does not stand outside the world around it. International federations prove as much every time they raise a flag or set a country’s name beside an athlete.
If those symbols matter when a nation celebrates victory, they matter when a governing body decides who may use them.
And until the reasons for restricting Russia’s national representation have gone, the question facing table tennis stays simple: Why should the restriction disappear first?

Ferry worker whose request to work on land were ignored wins almost £50,000 compensation
The senior deck hand had repeatedly asked to be transferred to work on shore
- Bookmark
- CommentsGo to comments
A senior boatswain at a ferry company has won almost £50,000 after bosses refused to let him work on land when he complained that working at sea aggravated his arthritis because of the vibrations on the ship.
English Channel worker Sean Donnelly had been unable to work at sea as he was in 'a lot of pain physically' and the vibrations from the ship had made his osteoarthritis worse, a tribunal heard.
The senior deck hand had repeatedly asked to be transferred to work on shore, but he was told that it was 'too difficult due to payroll and administration'.
Mr Donnelly wanted to work for three more years until he retired but was left without pay and seeking benefits, despite making it clear he was fit to work ashore.
At an employment tribunal held in Ashford, Kent, Mr Donnelly won his case of unfair dismissal and a failure to make reasonable adjustment, which is a type of disability discrimination.
He has won £47,089.
Mr Donnelly started working at DFDS in their Guernsey branch in October 2007, and he had become an Assistant Bosun.
As the Assistant Bosun, he was in charge of loading the deck, mooring stations and anchoring operations.
He worked two weeks on, two weeks off with a night and day shift pattern, and lived on board when at sea.
In September 2022 he was diagnosed with osteoarthritis of the spine, and he notified the ferry company the next day.
Mr Donnelly went on sick leave but rejoined the boat in January 2023, having passed the medical examination to be able to rejoin.
He had a phased return, but after eight days, he had to leave because he was in too much pain.
One of the Chief Officers told Matt Scott, the Crewing Manager: "As I say, he is obviously in a lot of pain physically and I have no doubt he is suffering mentally…I would suggest that at the moment [Mr Donnelly] is a long way off being capable of doing his job.
"Perhaps with more time he may be able to return to work but that is a decision for someone better qualified than me."
He said that Mr Donnelly was only planning to continue working for another three years until he retired but that he was keen to continue until this point.
Mr Scott said that he would make enquiries into other roles Mr Donnelly could do and said there was a possibility of computer-based work in the shore-side role but that they had had a number of staff from ship go to operations and that everyone making that transition has to pass a screening program.
In March 2023 Mr Donnelly discovered he had exhausted his sick pay, despite not being notified about this. He had not received notice of this and he raised a complaint about this.
He said that getting treatment for his arthritis was 'financially crippling' and he had to rely on benefits.
After speaking to a specialist, it was made clear to Mr Donnelly that he should enquire about jobs on land because the 'vibrations' on the ships were making his arthritis worse.
He inquired, but he was told that it was 'too difficult' because it was the sister company DFDS A/S who dealt with employees on land.
Mr Donnelly had a meeting with Lisa Abbott, Head of Marine HR, and told her that he was not hopeful he would be able to return to sea and so would be looking to be deployed to a job on shore if there was any availability.
In October 2023, Mr Donnelly had received treatment through physiotherapy and injections to help his pain.
He said that his condition meant that a number of adjustments could be taken to help him work on the ship such as an adjustment in his hours, having a support worker and not doing the forklifting duties.
Mr Donnelly said that despite making himself available to work, he had been removed from the payroll and that he could be temporarily deployed to shore, but this had been denied twice.
He was told that there were no available vacancies on shore because they had recently filled them with seasonal staff.
Mr Donnelly lodged a formal grievance, but the ferry company said that his redeployment had not been denied because he had asked them to wait for further medical assessment.
At an appeal hearing, Mr Donnelly said that he was told the move to onshore would have been 'too difficult due to payroll and administration'.
The company said: "DFDS A/S and DFDS Guernsey are two separate businesses and a transfer of roles is not something they have control over."
In March 2024, Mr Donnelly gave a list of reasonable adjustments and said that they could agree to these so he could work at sea, or he could be assigned to a position on land that didn't require heavy lifting.
He said that if the company did not agree to any of these, then it would effectively be terminating his employment.
The ferry company said that it was trying to get him back to sea but did not agree to his reasonable adjustments.
Employment Judge Anna Corrigan said that they had unfairly constructively dismissed Mr Donnelly.
She said: "The resignation letter is [Mr Donnelly's] email of 27 March 2024 read with the email of 8 April 2024.
"We agree with [him] that he resigned in part because of [DFDS'] failure to make the reasonable adjustments.
"We also consider that it was [DFDS'] failure to respond and/or ignoring his multiple requests to consider shore-side work."
Judge Corrigan added: "[Mr Donnelly] resigned because of [DFDS'] ongoing failure to consider and facilitate redeployment shore-side and the failure to return him promptly to the payroll and to his role at sea once he had his ENG1.
"We find the breach in respect of the latter was in not considering the OH report and recommended adjustments promptly or at all, leading to their response to the ENG1, and that was a last straw which added to the breach."
Judge Corrigian said that some adjustments should have been made by DFDS to allow him to return to work.
She said: "It was reasonable to expect [DFDS] to take steps from February 2023 to facilitate a temporary secondment or redeployment into the Operations Coordinator role on shore-side following the steer from the ship’s line management.
"This would have involved supporting [Mr Donnelly] in passing the screening programme as soon as he was fit enough to work.
"We do not accept that the two companies are so separate that [DFDS] had no influence over a transfer or redeployment."
