Congress breaks for the summer as they head home trying to save their jobs but saddled with Trump
Congress left Washington with homework left undone. Eric Garcia writes about what to watch when the House and Senate are away
- Bookmark
- CommentsGo to comments
School’s out for summer...or something like that for Congress.
After a marathon series of votes Friday evening into Saturday morning, the Senate broke for recess. That comes as the House already broke for it two weeks ago. The Senate confirmed Todd Blanche to be attorney general, passed a stopgap spending bill known as a continuing resolution to avoid a government shutdown and a bill to sanction Russia named for the late Sen. Lindsey Graham.
As much as House and Senate leadership routinely says that the August recess is not vacation, but rather a “district work period,” it’s hard to shake the idea that Congress has unfinished business. The stopgap funding bill means that the Senate has not crafted the 12 spending bills that are supposed to keep the government open, putting pressure on the most vulnerable incumbent Republican senator: Susan Collins of Maine, the Appropriations Committee chairwoman.
In addition, while the House passed its version of the National Defense Authorization Act, which is rife with anti-trans and culture war riders, the Senate has yet to pass its version due to Democrats objecting to the War in Iran.
And unsurprisingly, Senate Republicans have not passed the SAVE America Act, President Donald Trump’s top priority legislation that would not only require identification to vote, but also proof of citizenship when registering. The president has said that Democrats can only win elections by cheating even though there is little evidence of his claims that Democrats rig elections.
The president had suggested that the Senate cancel its August recess, which Senate Majority Leader John Thune roundly swatted down. And when Republican gadflies like Sen. Mike Lee of Utah had the opportunity to object to the Senate adjourning, they did not take it.
But whether it’s a recess, a work period, campaign season for the midterms or something else, don’t expect it to be easy for either side.
All of the members of the House will head home with many hoping they are reelected for another two-year term. A third of the Senate will also be asking voters in November to choose them. That makes this summer prime campaign season in home districts - and the fall’s decision will shape who controls DC for the remainder of Trump’s term.
On the Republican side, they have to reckon with the fact that ever since passing the One Big, Beautiful Bill, they have not done much in terms of passing major legislation. And voters still are not entirely sold on the legislation’s merits.
Republicans have sought to rebrand the law as the “Working Families Tax Cuts Act,” but it does not seem to have done much to improve American perception of the GOP. A Fox News poll from July showed that 54 percent of voters think that Democrats would handle the economy better. The same poll showed that 54 percent of voters also think Democrats would do a better job on inflation, the primary reason voters put Trump back in the Oval Office.
Voters have also turned off Trump with numerous polls showing unapproval no matter the area of the nation.
But that does not mean that Democrats are home free. The party still faces the internal war between the establishment and the insurgent left. Last week, Abdul El-Sayed won the Democratic nomination for Senate in Michigan despite millions of dollars by pro-Israel and other outside money groups for Rep. Haley Stevens.
This week, there will be another tussle in Wisconsin. Democrats hope that their anointed candidate David Crowley can stop socialist candidate Francesca Hong. The stakes are especially high in the perpetual swing state, given Democrats have a chance to control all three branches for the first time in more than a decade. Gov. Tony Evers has thrown his weight behind Crowley to prevent a candidate who has made a series of inflammatory statements about abolishing police and canceling Thanksgiving from winning the primary.
Then, in neighboring Minnesota, Lt. Gov. Peggy Flanagan, the preferred candidate of progressives, squares off against moderate Rep. Angie Craig for the nomination to replace Sen. Tina Smith.
The stakes are decidedly lower here, but Flanagan has signaled that she would not support Chuck Schumer as Senate Democratic leader. So far, Juliana Stratton, the Democratic nominee for Senate, has said the same, as has El-Sayed. If they win their seats, this bloc could cause a world of trouble for Schumer.
All of this to say that this will be far from a dull moment during the primary. For Republicans, they are trying to find a way to shake off an unpopular president during the tenth year of him being atop the party during a sluggish economy and an unpopular war. Democrats, for their part, want figure out who they are ahead of the midterm, but also to craft a winning message for 2028.
Let’s get ready to recess.

A ChatGPT-generated image, a photo of Hiroshima, and a line from a Soviet song: the copyright case that helped boot Russia’s only antiwar party off next month’s ballot
Russia’s Supreme Court has ruled to remove the opposition party Yabloko from the ballot in the election for the State Duma, the lower house of Russia’s parliament. The Rodina party filed the lawsuit last Friday, accusing Yabloko of an assortment of violations that included copyright infringement, illegal campaigning, and support for extremism. The case went before Judge Vyacheslav Kirillov, who declared the human rights group International Memorial “extremist” this spring. The ruling has not taken legal effect, and Yabloko says it will appeal.
Rodina cast Yabloko’s antiwar stance as incitement to extremism and to the violation of Russia’s territorial integrity. In court, a representative for the plaintiff set out the logic behind the accusation: “Some of Russia’s territories are occupied by a neo-Nazi regime. Calls to conclude an immediate cease-fire agreement are calls to stop the liberation of occupied territories or to abandon their liberation.” Rodina also cast Yabloko’s opposition to the persecution of the LGBTQ community as support for extremism.
The copyright claims were even more absurd. Rodina’s list included Yabloko’s use of phrases from the Soviet songs “Let There Always Be Sunshine” and “As Long As There’s No War,” which it said violated the songwriters’ rights. Publishing images generated by ChatGPT, it said, infringed the rights of the company that owns the neural network; using a photograph of the atomic bombing of Hiroshima infringed on the rights of the U.S. Army. Rodina also called Yabloko’s logo plagiarism of a poster the Soviet artist El Lissitzky made more than 100 years ago.
Nikolai Rybakov, Yabloko’s leader, rejected all the accusations. He said Rodina was faulting Yabloko simply for holding political positions and an election platform that differ from the current authorities’ course. “Different parties can’t shape the same public opinion. Citizens have different opinions, and different parties should express them. … I don’t know how to explain to the plaintiff’s representatives that we’re supported by people who don’t want to be killed,” Rybakov said in his closing statement.
The Central Election Commission and the prosecutor’s office backed Yabloko’s removal from the race, citing copyright infringement. The commission also said it had received information that some candidates on Yabloko’s federal list for the State Duma had received foreign funding. Yabloko attributed this to a requirement that candidates sell all foreign securities before the vote. The party also said it had received donations from Russian citizens who had themselves received money from foreign sources.
Hundreds of people came to the Supreme Court to support Yabloko, most of them teenagers and young adults. They ate apples (yabloko means “apple” in Russian), chanted the party’s name, and wrote messages on a sheet of paper, among them “We don’t need Mala Tokmachka,” a front-line village in Ukraine that has been fought over for years. Police watched the crowd. No mass detentions were reported, though riot police took two young women carrying Yabloko leaflets “for a talk” and fined the driver of a car that had been circling near the court playing a song called “Yabloko.” After the court issued its ruling, the crowd began chanting “Shame!” and police ordered the gathering to disperse.
Yabloko was the only party with an antiwar position set to run in the State Duma election. Yulia Navalnaya and many other Russian opposition figures in exile called on people to support it. Yabloko itself disavowed that support, saying the backing only increased the risks to the party under current conditions. Even before Rodina filed its lawsuit, the leaders of every party in Russia’s systemic opposition had come out against Yabloko: the Communist Party, New People, the Liberal Democratic Party, and A Just Russia.
