Add as a favourite source on Google Add DutchNews as a favourite source on GooglePublic transport workers across the Netherlands are to stage a one-day strike on Wednesday, September 9, bringing buses, trains, trams and metro services almost to a standstill.
The FNV union has called the strike in the latest escalation of its campaign against the government’s social welfare reforms, which it says will lead to lower living standards for millions of working people.
The three major unions FNV, CNV and VCP have vowed to fight the cabinet’s social welfare reforms, which are designed to secure €6.5 billion in cost savings during the goverment’s four-year term.
Social affairs minister Hans Vijlbrief scrapped a plan to increase the statutory retirement age faster by raising it in step with life expectancy, but the unions want the cabinet to go further by abandoning cuts to unemployment pay and long-term disability benefits.
Staff working for NS, ProRail, urban and regional transport sevices and DB cargo will down tools for 24 hours on September 9, making it impossible to run timetables.
Delays inevitable
“We are aware that a 24-hour strike will cause disruption,” said FNV official Edwim Kuiper. “We regret that deeply. But the consequences of the dismantling of our social security are much greater for everyone and will last much longer.”
National train operator NS said it could not say exactly what the impact of the stoppage would be because it had not been officially notified by the union.
Lobby organisation OV-NL, which represents nine smaller public transport companies such as Arriva, QBuzz and the major city network operators GVB, RET and HTM, said it “understood up to a point” the reason to call a strike.
But it said it was “very unfortunate that passengers are being disadvantaged by a social and political debate”.
Union leader Henri Janssen said that the strike was being called on behalf of commuters. “Every working person has to deal with sickness, work incapacity or involuntary unemployment. They should be able to count on a decent social safety net,“ he said.

Around 25,000 migrants returned to Morocco after mass border crossing into Ceuta

Some 25,000 of the roughly 60,000 people who crossed illegally into Ceuta since the early hours of Friday have already been returned to Morocco.
Nearly half of the 60,000 migrants who entered the Spanish enclave of Ceuta since the early hours of Friday have already returned to Morocco, according to Spain's Interior Ministry, as cited by local media.
The estimate covers the period up to 1 p.m. local time on Friday, meaning the number is expected to increase as returns continue. Local media also reported that at least 34 people died while attempting to swim from Morocco to Ceuta.
The mass arrival of migrants has overwhelmed the Spanish authorities and humanitarian organisations in the autonomous city, which has a population of just under 83,000. The latest arrivals come on top of the 1,500 to 2,000 migrants who had already reached Ceuta in recent days.
In response, Spain and Morocco reached an agreement on Thursday to speed up the return of migrants who entered the enclave irregularly.
The response from Madrid
The scale of the arrivals prompted the Spanish government to deploy the army to help reinforce security in Ceuta. Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez travelled to the enclave on Friday, accompanied by Interior Minister Fernando Grande-Marlaska.
Speaking from Ceuta, Sánchez described the mass crossings as "a violation of Spain's territorial integrity", adding that they "deserve our strongest condemnation".
Defence Minister Margarita Robles also urged migrants not to risk the journey. "Do not be deceived by the trafficking networks," she said. "The outcome will be return, in accordance with Spanish law."
Overnight, clashes broke out between Moroccan police and hundreds of people who continued trying to reach Ceuta. Migrants were still attempting to enter the Spanish enclave on Friday by swimming around the Tarajal breakwater near the main border crossing between Morocco and Ceuta.
