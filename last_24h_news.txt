How Germany supports recovery of Berlin Pride attack victims
August 16, 2026
On the evening of July 25, according to German authorities, Abdul Ballout, drove a white van into a crowd in Berlin's Tiergarten park near the Brandenburg Gate, where thousands were celebrating Berlin Pride. He then got out of the vehicle and began attacking people at random with a large knife. He killed a 65-year-old woman from Poland and injured her daughter, along with 30 other people, some of them seriously.
Many details have emerged about the perpetrator, his links to extremists, his criminal record and his family. Two days after the incident, he was shot dead by police officers while trying to flee during an arrest attempt.
The identities of the victims, and how they are coping after the attack on the event celebrating LGBTQ+ rights, are not publicly known. Still, many are likely to live with its consequences for months, if not years, to come. The only details disclosed by Berlin's governing mayor concern the age and nationality of the woman who was killed. Otherwise, Germany's strict data protection laws protect victims' privacy.
Victims can approach the media themselves at any time if they choose to make their stories public, Roland Weber, the German federal government's commissioner for victims of terrorism and extremist violence, told DW.
Support for the victims
In Germany, people affected by such attacks are often reluctant to come forward publicly or identify themselves as victims, says Julia Kühne. She should know: Kühne is the spokesperson for the Association for Victims of Terrorism (VoT Germany), which is part of an international federation of terrorism victims.
"The perpetrator needs to disappear from the media, now that he has been caught and killed. Those affected should naturally be brought much more to the forefront," Kühne told DW.
How are those who were injured in the attack doing today? Weber has visited some of them in hospital.
"What I saw there was distressing. But thanks to modern medicine, and because the doctors are optimistic, there is reason for hope that everyone will recover as fully as possible," he said. "In some cases, however, that remains uncertain. Only time will tell. Some of the injured have already been discharged from hospital and are now receiving outpatient treatment. Others will begin various forms of therapy and rehabilitation."
How are the victims being supported?
The Federal Commissioner for Victims and "Weisser Ring", a victim support organization, arrange trauma treatment, provide immediate financial assistance, and advise victims on long-term care and victims' pensions.
Weisser Ring is a volunteer-run organization that works closely with the police to provide advice and support to victims of crime in Germany.
"In cases of terrorist attacks, there is also a special provision: victims can apply for hardship payments," says Weber. "These are funds made available by the Bundestag. Hardly anyone knows about them, and making people aware of them is part of our job. We provide guidance and help ensure that applications are submitted as quickly as possible."
Weber is also responsible for supporting the relatives of the Polish woman who was killed and her daughter, who was injured in the attack. Nationality is not a factor in this regard. He assists victims with follow-up costs and compensation whenever an act of terrorism or extremist violence has been committed on German soil.
Number of traumatized victims still unclear
The victims of the attack are not only those who sustained physical injuries. According to VoT Germany's Kühne, even people who witnessed the attack from several hundred meters away may be considered victims.
"There are people who do not develop problems until years later," she said. "They may suddenly begin to suffer from sleep disturbances, experience nightmares, develop panic attacks or struggle with social phobia. In some cases, these symptoms do not emerge until years afterward because those affected were initially either still in shock or had returned to their everyday lives and were therefore able, at least to some extent, to suppress what they had experienced."
Kühne also notes that survivors of previous terrorist attacks may be retraumatized by media coverage of the current attack in Berlin and find themselves in need of support once again. She knows this from personal experience: she herself survived the 2016 attack on Berlin's Christmas market, which ultimately claimed 13 lives and left more than 70 injured.
Victim support: Problems in practice
Weisser Ring, which supports victims of crime across Germany, believes that adequate compensation schemes and pension entitlements for victims of terrorism are now in place. However, not everything runs smoothly in practice, says Rolf Schmachtenberg, the organization's state chairman in Berlin.
"In cases where people have suffered permanent harm, the assessment procedures are often quite lengthy. We also continue to encounter administrative bottlenecks, with some authorities still relying on paper files and fax communications. They then have to wait for information from other agencies, which can significantly delay the application process. In my view, the main problem is one of implementation."
Over the past decade, Germany has experienced 13 terrorist attacks motivated by Islamist or political extremist ideologies, leaving 39 people dead and 475 injured. The support provided by the federal government's commissioner for victims has no time limit. Roland Weber continues to assist people affected by attacks dating back to the 1980s.
Victims seek answers
For many victims, coming to terms with the horror and suffering also means finding answers to the question of why the perpetrator acted and why they, in particular, were targeted, says Weber. In some court proceedings, perpetrators have remained silent. In the case of the Berlin Pride attack, however, the perpetrator was killed, meaning there will never be complete clarity about his motives.
In Germany, investigations are discontinued once a suspect has died. No court will ever determine whether Abdul Ballout selected his victims for one particular reason or another.
"As a result, some questions will remain unanswered,"Weber told DW. "The search for answers is something most victims have in common, but it cannot always lead to certainty."

‘Excellent profit’: Meet the Bavarian hop farmer securing his harvest and income with solar 'hats'

No rain, no hops – no hops, no beer. Climate change, heat and dry farmland are hitting Bavaria’s key crop. Could solar panels save it?
A wave of farm closures is washing over Germany’s lush hop-growing regions as climate change, falling prices and declining demand for beer leave desolation in their wake.
As of 2026, there are just over 900 agricultural businesses in Germany specialising in hops – a dramatic drop of 40 per cent since two decades ago, with many farmers either switching to other crops or giving up altogether.
The phenomenon is so acute, it has even earned its own name: ‘höfesterben’ describes the creeping structural decline and closing of small and family-owned farms.
Increasingly, even traditional Bavarian farmers now speak openly of climate change as rising temperatures, water shortages and dry soils squeeze their yields.
Beer is integral to their region’s cultural identity, with school summer holidays timed to coincide with hop harvests replete with ‘hop queens’. Bavaria produces 86 per cent of German hops and roughly one third of global output.
The Hallertau, in the heart of the southeast German region, is the largest continuous hop-growing area in the world. Dating back to at least 860 AD, it is home to 722 of the country’s remaining hop farms.
One of them is run by fourth-generation hop grower Josef Wimmer. “The years are getting hotter and drier,” he tells Euronews Earth.
To secure the future of his farmstead in the small hamlet of Neuhub near Osseltshausen, drastic action was a necessity.
Could solar 'hats' save German hop farms?
Seven metres above the ground on Wimmer’s farm, solar panels glint, forming a sea of pale blue.
“We wanted to shade the hops because hops are a shade-loving plant,” the farmer explains. Heat slows the production of alpha acids, the compound that gives beer its pleasantly aromatic bitterness. Heat-related drought and the ensuing decline in soil health can also spark dramatic drops in yield.
Of course, the photovoltaic modules come with another key benefit: providing renewable energy for the farm. “In the first step we covered five hectares of hops with PV,” says Wimmer. “That gives us an output of two megawatts at its peak.”
He first came up with the idea 10 years ago, after seeing the benefits of rooftop solar he installed in 2009. His neighbours were awash with skepticism: “There were voices saying ‘Wimmer’s lost it now, that’ll never work!’,” he recalls.
“When we started building, we became the hot spot of the Hallertau. Everyone came by to have a look. And after every big storm… everyone wanted to know whether the installation was still standing or had been blown away,” Wimmer laughs. “But everything is still standing, even after the storms.”
After the curious neighbours came the hop farmers from more distant villages to inspect the system. They were followed by local councillors and district officials, right up to ministers from the Bavarian state capital Munich.
Now Wimmer plans to expand his successful project over 20 hectares and add a one-megawatt battery storage system.
How red tape is slowing farm solar installations
He got there with the help of Bernhard Gruber, a former Airbus engineer turned solar innovator who ran with Willmer’s unconventional idea.
After two years of planning and permitting negotiations, a five kilometre power cable is being installed to connect the panels to a substation capable of sending excess energy to the grid.
“Long approval procedures are a problem. They put some farmers off going for agri-PV on a larger scale, because the bureaucratic effort is simply too great for many of them,” Gruber tells Euronews Earth.
“Especially for hop growing, but also for other agricultural specialty crops, the decisions and approvals could be made much more easily. Why things aren’t moving faster is honestly a mystery to me,” he adds.
Agri-PV keeps soil moist – and hops like that
“We’ve found that the soil has become wetter since the agri-PV modules were installed,” Wimmer says, pointing to a moisture sensor tucked among the trellises, which takes measurements both at the surface and deeper down.
“Evaporation is lower, we can keep the water from rainfall in the soil for longer – and that is good for the hops, especially in June, July and August. That’s when the hops need water.”
Balancing the right amount of shade with a solar panel angle conducive to energy production from the sun is no easy feat.
Gruber puzzled over daily and seasonal movements of the sun, hourly fluctuations in power prices, and the outcomes for the crops.
Too much shade resulted in the hop plants producing bushy shoots in the shaded upper zone – but fewer cones. Overall yields fell and less hop extract was produced.
After two years of tweaks, he achieved a happy medium, settling on a 45-degree angle for the panels instead of the original 20 degrees. This means the panels receive slightly less direct sunlight, but the hops produce more cones.
Less energy but higher income
Despite producing less energy, the steeper panels actually earn Willmer more money.
If the modules are almost flat (as in the first prototype), the PV system generates most power around midday, precisely at the time when there is already surplus power in the grid and producer prices are accordingly low.
Positioned at a steeper angle, the solar system starts working efficiently from the early morning, as soon as the sun rises, or, depending on orientation, in the late afternoon and early evening – the times when electricity prices are high.
The 45-degree solution also boosts energy production in the winter, when the sun sits low on the horizon for longer periods.
“And in winter in particular you need every kilowatt hour,” says Gruber.
“At the moment we’re looking at a payback period of around 14 years, and the PV system will last at least 30 years,” the engineer calculates. “So you can imagine how much you can earn.”
Cutting-edge research in the hop garden
Wimmer and Gruber are not alone out in the fields. Their pilot project is the subject of several research programmes. Their cooperation and funding partners include the University of Applied Sciences Weihenstephan–Triesdorf, the internationally renowned Fraunhofer Institute for Solar Energy Systems in Freiburg, and the Federal Office for Agriculture and Food.
The Technical University of Munich, with its agricultural research station at Dürnast, and the Hop Research Centre in Hüll are also providing scientific support for the agri-PV project. Last but not least, the Agriculture Ministry is funding the three-year trial phase with €1.4 million in research money.
Analysis by Weihenstephan–Triesdorf university reveals that Wimmer’s agri-PV installation has resulted in a 10 to 20 per cent reduction in hop yields. But the farmer says these losses are more than made up for: “The profit from electricity generation is excellent, really very good. The strong power yield offsets the lower hop yield… It’s worth it.”
And with only two grams of hop extract needed to brew one litre of beer, one kilo of hops can be turned into 500 litres.
“In the end this is a win-win situation,” agrees Gruber. “You’re not only earning money from electricity, you’re also earning secure money from the hop harvest.”
“Even in the part of the year when no hops are growing, the soil is still partially shaded by the PV modules,” he adds. “This keeps the soil’s moisture profile more uniform overall – and that means the land is better prepared for the next planting season.”
Researchers are now testing whether the significant improvements in soil health beneath 20-degree panels are retained under a 45-degree shade.
Could agri-PV slow the decline of farming in Europe’s south?
As human-caused global warming continues to shift Europe’s climate, traditional growing areas, developed over centuries, may have to be abandoned for cooler climes.
It’s a pattern already troubling winemakers, and bringing new opportunities for vineyards in places like Scandinavia and the UK.
Bavaria’s hop farmers jokingly quip among themselves that in 30 years’ time the hops for good Bavarian beer may have to be imported from Norway. Sooner or later, some hop farmers in the Hallertau fear it could turn into bitter reality.
Farmers are desperately searching for solutions but not everyone can afford to install costly irrigation systems, which are also politically controversial due to their high water use.
Some hop growers are experimenting with cover crops between the hop rows to reduce evaporation, while researchers are breeding varieties that can cope better with drought.
Others have been forced to bid a reluctant farewell to the hop business – in some cases carpeting their former farmland with solar panels instead.
Wimmer has thought about this too: “I could have said, fine, I’ll rip out my hops and install ground-mounted solar instead of hop gardens everywhere. But then I’d no longer be a farmer, just a PV entrepreneur,” he says.
‘Energy must never compete with food production’
“For me it’s about preserving hop-growing on my land,” says Wimmer. “I want to hand the farm on to the next generation in a way that gives it security and a future – but with additional income from photovoltaics.”
On a broader scale, adds Gruber, “Energy must never compete with food production” – and agri-PV could be an important way to ensure both thrive in harmony.
Already, agri-PV trials have cropped up over vineyards, berry patches, apple orchards and asparagus fields in Germany.
“What we’re doing could become a model for the whole of Europe,” says Gruber, as French hop farmers battle sandy, drifting soils and drought, while Czech, Slovenian, Slovakian, Polish and Austrian hop growers face similar challenges.
With extreme dry spells worsening in Europe – Germany’s ‘drought monitor’ at the Helmholtz Centre for Environmental Research shows extreme droughts in 1976, 2003 and then almost continuously since 2018 – solutions like Wimmer’s could be a lifeline.

Exploring Andorra: The best things to do in the microstate aside from skiing and shopping
Unknown
Unknown Publisher
2026-08-16

14:00:40 KST

The highest capital city on the continent, Andorra la Vella boasts historic buildings, impressive sculptures and the largest thermal spa in southern Europe.
When I told my hairdresser I’d just come back from a trip to Andorra, their immediate response was, “I couldn’t point to it on a map”.
Given how little the country hits the headlines, that’s hardly surprising.
Located in the east Pyrenees between France and Spain, some 85,000 people call Andorra home, with around 8 million tourists descending on the country each year. To put that into context, France, the world’s most-visited country, has 102 million tourists a year, with Spain also potentially hitting 100 million visitors in 2026.
Spanning some 468 square kilometres, it’s technically the largest of the six European microstates (the others being Vatican City, Monaco, Malta, Liechtenstein and San Marino), but it’s not exactly on the typical tourist route – unless you’re a skier, that is.
Ahead of my trip in July this year, its ski resorts were the only thing I associated with the country. And, given I have zero experience in the winter sports department, it’s not exactly been on the top of my to-visit list.
Still, with more and more of us opting for lesser-known destinations as tourist hotspots grapple with overtourism, I thought it was high time – quite literally, as Andorra la Vella is the highest capital city in Europe – for me to see what’s on offer outside of the slopes.
Things to do in Andorra la Vella
Snap a photo with Salvador Dalí’s La Noblesse du Temps
In tourist destinations across the globe, there are certain “shots” that are a must-capture when visiting. In Santorini, it’s the blue domes of Oia; in Dubai, it’s the Burj Khalifa as seen from near the fountains.
In Andorra, the frame centres on Salvador Dalí’s La Noblesse du Temps statue with the Puente de Paris in the background, helpfully decorated with a large “Andorra la Vella” sign, in case it wasn’t clear where you were.
Thankfully, you won’t have to battle crowds to get a good shot of the sculpture, which was installed in its current spot in 2010 having been gifted to the Andorra government by Enric Sabater, a close friend of the artist, in 1999.
The bronze work, which weighs around 1,400 kilos, features a melting clockface set on a tree trunk. The crown above the clock “represents the dominion of time over humanity”, according to the Andorra La Vella tourism board.
Walk the Rec del Solà
For another spectacular view of Andorra La Vella, consider walking the Rec del Solà.
The short route runs along an irrigation channel in the hills near the capital, offering views of the city from above, and you can find exact details for the trail on the Visit Andorra website.
The website advises it takes up to three hours, but I completed it in less than an hour. From the end of the trail, you can reach the pretty old quarter in just 15 minutes.
Learn about the country’s history at Casa de la Vall
Speaking of the Barri Antic, aka the old quarter, if you only visit one museum or gallery during your trip, make it Casa de la Vall.
The historic home dates back to 1580, and served as the seat of the General Council of Andorra until 2011.
Here, you can learn about the origins of the co-principality, with the current heads of state being the Bishop of Urgell and the President of France, as well as the history of the flag and coat of arms. You’ll also be able to see the cabinet of the seven keys, which has locks for each of the parishes of Andorra.
Next door, you can see the new parliament, and you can also visit Església de Sant Esteve, one of the country’s most historic churches.
See the 7 Poets
In the plaza below the new parliament, you’ll be able to see another of Andorra’s most recognisable artworks: 7 Poets by Jaume Plensa.
Installed in 2014, the seven fibreglass figures have been placed on pedestals and are lit up at night.
According to the artist, “Poets are an integral part of society. They are condemned to failure, because they know they won’t even sell three books, but they are a source of fertility, like a light drizzle that penetrates the earth. In my opinion, this long process, akin to that of a farmer, is highly courageous.”
Splash the cash on the Shopping Mile
With some of the lowest rates of VAT in Europe, Andorra is known for its shopping.
Plenty cross the border to do their weekly food shop, but the main Shopping Mile is also home to many well-known international clothing brands, if that’s your cup of tea.
Explore the country’s spa heritage through the Caldes Project
The final ‘S’ associated with Andorra after skiing and shopping is spas.
The natural hot water springs found in Escaldes were originally used for the country’s textile industry until the first thermal hotels were opened here in the mid-19th century.
Today, you can explore that history with a walk alongside Javier Balmaseda’s Water and Form, which features a series of pipes, fountains and washhouses next to the river between the Engordany Bridge and the Tosca Bridge.
If you care less about the history and more about experiencing the springs for yourself, book a visit to Caldea, the largest thermal spa in southern Europe.
See the fountain show in front of Puente de Paris
As night falls, the Passeig de les Fonts de la Rotonda promenade in front of Puente de Paris plays host to a water and light fountain show.
The time differs depending on the season (more information can be found on the Andorra La Vella website), as does the choice of music, but no matter which songs play, it’s a nice way to end a day of exploration.
Where to eat and drink in Andorra
Andorra is known for its bordes, aka farmhouse restaurants, which are typically found outside of the city centre.
If you’re looking for a quick energy boost via carbs that won’t cost an arm and a leg, swing by Laganum, which serves freshly made pasta.
Next time I’ll visit Andorra for…
As picturesque as Andorra is in the summer, I imagine a fresh sprinkling of snow makes the scenery even prettier.
Just don’t expect to see me on the slopes. Unless it’s for après-ski drinks…
How to get to Andorra
The nearest major international airports to Andorra are Toulouse–Blagnac and Josep Tarradellas Barcelona–El Prat.
On the way to Andorra, I took a bus from Lleida, which took around two and a half hours. For the return journey, I went to Barcelona, which took around three hours. I booked both buses through Andbus.

Kėdainiai: A Lithuanian cultural capital shaped by Scots and the Radziwills
Unknown
Unknown Publisher
2026-08-16
05:00:00 UTC
14:00:00 KST

This year, the title of Lithuanian Capital of Culture belongs to Kėdainiai, a historic town once ruled by the powerful Radziwill family and shaped by centuries of encounters between different peoples and faiths.
Located in central Lithuania, Kėdainiai is perhaps best known as the country’s “cucumber capital”. But its compact historic centrr offers a richer story, with centuries-old churches and synagogues, distinctive architecture, a forgotten river port and traditional crafts that are still practiced today.
The town’s multicultural heritage is particularly visible in its houses of worship.
According to Rimantas Žirgulis, director of the Kėdainiai Regional Museum, the town was especially diverse during its period of prosperity in the 17th century, when six ethnic and religious communities lived there.
Today, visitors can see an Orthodox church housed in a brick building dating to the second half of the 19th century. The Church of the Transfiguration of Christ retains early 20th-century paintings and valuable icons.
Two Catholic churches also form part of Kėdainiai’s architectural heritage.
The Gothic Church of St George, built in the 15th century, is one of Lithuania’s oldest churches. It contains notable works of art, late Baroque altars and a relic of Blessed Teofilius Matulionis.
The wooden Church of St Joseph, dating to the 18th century, stands with its bell tower near the centre of town. It is known for paintings of St Joseph and the Virgin Mary with the infant Jesus, both featuring ornate metal coverings.
“The Church of St George is a unique 15th-century brick church, one of the oldest in Lithuania. The Church of St Joseph is wooden and dates to the 18th century,” Žirgulis told LRT.lt. “It is also unusual that what looks like a village church stood in the middle of what was, for its time, a large town.”
Kėdainiai’s religious heritage also reflects its once-powerful Protestant communities.
A 17th-century Evangelical Reformed church contains a mausoleum of the Radziwill family and historic pipe organs. The mausoleum was the first restored 17th-century burial site of Lithuanian Grand Duchy nobles in the country. It contains the sarcophagi of several members of the Radziwill family, including Krzysztof I “Perkūnas”, Janusz II, Mikolaj, Jerzy, Stepan and Elzbieta.
The church was once the principal Evangelical Reformed church in Samogitia and later became the main church of the Lithuanian Reformed community, Žirgulis said.
Another landmark is the 17th-century brick Evangelical Lutheran church, which retains original frescoes depicting the apostles Peter and Paul and the four evangelists.
The church reflects the influence of Kėdainiai’s German Lutheran community, Žirgulis said. It stood in Jonušava, then a suburb or separate settlement named after Radziwill nobleman Janusz Radziwill.
The town’s Jewish heritage is equally prominent.
On Senosios Rinkos Square stands a synagogue complex consisting of the Great Summer Synagogue and the Small Winter Synagogue. The Great Synagogue was rebuilt in the Baroque style after a fire in the 18th century and now houses the Kėdainiai Art School. The 19th-century Neoclassical Small Synagogue houses a multicultural centre with an exhibition about the town’s Jewish community and the Holocaust.
The complex is one of only three surviving synagogue complexes in Lithuania.
A historic town centre
The entire Old Town of Kėdainiai is a major attraction. It is a state-protected urban heritage site and one of just seven such sites in Lithuania.
Its compact streets preserve elements of Baroque, Gothic, Renaissance and Neoclassical architecture. Four historic market squares – Senosios Rinkos, Didžiosios Rinkos, Knypavos Rinkos and Janušavos – testify to the town’s role as a regional commercial centre.
Among the landmarks is the former 17th-century town hall on Didžiosios Rinkos Square, one of only three surviving historic town halls in Lithuania.
The Old Town also preserves houses associated with the Kėdainiai Scottish community, including the Grey House.
Another important landmark is Šviesioji Gymnasium, which dates to the middle of the 17th century. The Radziwills planned to develop it into a Protestant institution of higher education, although the ambitious project never came to fruition.
Janusz Radziwill had elevated Kėdainiai’s four-grade school to gymnasium status and hoped eventually to turn it into a Protestant academy capable of competing with the Jesuit academy in Vilnius, Žirgulis said.
When the River Nevėžis was a trade route
Kėdainiai’s history was also shaped by the Nevėžis River.
The site of the former Kėdainiai port, near the present-day Mikalojus Daukša Public Library, recalls the town’s history as a centre of commercial shipping.
Flat-bottomed sailing cargo vessels known as vytinės once carried goods along the river. From Kėdainiai, they travelled to Konigsberg, carrying local and Lithuanian raw materials and returning with a variety of goods and luxury products.
The town’s history of craftsmanship can still be experienced at the Traditional Crafts Centre, housed in the former Arnet House associated with the Scottish community of Kėdainiai.
The centre displays folk art and hosts workshops where visitors can learn traditional crafts that are increasingly rare, Žirgulis said.
The building also has a long religious history. From the second half of the 17th century until the end of the 19th century, it served as the residence of the superintendents of the Evangelical Reformed Church in Samogitia – effectively a regional religious centre.
Kedainiai was also an important centre of Jewish life in Samogitia, with the region’s chief rabbis living in the town.
Traces of that heritage can still be found along Žydų (Jewish) Street, including a surviving house at No. 2 with a wooden annex used for the Jewish festival of Sukkot.
The roof of the structure could be opened, allowing residents to pray beneath the sky without leaving the house, Žirgulis explained. Sukkot commemorates the biblical story of the Israelites’ 40 years of wandering in the desert after leaving Egypt.
A year as cultural capital
Kėdainiai was designated Lithuania’s Capital of Culture for 2026. The yearlong program, titled Kėdainiai Chronicles in Cultural Symbols, highlights the town’s history as a Radziwill city where different ethnic and religious communities lived side by side and where crafts, education and cultural exchange flourished.
The program includes more than 30 projects and 150 events.
August’s program includes the exhibition Universe From a Spiral by Nijolė Šivickas de Mockus, a national painters’ plein air event titled Kėdainiai on Both Sides of the Nevėžis, an exhibition of Impressionist graphics, a traditional weaving project titled Threads That Connect, and a ceramics workshop, Radziwill Symbols in Clay, among other events.

Athens is cleaning its all-marble Olympic stadium for the first time in more than a century
ATHENS, Greece (AP) — It has welcomed marathon runners, rock stars and the first modern Olympic athletes. Now it’s time for a monumental scrub.
Athens’ U-shaped Panathenaic Stadium — made entirely of white marble — is getting its first systematic cleaning in more than a century.
Rebuilt for the first modern Olympics in 1896, the site is the ceremonial send-off point for the Olympic flame, including for Los Angeles 2028.
A fresh look in Athens ahead of LA 2028
The stadium is made of the same glittering marble used on the Acropolis, making the cleanup job delicate.
The work is revealing a monument changed by time, weather and millions of visitors. Cleaners in white and blue overalls move across the stadium’s 47 steep tiers with thin spray lances, using clean water at carefully controlled, low pressure. Machines vacuum up the runoff as they go.
“The goal of cleaning is not to make everything white. It’s to remove the dirt and the first layers of organic material that are deposited on the surface,” said Dimitris Selemis, a senior conservator at the site.
“The idea is to clean all the material that (contains) dirt from the surface, so we have a better understanding of the monument,” he said, noting that there is more weed growth and dirt in areas that receive less direct sunlight.
Dealing with chewing gum, high heels and soft-drink stains
Crews are also using steam to remove blotches of chewing gum, which have turned black over time. Stadium officials say chewing gum, high heels and soft-drink stains are among the main culprits that stain the marble.
Now slowly being revealed, the original gleaming look of the venue earned it the widely used Greek nickname “Kallimarmaro,” or beautiful marble.
“We call it Kallimarmaro because it is made of the finest marble, the best that was ever produced ... We are very proud of it,” Isidoros Kouvelos, president of the Hellenic Olympic Committee, told The Associated Press in an interview.
“It needed to be cleaned of everything that has accumulated over all these years,” he said. “This way the Panathenaic Stadium will show all its brilliance and will once again be the foremost monument of humanity for the Olympic Movement.”
Built into a natural ravine, the stadium’s site was used for ancient Greek athletic contests and later Roman spectacles before falling into centuries of disuse.
An African celebration planned in Athens
Since its 1896 revival, it has staged fashion shows, rock concerts, boxing matches and national celebrations — as well as lavish patriotic ceremonies ordered by a military dictatorship in the 1960s.
Today, it’s a popular draw for tourists. Apart from the cleanup, the Kallimarmaro is also getting a new track, upgraded lighting and fresh support facilities. The project is privately funded by a shipping company and overseen by the Hellenic Olympic Committee with guidance from Culture Ministry experts.
The work will continue through 2026 but is currently concentrated on the stadium’s “sling,” or curved end, in time for the next torch-passing ceremony on Sept. 10.
That will be for the Dakar Youth Olympics, scheduled for Oct. 31-Nov. 13 in Senegal, which will be the first Olympic competition ever hosted in Africa.
IOC President Kirsty Coventry, a swimming gold medal winner from Zimbabwe, is due to attend the Sept. 10 ceremony, Kouvelos said.

Miami to decide fate of stunning stadium where speedboats raced and Jimmy Buffett played
MIAMI (AP) — The Miami Marine Stadium, considered a masterpiece of midcentury Brutalist architecture with its massive cantilevered roof and a stunning view of the city, opened in 1963 as the nation’s only race venue for powerboats, a major symbol of South Florida.
The historic gem designed by a Cuban refugee was the backdrop of Elvis Presley’s 1967 film “Clambake” and a beloved venue of Gloria Estefan and the late Jimmy Buffett, who performed on a movable, floating stage.
But since closing in 1992 after suffering serious damage during Hurricane Andrew, the 6,500-seat venue has fallen into disarray and today is fenced off and covered in graffiti despite being listed on the U.S. National Register of Historic Places.
On Tuesday, voters will decide whether to allow the city to partner with a private company to return the unique stadium situated on Virginia Key across the water from downtown Miami to its former glory.
President Richard Nixon and Sammy Davis Jr. appeared together at the stadium during a 1972 campaign rally to support Nixon’s reelection bid. And residents would gather for Easter sunrise services, as well as celebrate Our Lady of Charity, a representation of the Virgin Mary honored by many Cubans and Cuban Americans.
“It encapsulated a time period in Miami that’s incredibly unique with an incredible story that touches all Miamians,” Miami Commissioner Damian Pardo said. “So this is really a place that connects with Miami at a very deep and personal level.”
The stadium has a rich history in Miami
Hilario Candela was in his late 20s and a recent refugee from the Cuban Revolution when he designed the structure. He spent decades designing buildings around Miami and led community efforts to restore the marine stadium until his death in 2022.
When it was added to the National Park Service’s National Register of Historic Places in 2018, then-Secretary of State Ken Detzner called the stadium “one of the state of Florida’s most unique architectural and historic resources,” and “an ambitious example of mid-century Modernist architecture” that represented the “multi-cultural influences evolving in the city of Miami during the 1960s.”
The year before his 2023 passing, Buffett wrote a letter to Miami officials, urging them to save the “little cathedral of musical fun.” The Mayor of Margaritaville described a 1985 concert at the marine stadium that ended with him jumping off the floating stage into the surrounding water, joining cheering swimmers and boaters.
“Those were the ‘Miami Vice’ days, and Don Johnson introduced us,” Buffett wrote of the actor in the popular 1980s television crime drama. “WITCO Desperadoes from Trinidad opened the show, and the Coral Reefer Band finished the night with what I think was one of the most fun shows, for band and fans, that I have ever done.”
Buffett’s daughter, Savannah, continues to work with Restore Miami Marine Stadium. The group’s co-founder, Donald Worth, began a mission to save the stadium in 2008, shortly after city officials at the time announced plans to tear it down.
“The architecture gives it the wow, the history gives it its soul,” Worth said. “It’s just a special, magical place, and so many people in Miami have had such memorable experiences there.”
Voters will help decide the stadium’s future
Breakwater Hospitality Group co-founder Emi Guerra, who’s working with the company selected to partner with the city, said the stadium’s view of the city skyline and its location right off Biscayne Bay could make it an entertainment destination that rivals Denver’s Red Rocks Amphitheatre, an open-air venue built into the side of a mountain.
“There’s just something here that can’t be duplicated, and that’s the view,” Guerra said of the marine stadium.
Guerra said he can understand the cynicism felt by some voters, but he points out that this isn’t a developer land grab to build more condos. The city of Miami will retain complete ownership of the stadium and surrounding area, and the management company will be stewards of the city’s vision, Guerra said.
“When it’s something that’s historic, you have two options: restore it or let it rot,” Guerra said.
Ernesto Cuesta, head of the Brickell Homeowners Association, sees another option.
He said the group supports the preservation of a historical landmark, but members are worried about how noise and traffic from a large-scale event space will affect residents across the bay and nearby wildlife. He said he’d prefer a park with more green spaces.
Restoration plans aim to honor the stadium’s past
Despite sitting unused for more than three decades, multiple studies have shown that the stadium remains structurally safe.
Project architect Richard Heisenbottle, who has previously worked to restore other Miami landmarks, said he hopes reviving the marine stadium will honor its original architect.
He said renovations will have to include modern amenities and accessibility requirements, but the galvanized steel rebar used to reinforce the concrete during its original construction has protected the stadium from serious deterioration. Completed construction documents for the restorations have already been permitted by the city of Miami, he said.
Miami Commissioner Pardo said a significant hurdle to reopening the stadium previously has been the city government simply not having the capacity to properly run a major event space.
The referendum going before Miami voters on Tuesday would allow the city to enter into a management agreement.
The private company would develop the stadium’s large parking area into a flex park that includes public recreational facilities and a special events space. The city would collect revenue from events to fund the stadium repairs and upgrades, while paying the company a management fee. The total cost of the project hasn’t been determined, but Pardo insisted it will not be borne by taxpayers at all.

TAIPEI (Taiwan News) — Taiwan's average household disposable income reached a record NT$1.21 million (US$38,500) in 2025, up 3.9% from a year earlier, according to a report issued Friday by the Directorate-General of Budget, Accounting and Statistics.
Disposable income refers to income remaining after non-consumption expenditures such as taxes and social insurance premiums have been deducted, leaving money available for consumption or savings, per Storm Media.
Hsinchu retained its position as the municipality with the highest average household disposable income at NT$1.604 million, followed by Hsinchu County at NT$1.569 million. Taipei ranked third at NT$1.554 million per household.
Hsinchu's high ranking was attributed to its concentration of high-tech companies in the Hsinchu Science Park and the growth of the AI industry. Taipei, meanwhile, benefits from its position as Taiwan's political and financial center, per CNA.
Among Taiwan's six major municipalities, Taipei, New Taipei, Taoyuan, and Taichung all recorded average household disposable income above the national average of NT$1.21 million. Tainan and Kaohsiung were below the national average, with household disposable income of NT$1.044 million and NT$1.206 million, respectively.
DGBAS added that average household consumption expenditure reached NT$920,000 in 2025, an increase of 3.6% from 2024. Average household savings rose 4.9% to NT$290,000 during the same period, per the report.

NEW DELHI: Former Union minister and senior Congress leader P Chidambaram on Sunday hit back at Prime Minister Narendra Modi over his “dimagi naxal” remarks, saying he was “proud” to be described by the term.“I am proud to be a dimagi naxal!” Chidambaram said in a post on X, a day after PM Modi, in his 80th Independence Day address from the Red Fort, warned that although armed Naxalism was nearing its end, people with an ideological Naxal mindset continued to pose a threat.
PM Modi had urged people to “identify and isolate” such individuals, saying they were looking for opportunities to promote violence and unrest and “drag the country on the wrong path”.The Prime Minister said his government had made ending Naxalism a priority after coming to power in 2014. He said decades of Maoist violence had destroyed the lives and aspirations of young people and claimed that more than 3,500 security personnel had been killed in the conflict.“Naxalism, Maoism destroyed the dreams of lakhs of youth. It stole the sons of numerous mothers, crushed the dreams of numerous mothers,” PM Modi said in his Independence Day address.
He also alleged that people with a Maoist mindset had earlier occupied positions of influence in government committees and policymaking.
“For years, people with Maoist thinking were there in public life, even in government committees this thinking had affected various institutions and initiatives,” PM Modi said, before calling for the “dimagi naxal” to be identified and isolated.
Congress attacks PM Modi’s ‘dimagi naxal’ remark
Chidambaram's response came a day after Congress had already attacked PM Modi over the remarks.Congress general secretary Jairam Ramesh accused the Prime Minister of reviving the “urban naxal” label to target political opponents. He argued that the government had previously used the term despite Union home minister Amit Shah clarifying in Parliament that there was no official definition of “urban Naxal”.Ramesh also alleged that the government eventually adopted some of the issues raised by people it had labelled “urban Naxals”. He cited the caste census as an example, saying the idea had earlier been dismissed as reflecting “urban Naxal” thinking before the government announced a caste-based census.“First, you make derogatory remarks--calling them 'Urban Naxals' or 'Mental Naxals'--but later, you accept the very demands your political opponents were making,” Ramesh said, according to news agency ANI.The Congress leader had also earlier called PM Modi a “master abuser” in “Entire Political Science” while reacting to his use of the “urban naxal” and “dimagi naxal” labels.
The broader Opposition has also criticised PM Modi's remarks. CPI(M) general secretary M A Baby said his party opposed Naxalism but accused the government of using the “Naxal mentality” label to target political opponents.CPI general secretary D Raja described “dimagi naxal” as an attempt to stigmatise dissent.TMC leader Saket Gokhale similarly accused PM Modi of using the Independence Day platform to take “cheap shots” at his critics.
PM Modi says armed Naxalism ‘breathing its last’
PM Modi's remarks came as he declared that the government had made substantial progress in tackling armed Naxalism.He said Naxalism had kept large parts of the country and its population “hostage” for decades and had resulted in the deaths of thousands of security personnel.
“In 2014, when you gave us the opportunity we had decided that we will rid the country of naxalism, and today I am happy to announce that naxalism does not have even the strength for breathing,” PM Modi said.At the same time, he cautioned that the end of armed insurgency did not mean the ideological challenge had disappeared.“Even if these naxals are gone, but the 'dimagi naxal' (people with naxal thinking), are looking for an opportunity, looking to see ways of violence, and trying to drag the country to a wrong path,” PM Modi said.He linked the issue to his broader call for young people to contribute to the government's vision of a developed India by 2047, arguing that remote areas once affected by Naxal violence were now seeing development.
